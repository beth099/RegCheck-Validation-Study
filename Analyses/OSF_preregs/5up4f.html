Similarity-Based Interference in Native and Non-Native Language Comprehension

Study Information

Title

Retrieval Interference Study 1

Authors

Ian Cunnings, Hiroki Fujita

Description

This study investigates the similarities and differences between native (L1) and non-native (L2) sentence processing (for reviews, see Clahsen &amp; Felser, 2006, 2018; Cunnings, 2017; Hopp, 2018). It in particular investigates similarity-based interference during sentence processing (for review, Lewis et al., 2006; Vasishth et al., 2019). This experiment focuses on retrieval interference in so-called filler-gap dependencies as in sentences like (1), where correct interpretation requires that 'the beer' is interpreted as the direct object of the verb 'drank'. A distractor constituent ('the wine') however intervenes, and may interfere in how the dependency is resolved (see Cunnings &amp; Sturt, 2018). This study aims to examine how interference in such sentences affects L1 and L2 comprehension. (1) Mary saw the beer that the man with the wine very happily drank during the party. Hypotheses

The main aim of this study is to test the hypothesis that L2 English speakers may be more susceptible to interference than L1 English speakers (Cunnings, 2017). This would predict that L2 readers should show larger interference effects than L1 speakers. Please see "Design Plan" for some more specific predictions based on this hypothesis. The study will also examine whether any L1/L2 differences result from 'shallow' L2 processing (Clahsen &amp; Felser, 2006; 2008), or due to L2 proficiency/slowed lexical processing (Hopp, 2018).

Study design

The study involves a mixed design (between groups with repeated measures). Participants will be either native or non-native English speakers, and will take part in two sessions. In the first session, participants will read texts in four conditions as in (2), while their eye-movements are monitored. (2) manipulates sentence plausibility, such that (2a/b) are plausible ('the beer that the man... drank') while (2c/d) are implausible ('the cake that the man... drank'). The plausibility of a distractor constituent has also been manipulated, and is a plausible object of 'drank' in (2a/c) ('the wine') but is implausible in (2b/d) ('the food'). Participants will read 24 texts like (2) in a latin-square design, which will be randomised with 72 filler texts.The experiment will start with 4 practices. All texts will be followed by a simple yes/no comprehension question (e.g. 'Did the man drink happily?'). For the experimental items, questions will not test how interference may affect the filler-gap dependency, to avoid bringing too much attention to our manipulations. (2a) Plausible Sentence, Plausible Distractor Mary saw the beer that the man with the wine very happily drank during the party. The night was fun. (2b) Plausible Sentence, Implausible Distractor Mary saw the beer that the man with the food very happily drank during the party. The night was fun. (2c) Implausible Sentence, Plausible Distractor Mary saw the cake that the man with the wine very happily drank during the party. The night was fun. (2d) Implausible Sentence, Implausible Distractor Mary saw the cake that the man with the food very happily drank during the party. The night was fun. A main effect of plausibility, with longer reading times for implausible (2c/d) than plausible (2a/b) is expected at and after the critical verb 'drank'. Interference would be indicated by two potential patterns. Firstly, facilitatory interference would be indexed in implausible sentences by shorter reading times in (2c) than (2d). Inhibitory interference in plausible sentences would be indexed by longer reading times in (2a) than (2b). Thus, a key prediction of interference is a plausibility by distractor interaction. If L2ers are more susceptible to interference than L1ers, they should show larger interference effects than L1ers. Thus a crucial test is whether the plausibility by distractor interaction is further modulated by an interaction with group. The second session will take place at least one week after the first session. In the second session participants will again read a series of sentences, but this time without eye-tracking. Experimental sentences will consist of 24 texts as in (3) that manipulate whether the sentences includes a filler-gap dependency (3a/b vs. 3c/d) and the plausibility of a distractor (3a/c vs. 3b/d). Participants will read each sentence in a self-paced whole-sentence fashion. After reading each sentence, a question will be displayed with two options that participants must choose from (see example below). This time, the question directly relates to the resolution of the dependency (i.e. what is interpreted to be the direct object of "ate"). The 24 experimental items will be randomised with 72 fillers in a latin-square design, and the experiment will begin with 4 practices. (3a) Filler-Gap Dependency, Plausible Distractor Kevin saw the sandwich that the boy by the cake quickly ate during lunch. (3b) Filler-Gap Dependency, Implausible Distractor Kevin saw the sandwich that the boy by the milk quickly ate during lunch. (3c) No Dependency, Plausible Distractor Kevin saw the boy by the cake who quickly ate the sandwich during lunch. (3d) No Dependency, Implausible Distractor Kevin saw the boy by the milk who quickly ate the sandwich during lunch. Question: What did the boy eat during lunch? (The sandwich / The cake) We expect a main effect of distractor, with lower accuracy when the distractor is plausible (3a/c) than implausible (3b/d). This effect should be larger in the case of filler-gap than no-dependency sentences. Thus (3a) should have lower accuracy than (3b), but this effect should be smaller or absent in (3c/d). Thus, a sentence type by distractor interaction is predicted as an index of interference. If L2ers are more susceptible to interference than L1ers, they should show a larger difference between (3a/b) than L1ers. This would thus predict a group by sentence type by distractor interaction. In addition to the reading experiment, participants will also complete a short lexical decision experiment in the second session. Participants will be presented with 90 randomised words (half real words, half non-words) and must decide as quickly as possible whether the word is a real word or not. Non-native speakers will also complete a short English proficiency test.

Randomization

For the eye-tracking and reading experiments, each participant will be assigned a random order of items that presents each participant with 6 items from each of the four experimental conditions in a latin-square design.

Data collection procedures

Data collection will continue until 80 L1 and 80 L2 English speakers are recruited. L1 English speakers must have learnt English since birth in a majority English-speaking country. L2 English speakers must have learnt a different language since birth, and must have started learning English after at least age 8. If recruiting enough L2 participants using this criteria proves difficult, we will also include L2 participants who started learning English after age 5, as long as English was learnt in a school setting.

Sample size

Participants will include 80 L1 English speakers and 80 L2 English speakers. Participants must score at least 70% correct on the comprehension questions in the eye-tracking task to be included in any analysis, as this indicates that they paid attention to the content of the sentences. L2 participants must also score 30/60 (CEFR B1) or above on the English proficiency test. Any participant that does not meet these thresholds will be removed, and replaced with another participant until 80 participants that meet these criteria are recruited.

Sample size rationale

The sample size is based on previous studies and the time-frame of the project.

Variables

Manipulated variables

For the eye-tracking task, the independent variables are sentence plausibility (plausible vs. implausible), distractor plausibility (plausible vs. implausible) and sentence region (critical region vs. spillover region) (see below for description of regions). For the second reading task, the independent variables are sentence type (filler-gap dependency vs. no dependency) and distractor plausibility (plausible vs. implausible). Both experiments also manipulate group (L1 vs. L2).

Measured variables

For the eye-tracking task, reading times will be recorded and analysed at two regions of text - the critical region will consist of the critical verb, while the spillover region will consist of the rest of the critical sentence (see / in the example below). Mary saw the beer (the cake) that the man with the wine (the food) very happily/ drank/ during the party./ The night was fun. Three standard reading time measures will be calculated at each region. First-pass reading time is the sum of fixations, starting when a region is first entered from the left, up until it is exited. Regression path time is the sum of fixations, starting when a region is first entered from the left, up until (but not including) the first fixation in a region to the right. Total viewing time is the sum of all fixations in a region. As is standard, if a region is initially skipped during reading, first-pass and regression-path times will be treated as missing data. Trials in which a region is not fixated at all will be treated as missing data in all measures. In calculating reading times, individual fixations shorter than 80ms that are within 1 character of a neighbouring fixation will be merged. All other fixations below 80ms, or above 800ms, will be discarded before further analysis. Trials with excessive track loss will also be removed before analysis. For the second (non eye-tracking) reading experiment, the dependent variable will be accuracy to the comprehension question, i.e. correct (1) or incorrect (0).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Statistical analysis will be conducted using mixed-effects models fit using the lme4 package in R. For the reading time data in the eye-tracking task, reading times will be log-transformed to remove any skew in the data (see Nicenboim &amp; Vasishth, 2016). Linear mixed effects models will be fit to each of the three reading time measures, containing fixed main effects and interactions for group (L1/L2), sentence region (critical/spillover), sentence plausibility (plausible/implausible) and distractor plausibility (plausible/implausible). Each fixed effect will be sum-coded (-1/1). Random effects for subjects and items will be included using the “maximal” random-effects structure that converges (Barr, 2013; Barr, Levy, Scheepers, &amp; Tily, 2013). For reading times, random effects of trial will also be included (see Cunnings &amp; Sturt, 2018). Example code is as below: model = lmer(log(rt) ~ group * region * plausibility * distractor + (1+region*plausibility*distractor|subject) + (1+group*region*plausibility*distractor|item) + (1+region|trial), data = rt_data) In the case of interactions between plausibility and distractor, nested contrasts will be conducted at the two levels of plausibility to examine interference effects. In the case of any interactions with region and/or group, separate analyses will be conducted on each region and/or group as appropriate. For the accuracy data in the second (non eye-tracking) reading task, analysis will be conducted using generalised (binomial) mixed models. Sum-coded fixed effects will include group (L1/L2), sentence type (filled gap/no filled gap) and distractor plausibility (plausible/implausible). Models will be fit with the maximal random effects structure that converges. See example code below: model = glmer(acc ~ group * type * plausibility + (1+type*plausibility|subject) + (1+group*type*plausibility|item), family=binomial, data = acc_data) In the case of interactions between sentence type and plausibility, nested contrasts will be conducted at the two levels of sentence type to examine interference effects. In the case of any interactions with group, separate analyses will be conducted on each group as appropriate. If the maximal model in any analysis fails to converge, the model will be refit removing the random correlation parameters. If this model still fails to converge, the random effects parameter that accounts for the least variance in the data will be iteratively removed until convergence is achieved. References Barr, D. (2013). Random effects structure for testing interactions in linear mixed-effects models. Frontiers in Psychology, 4, 328. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00328 Barr, D., Levy, R., Scheepers, C., &amp; Tily, H. (2013). Random-effects structure for confirmatory hypothesis testing: keep it maximal. Journal of Memory and Language, 68, 255–278. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2012.11.001 Cunnings, I., &amp; Sturt, P. (2018). Retrieval interference and semantic interpretation. Journal of Memory and Language, 102, 16–27. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2018.05.001 Nicenboim, B &amp; Vasishth, S. (2016). Statistical methods for linguistic research: Foundational Ideas - Part II. Language and Linguistics Compass, 10, 591-613.

Transformations

Reading times will be log-transformed. All fixed effects will be coded -1/1.

Inference criteria

As is standard, a p value of .05 will be used to test significance. All analyses will use 2-tailed tests.

Data exclusion

Beyond the participant exclusion criteria outlined under "Design Plan", no data will be excluded.

Missing data

Beyond the missing data criteria described for the calculation of reading time measures in the eye-tracking data (see "Design Plan"), no data should be missing. Mixed-models are robust against data missing at random.

Exploratory analysis

Depending on the results of the main analysis, we may conducted additional analyses. This will especially be the case if we observe L1/L2 differences, to further test the nature of these differences. Additional analyses may include an analysis of the L2 data with English proficiency as a predictor, or including the L2 learners' first language as a fixed effect (specifically, testing whether the L2 learners come from a wh-movement or wh-in-situ L1). We may also examine how performance on the lexical decision task influences behaviour in both L1 and L2 readers.
