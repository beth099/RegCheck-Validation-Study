The effect of sleep on subjective well-being: An experience sampling study

OSF ID: rzdv5 | Registered: 2019-09-17T10:14:35.363841 | Showing: original version (1 version(s) on OSF)

Study Information

Title

The effect of sleep on subjective well-being: An experience sampling study

Authors

Anita Lenneis, Ahuti Das-Friebel, Sakari Lemola, Henrik Singmann, Dieter Wolke, Adrian von Muhlenen, Nicole Tang, Anu Realo

Description

Previous research has shown that a variety of sleep variables (e.g., sleep quality, sleep duration, chronotype, and social jetlag) are linked to subjective and physical well-being (see for example, Galambos, Dalton, &amp; Maggs, 2009; Gangwisch et al., 2006; Merikanto et al., 2013; Steptoe, Peacey, &amp; Wardle, 2006; Wittmann, Dinich, Merrow, &amp; Roenneberg, 2006; Wrzus, Wagner, &amp; Riediger, 2014; Zohar, Tzischinsky, Epstein, &amp; Lavie, 2005). In the current study, we want to extend previous findings by applying experience sampling methodology under more ecologically valid conditions and by incorporating multiple sleep variables in the same study. The purpose of this study is to examine the within-subject effects of previous night’s sleep (as measured by self-rated sleep duration, mid-sleep timepoint, sleep satisfaction, sleep-onset latency, i.e., time it takes to fall asleep, and actigraphy derived sleep efficiency) on next day’s subjective well-being using experience sampling methodology in University students during a fourteen-day period. Subjective well-being will be conceptualized and operationalized via positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction (cf. Realo, Johannson, &amp; Schmidt, 2016). We will person-mean-center the scores (Wang &amp; Maxwell, 2015) of self-reported sleep duration, sleep satisfaction, sleep-onset latency, and actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency which means that the scores will be centered around one’s personal fourteen-day average of the relevant sleep variables. For the mid-sleep time point we will center the daily scores of mid-sleep around one’s mid-sleep on free days (absolute value) that can be seen as a proxy of social jetlag which is defined as a misalignment between social and biological time (for example when school and work schedules do not match one’s individual sleep preferences; Wittmann et al., 2006). Depending on the results we get, we then want to come up with a simpler joint model to assess which sleep variable contributes the most to the different aspects of one’s subjective well-being on the next day. All models will be calculated separately for each dependent variable (i.e., positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction) in order to examine whether sleep measures have independent effects in predicting different components of subjective well-being, hence supporting their distinctiveness. The independent variables (sleep variables) are measured once a day whereas the dependent variables (positive affect, negative affect, life satisfaction) are operationalized as daily mean scores of up to five measurements per day.

Hypotheses

Self-reported night sleep duration ○ Hypothesis 1: Shorter or longer sleep duration than average is negatively related to next day’s positive affect (cf. Wrzus et al., 2014). ○ Hypothesis 2: Shorter or longer sleep duration than average is positively related to next day’s negative affect (cf. Wrzus et al., 2014). ○ Hypothesis 3: Shorter or longer sleep duration than average is negatively related to next day’s life satisfaction. Self-reported absolute social jetlag (i.e., daily scores of mid-sleep centered around mid-sleep on free days) ○ Hypothesis 4: Higher levels of absolute social jetlag are negatively related to next day’s levels of positive affect. ○ Hypothesis 5: Higher levels of absolute social jetlag are positively related to next day’s levels of negative affect. ○ Hypothesis 6: Higher levels of absolute social jetlag are negatively related to next day’s levels of life satisfaction. Self-reported night sleep satisfaction ○ Hypothesis 7: Higher self-reported sleep satisfaction than average is positively related to next day’s positive affect. ○ Hypothesis 8: Higher self-reported sleep satisfaction than average is negatively related to next day’s negative affect. ○ Hypothesis 9: Higher self-reported sleep satisfaction than average is positively related to next day’s positive affect. Self-reported night sleep onset latency (i.e., the time it takes to fall asleep) ○ Hypothesis 8: Longer self-reported sleep-onset latency than average is negatively related to next day’s positive affect. ○ Hypothesis 9: Longer self-reported sleep-onset latency than average is positively related to next day’s negative affect. ○ Hypothesis 10: Longer self-reported sleep-onset latency than average is negatively related to next day’s life satisfaction. Actigraphy-measured (objective) night sleep efficiency ○ Hypothesis 11: Higher objective sleep efficiency than average is positively related to next day’s positive affect. ○ Hypothesis 12: Higher objective sleep efficiency than average is negatively related to next day’s negative affect. ○ Hypothesis 13: Higher objective sleep efficiency than average is positively related to next day’s life satisfaction. There is general consensus that positive emotions increase throughout the day whereas negative emotions decrease, but not as drastically (Anusic, Lucas, &amp; Donnellan, 2017). However, it is not yet known how momentary appraisals of life satisfaction change throughout the day. Thus, we cannot be certain whether the relationships between the sleep variables and the three components of subjective well-being remain the same across the day. For this reason, we examined the relationship between the sleep variables, positive affect, negative affect, life satisfaction, and time of day in our preliminary analyses (see section "Explanation of existing data").

Study design

The study consisted of two parts: (1) a baseline questionnaire and (2) a fourteen-day experience sampling study with actigraphy measurements. (1) Baseline questionnaire that was completed before the 14-day experience sampling study: ■ Chronotype (Munich Chronotype Questionnaire; Roenneberg, Wirz-Justice, &amp; Merrow, 2003)- used in this study ■ Self-esteem/(mental) health/life satisfaction/well-being ■ Sleep quality ■ Smoking habits ■ Caffeine consumption ■ Alcohol consumption ■ Physical activity ■ Nutrition ■ Personality ■ Electronic media/social media use (2) Experience sampling study: The experience sampling study lasted 14 days (repeated measures) and consisted of the following three parts: ■ Open Survey: In this survey, participants were mostly asked questions about the previous day (i.e., sleep- used in this study, diet, exercise, social media usage). The survey opened at 8am and participants had 24 hours to respond to this survey. It was the longest daily survey to fill in and took around two minutes. ■ Momentary Surveys: Participants were asked about their current experiences five times a day, e.g. how they were doing at the moment- used in this study, where they were, with whom they were with, etc. From Monday to Friday, participants received randomly allocated prompts between 8am and 10pm. On Saturday and Sunday, they received the randomly allocated prompts between 10am and 10pm. There was a minimum of 60 minutes in between the prompts. Participants had 20 minutes to fill out each survey. ■ Actigraphy (used in this study): During the period of experience sampling study, participants also wore an actigraph for the 14 days to measure sleep objectively using accelerometry. They were instructed to wear it as often as they could and should take it off when they were doing anything that could harm other people or the actigraph (e.g. martial arts). Participants did not have to press any buttons since the actigraph recorded the activity automatically.

Explanation of existing data

The experience sampling study was an extension of the SMaRT (Student Mental Health and Resilience in Transition) feasibility study which was led by Professor Dieter Wolke and Dr Nicole Tang and involved members of the Lifespan, Health and Wellbeing Group of the Department of Psychology, University of Warwick. One aim of the SMaRT questionnaire study was to find out how many students would be willing to participate in an experience sampling study. The experience sampling study was not pre-registered, but we consider it important to list our hypotheses before starting the main analyses of the data. In order to specify the research questions for the present study, we ran the following preliminary analyses: ○ Between-person exploratory factor analyses of the eleven items measuring positive and negative affect with an aim to select the best indicators to measure positive and negative affect, respectively. As a result, one item (“tiredness”) was excluded from further analyses; ○ Multilevel analyses of how time of day influences one’s positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction in order to see whether time of day should be included in the analyses. The findings showed that positive affect increased throughout the day whereas negative affect and life satisfaction remained stable throughout the day; ○ We examined correlations between the mid-sleep scores on free days extracted from the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire (MCTQ; Roenneberg et al., 2003) that the participants completed before the 14-day experience sampling study and the average mid-sleep scores on free days in the experience sampling data (free day when going to bed and free day the day after) to see which score works best to center mid-sleep scores. We will use the mid-sleep on free days scores extracted from the MCTQ in our analyses because the correlation between both mid-sleep on free days scores was quite high, r = .59 and all participants had a score for the mid-sleep on free days from the MCTQ whereas only 96 participants had a score for mid-sleep on free days (free day when going to bed and free day the day after) extracted from the experience sampling study. ○ Correlational analyses of the following self-reported (sleep onset latency, sleep satisfaction, sleep duration, mid-sleep) and actigraphy-measured (sleep efficiency, latency, wake after sleep onset, total sleep time) sleep variables to select appropriate variables for the models to assure that those variables included in the joint model did not share too much variance with each other in order to decrease the degree of multicollinearity (Baguley, 2012), i.e. no correlations higher than r = .30; (H. Singmann, personal communication, August 17, 2019); ■ All the above mentioned self-reported sleep variables correlated at most at r = .24 with each other; ■ Actigraphy measured sleep efficiency correlated highly with actigraphy measured sleep onset latency (r = -.39, p smaller than .001) and time awake after sleep onset (r = -.85, p smaller than .001) which is not surprising since sleep efficiency is derived from sleep onset latency and time awake after sleep onset. In order to avoid including confounding variables, we only included one actigraphy measured variable, namely sleep efficiency.

Data collection procedures

Eligible to participate in the study were those who were 18 years or older and were pursuing an undergraduate degree at the University of Warwick. Participants were recruited either through the SMaRT study (students who filled in the study’s questionnaire could also indicate whether they would be interested in participating in the experience sampling study) or the SONA research panel. Those who had indicated their interest in the SMaRT study to participate in the experience sampling study, were contacted by e-mail by either Ahuti Das-Friebel or Anita Lenneis. They then were invited to sign up to an introductory session which lasted an hour. The participants recruited through SONA were able to sign-up to the introductory session through the SONA system. During the introductory session, participants received an information sheet and were asked to give their consent. They then were asked to fill out an online questionnaire, received instructions for the experience sampling study (download an app), and were handed out an actigraph. Participants received £5 for attending the first session. Participants then took part in the 14-day long experience sampling study. They could e-mail Ahuti Das-Friebel or Anita Lenneis if they had any more questions. After the 14-day-period, participants came back to the lab, filled out a feedback questionnaire, returned their actigraphs, and received monetary compensation of up to £35 depending on how many prompts they had filled out during the 14-day-period. Twenty-five participants were able to take part in the study at a time due to the number of actigraphs available. Therefore, we conducted the study in five waves.

Sample size

Overall, 114 participants took part in the study- We wanted to get at least 100 participants and therefore had five waves of participants where a maximum number of 25 could participate at a time (number of actigraphs). At the level of individuals, participants could possibly have ● 14 daily assessments about their sleep (i.e., self-reported sleep satisfaction, sleep-onset latency, sleep duration, mid-sleep, actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency) ● 5 daily assessments across 14 days = 70 momentary assessments of their subjective well-being (i.e., positive affect, negative affect, life satisfaction) However, due to the design of the study, most participants did not manage to fill out all prompts. Participants were advised to fill them out as often as they could but should not fill them out in any situation where they could cause harm to others (i.e. driving).

Sample size rationale

Power and sample size calculations in experience sampling methods are very complex due to the multilevel nature of the data (Kirtley, Lafit, Achterhof, Hiekkaranta, &amp; Myin-Germeys, 2019) and for this reason are rarely reported. This is why we did not examine the effect of sample size on power before conducting the study. Kirtley et al. (2019) recently developed an R script for the power calculations of experience sampling studies but it is not recommended to do the power calculation retrospectively (see Gilbert &amp; Prion, 2016; Hoenig &amp; Heisey, 2001; Nakagawa &amp; Foster, 2004). Effect sizes will be reported with 95% confidence intervals. We thought that 100 participants would be feasible and give us enough power considering the amount of data we would have per participant. Furthermore, we could only test 25 participants at a time and each participant was eligible to receive up to £40 so we had to adjust the number of participants to the available funding.

Measured variables

Predictor (sleep) variables: ○ Self-reported night sleep duration: Sleep duration was measured as the difference between sleep onset (time one indicated to turn off the lights plus the number of minutes it took them to fall asleep) and wake-up time; ○ Self-reported absolute social jetlag (i.e., daily scores of mid-sleep centered around mid-sleep on free days): Mid-sleep was calculated as sleep onset plus sleep duration divided into two; ○ Self-reported night sleep satisfaction: Participants could indicate how satisfied they were with last night’s sleep, from 1 = “very dissatisfied” to 4 = “very satisfied” ○ Self-reported night sleep onset latency (i.e., time it takes to fall asleep): Participants had to indicate how long it took them (in minutes) to fall asleep; ○ Actigraphy-measured (objective) night sleep efficiency: Daily actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency (ActiLife ActiGraph wGT3X-BT): Sleep efficiency was calculated by the ActiLife software as the number of sleep minutes divided by the total number of minutes the subject was in bed (i.e., the difference between the In-Bed and Out Bed time) Potential control variables: ○ Gender (female, male, other) ○ Free day/workday: when going to bed and when getting up ○ Alarm clock usage (yes, no) ○ Time of day; morning vs. afternoon Outcome (Subjective Well-being) variables: ○ Positive affect (Mean score of happy, enthusiastic, content, relaxed, attentive); Participants were asked to indicate how happy, enthusiastic, content, relaxed, attentive they felt at this moment, from 1 = “not at all” to 5 = “to a large extent” ○ Negative affect (Mean score of annoyed, upset, bored, sad, worried); Participants were asked to indicate how annoyed, upset, bored, sad, worried they felt at this moment from 1 = “not at all” to 5 = “to a large extent” ○ Life satisfaction: Life satisfaction was measured with the question “All things considered, how satisfied are you with your life at the moment?” (1 = “extremely dissatisfied” to 10 = “extremely satisfied”) The outcome variables were measured five times a day: twice in the morning, twice in the afternoon and once in the evening.

Indices

• We will use mean scores of positive and negative affect. • Daily self-reported sleep duration is calculated as the difference between sleep-onset (time one goes to bed + time they need to fall asleep = time one falls asleep) and the time they wake up • Daily self-reported mid-sleep is calculated as sleep-onset + sleep duration/2 and is expressed in time • Daily self-reported absolute social jetlag is calculated as the absolute value of the difference between mid-sleep on free days extracted from the MCTQ and daily self-reported mid-sleep • Sleep efficiency was calculated by the ActiLife software as the number of sleep minutes divided by the total number of minutes the subject was in bed (i.e., the difference between the In-Bed and Out Bed-time)

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

○ We will use linear mixed models to test our hypotheses. ○ The dependent variables will be within participant daily mean scores of positive affect, negative affect, or life satisfaction (models are run separately for each dependent variable). ○ The independent variables are daily self-reported sleep duration, self-reported sleep satisfaction, self-reported sleep-onset latency, and actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency which are person-mean-centered over their 14-day-average as well as daily self-reported mid-sleep which is centered on person-mean mid-sleep on free days (taken from the MCTQ (Roenneberg et al., 2003) which participants filled out at the beginning of the study), i.e. a proxy for social jetlag. ○ We will predict one dependent variable at a time using one independent variable. We predict that the relationship will be the same across the day in negative affect and life satisfaction. We will explore the relationship for positive affect. ○ The model will include by-participant random intercepts and by-participant random slopes for all independent variables that vary within-participants. This then constitutes the maximal-random effects structure justified by design. We will start with a model that includes correlations and we will remove them in case of convergence problems. Should this model show further convergence problems (e.g., a singular fit), we will iteratively reduce the random-effects structure, beginning with removing the random-slopes, until the model converges successfully. ○ The results will inform us to come up with an exploratory model where sleep variables that are significantly related to subjective well-being will be included and the influence of time is being considered. ○ To test model terms, we will use the Satterthwaite method as implemented in R package afex.

Transformations

As already said above, we will center daily self-reported sleep duration, self-reported sleep satisfaction, self-reported sleep-onset latency and actigraphy-measured sleep efficiency around their person-mean score of the fourteen-day period. For mid-sleep, we will center the daily scores around the person-mean mid-sleep on free days taken from the MCTQ (Roenneberg et al., 2003) since this will provide us with a proxy of social jetlag.

Inference criteria

Null-hypothesis significance testing based on the Satterthwaite corrected degrees-of-freedom of the optimal model (i.e., maximal-model reduced such that no singular fit is observed) using a significance criterion of p smaller than .05.

Data exclusion

We will exclude one participant due to age (31-years-old) since chronotype is likely to change throughout young adulthood. All other participants were 18 to 22 years old and this is exactly the age when chronotype reaches its peak and then gradually decreases over time (Adan et al., 2012). Since participants had to enter their sleep times using an am/pm format, we corrected the sleep times when they were not logical. We will then exclude daily sleep variables if they are above or below two standard deviations.

Missing data

In case either the independent or dependent variable of a particular day is missing for an individual, this day will be ignored in the specific mixed model analysis. In general, the mixed model does not have problems with missing values.

Exploratory analysis

Depending on the results we get in the confirmatory analyses, we want to come up with a joint model explaining which sleep variables are the most important ones when predicting positive and negative affect as well as life satisfaction. Time of day might also be considered in this model if we find that either sleep variable has a different effect on positive affect, negative affect, and life satisfaction depending on the time of day. We would also like to see which variables play a role not only within but also between subjects. If social jetlag has an influence on subjective well-being, it would also be interesting to look at the influence of drinking alcohol and smoking.
