Intergroup bias and the happy face advantage: How social categories influence emotion categorization. OSF Data repository.

Study Information

Hypotheses

If there is evidence that intergroup bias is a mechanism driving social category modulation of emotion categorization, we would expect interactions between perceiver categories and target categories. We hypothesized there would be an interaction between Perceiver Sex, Target Sex, and Target Emotion. Specifically, we expected that female perceivers would show a happy face advantage for female targets (i.e., sex in-group) and male perceivers would show a happy face advantage for male targets (i.e., sex in-group). Given our previous findings, which suggest all perceivers show a happy face advantage for female targets, we also expected that male perceivers would show a happy face advantage for female targets; however, we expected female perceivers would not show a happy face advantage for male targets or that any advantage would be significantly smaller than the advantage for male targets. We hypothesized there would be an interaction between Prime Condition, Target Race, and Target Emotion. Specifically, we expected that White perceivers primed to believe that White targets were an out-group nationality and Black targets were in-group nationality would show a happy face advantage for both White targets and Black targets. Whereas White perceivers primed to believe that White targets were an in-group nationality and Black targets were an out-group nationality would show a happy face advantage for White targets but not Black targets. Further, if there is evidence that intergroup bias is a mechanism driving social category modulation of emotion categorization, we would also expect the size of the happy face advantage would be determined by the relative category overlap between perceivers and targtes. Specifically, we predicted that the happy face advantage for White targets would be significantly larger when perceivers believed they belonged to an in-group nationality relative to when they believed they belonged to an out-group nationality.

Study design

The experiment had a 2(Nationality Prime Condition: Black in-group/White out-group vs. White in-group/Black out-group) X 2(Perceiver Sex : White female perceivers vs. White male perceivers) X 2(Target Sex: female targets vs. male targets) X 2(Target Race: Black targets vs. White targets) X 2(Target Emotion: angry targets vs. happy targets) mixed factorial design, with Nationality Prime Condition and Perceiver Sex as between-subjects factors.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

We aim to have a final sample of around 384 participants, with approximately equal numbers of White women and White men in each of the two nationality prime conditions. In previous experiments we excluded ~8% of participants from the original sample if they exhibited excessively high error rates or excessively slow reaction times (both &gt; 3 S.D. above the median). To address this issue, we aim to over-recruit our initial sample by around 10% to allow us to exclude outlier participants based on their performance relative to the entire sample, whilst still leaving sufficient power to test our hypotheses. To this end, we aim to initially recruit 424 young adult participants (106 White females in the US/White condition, 106 White females in the US/Black condition, 106 White males in the US/White condition, 106 White males in the US/Black condition,). Participants will be recruited via the online recruitment platform Prolific Academic (www.prolific.ac), will complete the experiment remotely via the online testing platform Gorilla (www.gorilla.sc), and will be compensated around US$3 for their time. We will use pre-screen criteria available in Prolific Academic to recruit only participants who self-identified as “Located in the USA”, “Female” or “Male”, and “White/Caucasian”. We will exclude participants prior to analyzing the data if they exhibit excessively high error rates or excessively slow reaction times (both &gt; 3 S.D. above the median).

Sample size rationale

We ran an a priori power analysis, using G*Power3 (Faul, Erdfelder, Lang, &amp; Buchner, 2007), based on a small effect size ηp2= .10, α = .05, 4 between-subjects groups, 3 repeated measures with r = .50, and a non-sphericity correction = 1, G*Power3 indicated that a total sample of 352 participants, with four equal sized groups of n = 88, would be sufficient to achieve statistical power of .95. To maximize statistical power, we aim to have a final sample of no less than 384 participants, with four equal sized groups of 96 participants (i.e., 96 White female participants in the US/White condition, 96 White female participants in the US/Black condition, 96 White male participants in the US/White condition, and 96 White male participants in the US/Black condition). In the previous Experiments in this series we excluded 8% of participants from the original sample because they exhibited excessively high error rates or excessively slow reaction times (both &gt; 3 S.D. above the median); to ensure a gender-balanced and race-balanced sample, we then recruited new participants of the same gender and race to replace the excluded participants. However, the process of excluding and replacing participants has been identified as a potential cause of alpha error inflation (see Simmons et al., 2016). To address this issue, in the current study we aim to we over-recruit our initial sample by around 10% to allow us to exclude outlier participants based on their performance relative to the entire sample, whilst still leaving sufficient power to test our hypotheses. Thus to maximize our chances of achieving a final sample of 384 participants, we aim to recruit an initial sample of 424 participants split evenly across the four between subjects conditions (i.e., 106 participants per condition).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will use R (R Core Team, 2021) and lme4 (Bates et al., 2012) to perform linear mixed effects analyses of the relationship between correct emotion categorization reaction times and errors, and perceiver and target factors. The model with the maximal random structure justified by the design included a five-way interaction between the five categorical variables of interest as well as random by-participant and by-item intercepts, random by-participant slopes for Target Sex, Target Race and Target Emotion, and random by-item slopes for Nationality Prime Condition and Perceiver Sex. Regression-style contrast coding (-.5 and .5 for the two levels of each variable, with reference levels determined alphabetically) will be used for all categorical variables. The bound optimization by quadratic approximation (bobyqa) with a set maximum of 200,000 iterations will be used. When convergence or singularity issues are encountered in the full model, the random effect structure will be simplified to the next best random effect structure (by iteratively removing random slopes for the variables that account for the smallest amount of variance). Including slopes for all variables resulted in singular fit convergence warnings, with the exception of by-participant slopes for Target Emotion. The most complex models would be: lmer (RT ~ 1 + Nationality Prime * Perceiver Sex * Target Sex * Target Race * Target Emotion + (1 + Target Emotion * Target Sex * Target Race * Target Emotion |participant) + (1 + Nationality Prime * Perceiver Sex |item)) glmer (Errors ~ 1 + Nationality Prime * Perceiver Sex * Target Sex * Target Race * Target Emotion + (1 + Target Emotion * Target Sex * Target Race * Target Emotion |participant) + (1 + Nationality Prime * Perceiver Sex |item))

Transformations

Error data will be analysed using binomial general linear mixed effects regression (glmer), with Odds Ratios (OR) described for the analyses and reported in Tables and proportion errors reported in Figures. An OR is a measure of the likelihood of a particular outcome (see Szumilas, 2010). Where an OR equals 1, it indicates there is no difference in the likelihood of a particular outcome; thus, when lower and upper confidence intervals overlap 1 it indicates a null effect. When an OR is less than 1, it indicates a particular outcome is less likely to occur; thus, when lower and upper confidence intervals are both less than 1 it indicates an outcome is significantly less likely to occur. When an OR is greater than 1, it indicates a particular outcome is more likely to occur; thus, when lower and upper confidence intervals are both greater than 1 it indicates an outcome is significantly more likely to occur.

Inference criteria

We will use the standard p&lt;.05 criteria for determining if the ANOVA and the post hoc test suggest that the results are significantly different from those expected if the null hypothesis were correct.

Data exclusion

We will exclude trials where reaction times are likely to be too fast to reflect a deliberative response to the stimulus (i.e., &lt; 250ms) and trials on which reaction times are likely to be too long to reflect attention to the task (i.e., &gt; 1500 ms). If, as is typical, visual inspection of the distribution of correct RTs reveals a long-righthand tail (i.e., relatively more longer reaction times); we will exclude trials with response times greater than 3 standard deviations of the mean.

Missing data

If a participants does not complete all trials, they will not be included in the analysis.
