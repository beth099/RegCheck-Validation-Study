Study Information

Title

Are there perceptual benefits to personal familiarity? Examining the robustness of the personally familiar voice pattern

Authors

Elise Kanber, Carolyn McGettigan

Description

Personal familiarity with a face/voice has been proposed to be accompanied by a stronger or more robust representation of the face/voice pattern in the brain (Krix, Sauerland, &amp; Schreuder, 2017; Fontaine, Love, &amp; Latinus, 2017; Maguinness, Roswandowitz, &amp; von Kriegstein, 2018). At present, there is some evidence to suggest that personal familiarity may be associated with a number of benefits for the perception of these voices (e.g. Lavner, Gath, &amp; Rosenhouse, 2000; Johnsrude et al., 2013). For instance, in the speech perception domain, there is a growing amount of research that has shown an “intelligibility benefit” for speech spoken by personally familiar individuals (Johnsrude et al., 2013; Holmes, Domingo, &amp; Johnsrude, 2018; Souza, Gehani, Wright, &amp; McCloy, 2013). For vocal identity perception, the picture is less clear. Several studies have demonstrated that changes to different acoustic properties of the voice can affect recognition/discrimination of vocal identity in both listeners familiar with the test voice, as well as listeners unfamiliar with the voice (Gaudrain, Li, Shen Ban, &amp; Patterson, 2009; Lavner et al., 2000). For example, research has demonstrated that introducing variability in vocal sounds (e.g. whispered speech, vowel sounds, laughter) had equally detrimental effects on voice identity discrimination for familiar and unfamiliar voices, despite better overall performance when the voices were familiar (see e.g. Lavan, Scott, &amp; McGettigan, 2016; Yarmey, Yarmey, Yarmey &amp; Parliament, 2001). Thus, the extent to which extremely high familiarity with a voice (e.g. that of a romantic partner) means that a listener can tolerate a greater degree of change or “noise” in the vocal signal, is not yet known. This is because research has either utilised voices that may not be “familiar enough” for these benefits to be observed, or has used high familiar voices, but the specific focus of the research was not on exploring identity perception. Therefore, this research specifically aims to test whether high familiarity allows for more accurate performance on two vocal identity tasks and a speech intelligibility task, despite various types of “noise” in the signal.

Hypotheses

1. It is predicted that voice modulation (of F0 &amp; VTL) will produce a significant difference in vocal identity recognition of personally familiar and familiarised voices (for group 1*). For group 2**, we expect no significant difference in listeners' performance between the two learned vocal identities. 2. We also predict that there will be a significant difference in the recognition of identity from filler sounds produced by personally familiar (i.e. one's romantic partner) compared to familiarised speakers. Specifically, group 1 listeners are predicted to perform significantly better on this task when hearing their partner's voice, compared to familiarised voices. 3. A significant intelligibility benefit for personally known voices is expected. That is, on average, a greater number of correct responses to the CRM sentences produced by one's partner (when listened to in noise) will be observed, compared to responses to CRM sentences produced by an unfamiliar voice (group 1). For group 2, it is predicted that there will be a significant but smaller difference in listeners' accuracy scores between the familiarised and unfamiliar voices (i.e. a smaller intelligibility benefit for this group). *group1 = listen to their romantic partner’s voice throughout the experiment and are familiarised with a second voice **group 2 = listen to one of the romantic partner voices from Group 1, and are familiarised with both this and a second voice. Note that the familiarised second voice is matched across groups, and that test voices are always sex-matched (i.e. all male or all female).

Data collection procedures

Couples in romantic relationships will be recruited via the UCL Psychology subject pool, as well as through social media (e.g. Twitter, Facebook), and the researchers' personal networks. Participants will be pre-screened for language (must be Native speakers of British English), must have no significant hearing impairment, must be aged between 18 and 40 years, and must currently be in a relationship (for a minimum of 6 months), where both members are willing to participate together. Eligible participants will be invited to the lab for the first testing session, where voice recordings will be obtained. Session two will take place online and data will be collected on the platform Gorilla (www.Gorilla.sc). An unfamiliar group of participants that will serve as a control group will be recruited via Prolific and data collection will be completed online (via Gorilla). Current demographics for the participant pool of Prolific (N ~ 61,000) can be found here: https://app.prolific.ac/demographics. From this pool, participants will be pre-screened as in the experimental group (see above), except that they will not be required to be in a romantic relationship, nor participate with their romantic partner. All participants will need to pass a headphone check (Woods, K. J., Siegel, M. H., Traer, J., &amp; McDermott, J. H. (2017). Headphone screening to facilitate web-based auditory experiments. Attention, Perception, &amp; Psychophysics, 79(7), 2064-2072) to ensure that they are able to hear the stimuli. Participants will be reimbursed at a rate of £7.50 per hour of planned testing.

Sample size

15 couples, with a ceiling of 20 (30-40 participants; group 1) and 30-40 controls (group 2; recruited via Prolific) will be recruited to participate in the current study.

Sample size rationale

A power analysis for Generalised Linear Mixed models (GLMMs) was run using the SIMR package in R (Green &amp; McLeod, 2016; https://doi.org/10.1111/2041-210X.12504) with pilot data from 6 participants in a version of the voice modulation task. Running a simple LMM with SIMR indicated that there was 99% power to detect an effect size of 0.05 with only 6 participants, suggesting large expected effects. Due to changes in the design of this task from the pilot, as well as prior speech in noise research finding sufficient power (0.95) to detect an effect size of 0.41 with 11 pairs (22 participants; Holmes &amp; Johnsrude, 2018), a sample size of 15 couples and 30 controls (60 participants) was chosen. This also allows for practical constraints, as it may not be straightforward to acquire a larger number of eligible couples that are willing to participate.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Voice modulation: IV1: Degree of voice modulation (F0/VTL; 5 levels) IV2: familiarity (3 levels; Partner, Familiarised voice, unfamiliar). Group 1: Participants will be presented with either their partner's, a familiarised (Anna/Adam), or an unfamiliar voice which will be modulated along the F0-VTL axes. Group 2: Participants will be presented with the same three voices (matched to a single group 1 participant), the only difference being that the partner voice is simply another familiarised voice (named Beth/Ben) to participants in this group. Filler sounds: IV: Familiarity (3 levels; Partner, Familiarised voice, Unfamiliar) Group 1: Participants will be presented with Filler sounds (e.g. "umm", "erm") from either their romantic partner, a familiarised voice, or an unfamiliar voice on any given trial (same three voices as first task above). Intelligibility task: IV: Familiarity (2 levels; Partner, Unfamiliar) Group 1: Here participants will be presented with CRM sentences (see design slides) in noise (fixed SNR producing 60% correct performance) from either their romantic partner, or a novel voice. Group 2: Participants in this group will be presented with the familiarised voice (Beth/Ben) that was heard in the previous tasks.

Measured variables

During familiarisation, which involves an explicit recognition task, all participants' responses will be recorded. Specifically, participants will indicate whether the voice they heard was their partner (trained-to-familiar voice "Beth/Ben" for group 2), "Anna/Adam", or "Clara/Charlie" (unfamiliar voice; see design slides). For the voice modulation and Fillers task, participants' responses (3AFC) will be recorded and hit rates (Hu scores; see Analysis_plan.docx &amp; Indices) will be calculated. For the speech intelligibility task, participant's responses will be measured and accuracy scores (total correct/incorrect) calculated.

Indices

For the purposes of data visualisation, summary statistics may be computed. For instance, for each main task, mean accuracy scores per condition per group will be calculated. e.g. Vocal ID1: mean accuracy for each familiarity condition per modulation ‘step’ per group. For Vocal ID1 &amp; 2 tasks, Hu scores will be calculated. Hu scores are unbiased hit rates. These are calculated using the total number of ‘hits’ (i.e. responding ‘x’ when the correct answer is ‘x’), the total number of the target stimulus (i.e. total ‘x’ trials), and the total number of responses of a particular stimulus type (i.e. total ‘x’ responses regardless of whether this response is correct). These scores are then arcsine transformed as Hu scores are proportional. Formula: Hu = a^2/(a+b+c)*(a+d+g) Where a = total hits a+b+c = total number of responses of a particular stimulus type a+d+g = total number of the target stimulus [Wagner, H. (1993). On measuring performance in category judgment studies of nonverbal behaviour. Journal of Nonverbal Behavior, 17(1), 3-28.]

Inference criteria

Inferences will be made using frequentist statistics, where p less than 0.05 indicates a significant result. Alpha will be adjusted in all analyses for multiple comparisons where necessary.

Data exclusion

Participants that fail to learn the familiarised voices (i.e. chance performance (33.33%) + 95% confidence interval) will be excluded. Data will be excluded on a task-by-task basis for participants who fail to respond correctly to more than 20% of the vigilance trials. Participants who fail the headphone check will be excluded.

Missing data

Only complete datasets will be included.

Exploratory analysis

In the confirmatory analysis plan, separate models will be constructed for group 1 and 2 participants' data. However, 2- or 3-way interactions between group and the fixed factor(s) may also be of interest, to determine whether any personal familiarity advantage observed is due to knowing the speaker. Thus, these interactions may be modelled as follows: VoiceID1: Full model = lmer(HU_SCORE ~ FAMILIARITY*MODULATION_STEP*GROUP + (1|PARTICIPANT) + (1|VOICE_ID), data = data, REML = FALSE) VoiceID2: Full model = lmer(HU_SCORE ~ FAMILIARITY*GROUP + (1|PARTICIPANT) + (1|VOICE_ID), data = data, REML = FALSE) Intelligibility: Full model = glmer(ACCURACY ~ FAMILIARITY*GROUP + (1|PARTICIPANT) + (1|VOICE_ID), data = data, family = "binomial") All models above will be compared to reduced models that remove one parameter in each new model. In the event of no significant interactions, the interaction will be omitted from future models, instead comparing each individual fixed effect to a null model to establish significance of the factors. Significance will be determined via likelihood ratio tests.
