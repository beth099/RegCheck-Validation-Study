Minimum length and spelling

OSF ID: k2xtq | Registered: 2019-08-28T22:34:43.501207

Study Information

Title

Minimum length and spelling Experiment 2

Authors

Rebecca Treiman, Kristian Berg, Rebecca Jewell, Mark Aronoff

Description

Linguists who have examined the English writing system have observed that one-and two-letter words (e.g., ‹in›, ‹of›, ‹I›, ‹up›) tend to be function words. Words that can only be used as content words (e.g., ‹inn›, ‹ebb›) generally have three or more letters. A consonant letter such as ‹n› that does not normally double in word-final position may therefore be doubled in a content word would otherwise have only two letters. In the present experiment, we ask whether adult spellers of English follow this three-letter rule. Specifically, we ask whether people are more likely to use a final consonant doublet when spelling a VC (vowel-consonant) item when it is presented as a noun (a content word) than when it is presented as a preposition (a function word). Participants will hear a nonword, will be given a definition for it, and will hear it in a stressed position of a sentence. They will then be asked to spell the nonword. Approximately half of the participants who are tested will perform a spelling production task, spelling the nonwords on their own. For the other half of the participants, the spelling test will be a choice task in which participants are shown two spellings that differ only in the presence or absence of a final doublet (e.g. ‹ut› vs ‹utt›) and are asked to pick the spelling that they consider to be most appropriate. The experimental nonwords will be mixed with filler items. Some of the fillers will be real words with their correct definitions. There will also be nonword fillers that differ in their characteristics from the target items. The fillers are the same as in Experiment 1 of this project. At the end of the experiment, participants will be given a short questionnaire to assess whether they knew about the three-letter rule before the experiment and, if so, where they learned about it. Then, they will be told that some of the items were not actual English words and will be told the words that do have those definitions.

Hypotheses

We hypothesize that there will be a higher rate of responses with final double consonants when vowel-consonant items are presented as nouns (content words) than when they are presented as prepositions (function words). That is, there should be a main effect of item type. This result would suggest that people tend to double the final consonants of content words that would otherwise have two letters in order to make the spellings longer and that they are less likely to do so for function words. We also predict a main effect of task, with more doublet use in the choice task than in the production task. We do not hypothesize a statistically significant interaction between task and item type. We further hypothesize that, in the production task, responses that lengthen the spelling in other ways than by using the expected vowel and final double consonant ("other" responses) will be more common for content words than for function words. Finally, we hypothesize that these patterns will hold true even for participants who were not aware of the three-letter rule.

Study design

There is one within-participant factor, item type (content versus function) and one between-participant factor, task (choice versus production). A given experimental nonword will be a function word for approximately half of the participants and a content word for the other half. This will be achieved by preparing two lists of items.

Randomization

The experiment will be run in small groups. The order in which the items are presented will be randomized for each group. Groups will be randomly assigned to either the production task or the choice task. There are two lists of items, and each group will be randomly assigned to one or the other list.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited from the Sona database at Washington University in St. Louis, which includes students who participate in experiments either for course credit or for payment. We indicate that participants in this study must be at least 18 years old and native speakers of English with no disabilities involving reading, spelling, hearing, or speech. For those participants who are paid, the rate is $5 per half hour, which is the expected duration of the experiment.

Sample size

We plan to obtain data from at least 80 eligible participants, approximately half of whom will participate in the choice task and the other half in the production task. Because participants will be run in groups, and because the number of people who sign up for and attend a group testing time cannot be completely predicted in advance, the final sample size may be slightly more than 80.

Sample size rationale

In Experiment 1 in this series, we found statistically significant effects with 40 participants and 22 experimental trials. The number of experimental trials is lower in the present study, 12 versus 22, so we are increasing the sample size to 80 on the idea that this will allow us to detect effects that are similar in size to those of Experiment 1. We did not conduct a formal power analysis given the lack of previous research on the topic.

Stopping rule

We will stop data collection when we have run enough groups of participants to have data from at least 80 participants who fit the eligibility criteria.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Manipulated variables are item type (content word or function word) and task (choice task or production task)

Measured variables

The main outcome variable will be whether the spelling has the phonologically appropriate double consonant or the phonologically appropriate single consonant after the appropriate vowel letter. In the production task, we expect to find some responses that do not fall into either of these categories, such as ‹abh›, ‹at›, and ‹ahb› for /ab/. These responses will be classified as “other,” and some analyses will examine the nature of and predictors of these responses. Another variable will be people’s responses to a short questionnaire at the end of the experiment asking them if they were previously aware of the proposed three-letter rule. This will be scored as yes or no.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Our main analysis will be a mixed-model analysis with data from experimental nonwords at the level of trials. Trials in the production task in which participants produce a response that coded as “other” will be omitted from this analysis. Any trials that are skipped or where the response is unreadable will also be omitted. The fixed factors are item type (content vs. function) and task (production vs. choice) and their interaction. The dependent variable is binary (final double consonant or not). For the production task, we will also conduct a mixed-model analysis to determine whether “other” responses are more common for content words than for function words. The dependent variable here is whether a response is in the "other" category and the predictor is item type. We will also conduct t tests by subjects and by items for the choice task data to determine whether participants select the single final consonant spelling significantly more often than expected by chance when the nonword is presented as a function word and significantly less often than expected by chance when it is presented as a content word. Additional analyses will examine the nature of the “other” spellings by placing them into categories including (1) two letters before the phonologically appropriate final consonant (e.g., ‹ahb› for /ɑb/), and (2) a vowel letter, the phonologically appropriate consonant or consonant doublet, and another letter (e.g., ‹ezze› or ‹ezh› for /ɛz/). We predict that participants will sometimes lengthen the spellings of content words in such ways. In another set of analyses, we will consider whether participants were aware of the three-letter rule before the experiment. If models that include rule awareness as a factor in addition to the other variables described above do not converge or show singularities, as is likely to be the case if few participants were aware of the rule before the experiment, we will run the models on the set of participants who were not aware of the rule. This will allow us to check if the main results hold true for this group.

Transformations

Inference criteria

We will use the standard p less than .05 criterion for determining statistical significance.

Data exclusion

We indicate in the recruitment and sign-up materials that participants in the experiment should be native speakers of English with no disabilities involve reading, spelling, hearing, or speech. If any people who do not qualify nevertheless sign up for and participate in the study, their data will be excluded. As described earlier, certain types of spellings on the production task that are classified as “other” will be excluded from the main analyses.

Missing data

Any trials that are missed or that cannot be scored will not be included in the analyses. In order to reduce the number of such trials, we will ask participants to check that they have answered every item and that their writing is readable.

Exploratory analysis

In exploratory analyses, we will compare the results from this study to the results from Experiment 1 of this series. First, we will determine whether performance on the filler words is similar for participants in the two experiments. It is likely that we will not find significant differences because both experiments are run with Washington University students. However, it is possible that differences could arise as a function of the time during the semester that the experiments are run (e.g., participants who are tested at the end of a semester may do more poorly due to the stress of upcoming exams or other factors). We will then compare performance on the experimental items across the two experiments.
