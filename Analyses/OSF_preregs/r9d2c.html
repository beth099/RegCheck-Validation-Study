The effect of accent and input modality when processing language switches in bilingual language comprehension

OSF ID: r9d2c | Registered: 2022-07-22T14:22:52.163642

Study Information

Hypotheses

While switching costs are typically observed in language-production studies (e.g., Meuter &amp; Allport, 1999; De Bruin et al., 2018), recent research suggests that they might be less consistently (or not at all) observed when comprehending speech (Declerck et al., 2019). This may be because parallel activation of a bilingual’s non-target language is reduced during comprehension so that less language control is necessary during comprehension switching tasks. However, findings regarding language switching and control are mixed across the comprehension literature and the amount of parallel activation (and through that the potential need for language control) might depend on many different variables. Here we study the role of two specific factors: the accent with which the L2 words are produced and the modality of word presentation (auditory or visual). In a first auditory comprehension switching task we previously conducted with Mandarin-English bilinguals (Experiment 1, which is part of a longitudinal study preregistered at: https://osf.io/dxm3h; although note that the analysis we conducted for Experiment 1 as part of this new series of experiments was not included in that previous preregistration), the participants exhibited significant switching costs when switching from English to Mandarin, but not from Mandarin to English. This may be because while processing the English L2 words, participants had to inhibit the influence of Mandarin, their L1. Such inhibition could have slowed down access to the L1 when exposed to Mandarin words following English words, which in turn could have led to a cost in processing the switch (Green, 1998). However, in that experiment, because we wanted the participants to be familiar with the accent, the words presented in English (i.e., the L2 words) were pronounced with a Mandarin (i.e., L1) accent. Since some research suggests that the phonetic information of a language can influence whether language co-activation is hindered or facilitated (e.g., Fricke et al., 2016; Lewendon, 2020), it is possible that English to Mandarin switching costs arose because during the English trials, the Mandarin lexical items were particularly strongly activated due to the L1 accent. This could have led to more interference during the processing of the English words and consequently, the bilinguals might have needed to inhibit Mandarin strongly during the English trials, which might have made it difficult to switch back to Mandarin. In other words, the L2 accent of the English words could (at least partly) explain why we observed significant switching costs in Experiment 1, and why there was an asymmetry in language costs. To further examine if and how pronunciation can influence processing of switching costs, Experiment 2 will directly compare bilinguals’ processing of language switches when they are exposed to L2 English words pronounced with an English accent (two-accents auditory condition) or with a Mandarin accent (one-accent auditory condition). In both conditions L1 Mandarin words will be pronounced with a Mandarin accent. Furthermore, several studies conducted with Mandarin-English bilinguals (e.g., Shiron, Liu, &amp; de Bruin, 2021; de Bruin &amp; Xu, submitted) show switching costs when words are presented visually. Based on these studies it remains, however, unclear how visual switches relate to auditory switches. Modality might be another factor influencing cross-linguistic lexical co-activation. Furthermore, some research suggests that when participants are exposed to languages with different orthographies, such language-specific orthography can reduce the activation of lexical representations in the other language, which in turn can reduce switching costs (Orfanidou &amp; Sumner, 2005). Thus, in Experiment 3, we will compare bilinguals’ processing of language switches when they are exposed to visually presented words. English and Mandarin are particularly interesting languages to study in a visual task given that they have very different scripts: while English uses the Latin alphabet, Mandarin is written with logograms. In this experiment, we will compare switching costs in the visual modality to the switching costs in the one-accent and the two-accents auditory conditions included in Experiment 2. These multiple comparisons should allow us to test if the processing of language switches differs across the visual and the auditory modalities. In both experiments, phonology (in the form of accents) and orthography might influence the processing of language switches during comprehension in two different directions: 1. Switching between different accents (i.e., between phonological systems) or orthographies might increase processing time and add a switch cost beyond the lexical level. This would not necessarily relate to language co-activation/competition at the lexical level but rather to increased demands at the phonological/orthographic level. In this case we would expect: a. A larger switching cost in the two-accents auditory condition than in the one-accent auditory condition reflected by a Trial Type (switch vs nonswitch) x Accent (one-accent vs two-accent) interaction. (Experiment 2) b. A larger cost in the visual condition than in the one-accent auditory condition reflected by a Trial Type x Modality (visual vs. one-accent condition). (Experiment 3) c. Furthermore, if switching in orthographies (visual) incurs a larger processing cost than switching between differences in accents (phonologies), we expect a larger switching cost in the visual condition than in the two-accents auditory condition reflected by a Trial Type x Modality (visual vs. two-accents condition). (Experiment 3) 2. Language-specific cues, coming both from the phonological (i.e., accents) or orthographic linguistic levels, might reduce parallel activation of the two languages and interference from the non-target language (Fricke et al., 2016; Lewendon, 2020; Orfanidou &amp; Summer, 2005). In this case we would expect: a. A larger switching cost in the one-accent auditory than in the two-accents auditory condition. In the one-accent auditory condition, English words produced with a Mandarin accent might increase interference from Mandarin. (Experiment 2) i. This could emerge as a three-way interaction between Language (Mandarin vs English) x Accent x Trial Type. There might be a larger cost of switching back to Mandarin in the one-accent condition because of more Mandarin activation and thus inhibition during English trials. ii. This could also emerge as a two-way interaction between Accent x Trial Type. There could be a larger cost switching back to Mandarin as well as a larger cost switching to English because of Mandarin interference (i.e., an effect of both resolving and applying inhibition). b. A larger cost in the one-accent auditory condition than in the visual condition as the visual cues might reduce parallel activation more than the (one-accent) auditory cues. This could emerge as two- (Modality (visual vs one-accent) x Trial Type) or three-way interactions (Language x Modality (visual vs one-accent condition) x Trial Type). (Experiment 3) c. If cross-linguistic differences in scripts reduce lexical co-activation (see e.g., Orfanidou &amp; Sumner, 2005) more than differences in accents (phonologies), then we may also find smaller costs in the visual than in the two-accents condition, reflected by two- (Modality x Trial Type) or three-way interactions (Language x Modality (visual vs two-accents condition) x Trial Type). Finally, Experiment 1 will also allow us to test if the L1 accent during L2 trials in general helps bilinguals process the L2 (due to familiarity with L1 pronunciation) or if, on the contrary, due to heightened interference from the L1 lexicon, it makes such processing more difficult. Thus, we expect to observe a significant Language x Accent interaction (with only English but not Mandarin RTs differing between accent conditions). If the L1 accent helps bilinguals process the L2 English due to familiarity, then this interaction should reflect that responses to the L2 trials are overall faster in the condition with the L1 accent (one-accent condition) than in the condition with the L2 accent (two-accents condition). If on the contrary the L1 accent interferes with L2 English processing in general, then responses to the L2 trials should be slower in that condition (one-accent) than in the condition with the L2 accent (two-accent condition). This will allow us to examine any potential effects of accents on language processing in general, regardless of it being a language switch.

Study design

We use a series of comprehension switching tasks to assess bilinguals’ processing of language switches during comprehension. While Experiment 1 only contained the within-subject variables Language (English vs Mandarin) and Trial Type (switch vs non-switch trials), Experiments 2 and 3 each contain an additional within-subject variable, as described below: Experiment 2: In this task, participants are presented with spoken words in English or in Mandarin and asked to perform a semantic decision task to indicate whether the presented words represent something animate (e.g., “dog”) or inanimate (e.g., “table”) (cf. Declerck et al., 2019). While in Experiment 1, the words presented in English were pronounced with a Mandarin accent, in Experiment 2, we manipulate in separate testing blocks whether the English words are pronounced with a Mandarin or with an English accent. The Mandarin words will all be presented with a Mandarin (L1) accent. Experiment 3: The task of this experiment is the same as the ones in Experiments 1 and 2, except that the words are presented in the visual modality. Experiments 2 and 3 are followed by a language background questionnaire measuring the participants’ use of each language, their proficiency and their language switching behaviours. They are also followed by lexical decision tasks in English and in Mandarin, which will allow us to further assess participants’ proficiency in the two languages.

Randomization

The order in which participants will complete the three testing blocks (i.e., the two blocks of Experiment 2 and the single block of Experiment 3) will be randomized and counterbalanced across participants. The set of items used in each task (L1 or L2 auditory experiment, or visual experiment) will also be counterbalanced across participants.

Explanation of existing data

We have already collected and analysed the data of Experiment 1 and the results of this experiment form the basis for Experiments 2 and 3. Data collection for Experiments 2 and 3 has not started yet.

Data collection procedures

We will recruit Mandarin/English bilinguals who are currently living or have previously lived in the UK, who have no reading, hearing or vision impairments. We will recruit them via Prolific or via personal connections and existing databases in the UK. In the latter case, the participants will be reimbursed with Amazon.co.uk vouchers. If we cannot reach the intended sample size, we might open up the study to US participants too (in which case, we will use recordings with an American English instead of British English accent). Importantly, Experiments 2 and 3 will be included in a single online session so that the same participants will complete the two experiments. Data collection should be completed by December 2022.

Sample size

For Experiments 2 and 3, our aim is to recruit 96 participants in total based on a power analysis (see below) and due to our counterbalancing scheme.

Sample size rationale

We ran a series of simulations using mixed-effect models to determine our sample size. First, we used the data of a previous language switching comprehension study with visual stimuli and found that a sample size of 90 would allow us to detect a switching cost with an effect size of 0.021 with 91% power. Second, to determine what would be the appropriate sample size for the auditory comprehension trials, we used the Mandarin trials of Experiment 1 (to focus on the significant switching costs we observed in Experiment 1) and found that a sample size of 90 would allow us to detect an English-to-Mandarin switching cost with an effect size of 0.013 with 72% power. The sample size of 96 will thus give us sufficient power to detect switching costs of a small to medium size. However, we could not run simulations with the exact factors that be will included in Experiments 2 and 3 (i.e., accent and modality) as previous studies have never used similar designs.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will analyse the data using mixed-effect models in R with the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2014). We will start the analyses with maximal structures (Barr et al., 2013), with models that will include random intercepts for participants and items and all within-participant/-item predictors. In the case of non-converging models, we will first remove correlations between random slopes and random intercepts. If the models still do not converge, we will reduce the random effect structures by removing the random slopes explaining the least variance until convergence is reached. Experiment 2: The full model will include: -fixed effects: Language x Trial Type x Accent -random slopes for Language, Trial Type and Accent per participant and for Language, Trial Type and Accent per item Experiment 3: The full model will include: -fixed effects: Language x Trial Type x Modality (three levels) -random slopes for Language, Trial Type and Modality per participant and for Language, Trial Type and Modality per item

Transformations

We will log transform the RTs to improve the normality of the distribution and contrast-code the two-level categorical predictors as -0.5 and 0.5. We will contrast-code the three-level predictor (Modality) in Experiment 3 using anova coding, in order to be able to perform the following two comparisons: -the visual condition (-0.333) vs one-accent auditory (0.667; two-accent coded as -0.333) -the visual condition (-0.333) vs two-accents auditory (0.667; one-accent coded as -0.333)

Inference criteria

P-values smaller than .05 will be interpreted as significant effects.

Data exclusion

Participants will be excluded from the analysis of a given experiment if: • They report having any known neurological impairment, reading or language disability, or if they have vision or hearing issues that are not fixed through e.g., glasses or hearing aids • They do not score at least 70% accuracy on the animacy judgment task. For the analysis of RTs, trials will be removed following these procedures: • Outlier RTs will be removed as 2.5SDs below or above the mean per participant, per condition/language/trial type, per task • RTs below 100ms will be removed • The first trial after a break will be removed as trial type cannot be determined

Missing data

We will include participants with missing data in the background measures (e.g., proficiency questionnaire) as long as they have completed the comprehension tasks.
