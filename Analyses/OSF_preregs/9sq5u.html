Rational Number Arithmetic

OSF ID: 9sq5u | Registered: 2020-08-24T16:51:22.043372

Study Information

Hypotheses

The current study has four main hypotheses: 1. Accuracy on the main arithmetic task will be higher in the different-notation condition than in the same-notation condition. 2. We predict a notation*operation interaction effect on accuracy on the main arithmetic task, such that accuracy on decimal problems relative to fraction problems will be higher for addition than for multiplication. 3. On the main arithmetic task, participants will convert fractions to decimals or vice versa more often in the different-notation condition than in the same-notation condition. 4. On the main arithmetic task, in the different-notation condition, the frequency of converting fractions to decimals or vice versa on addition relative to multiplication trials will be higher for fractions than for decimals. 5. On the mixed arithmetic task, the frequency of converting fractions into decimals will be higher on addition than multiplication problems, and the frequency of converting decimals into fractions will be higher on multiplication than addition problems.

Is there any additional blinding in this study?

Participants will not be informed of the experimental manipulation prior to the study, but they will know whether the problems they receive are presented in same-notation or different-notation pairs.

Study design

The study will employ a 2*2*2 mixed factorial design. Condition (same- or different-notation) is a between-subjects factor and will be randomly assigned. Arithmetic operation (multiplication vs. addition) and number type (fraction or decimal) are within-subject factors.

Randomization

Individuals will be randomly assigned to conditions with the requirement that each condition is assigned equally often. Eight different versions were created where four versions of the study correspond to the different-notation condition and four versions of the study correspond to the same-notation condition. A randomizer component was created through Qualtrics such that one of the eight versions will be randomly presented to the participants and the conditions will be evenly presented to the participants.

Explanation of existing data

We have conducted the same study but in a in-person format. A total of 28 participants were ran before the study was paused due to Covid-19. By the time of the pre-registration, no data has been collected for the current study.

Data collection procedures

Participation in the study will involve an interview session of one hour or less, conducted through Zoom. Participants will be instructed to fill out a Qualtrics survey with the guidance of the experimenter. Participants will be current undergraduate students that are enrolled in eligible psychology courses (general psychology or upper-level psychology that allow extra credit for participants) and are age 18 or older. Students will be recruited through the university psychology research pool website. Information about the study will be posted and students will have the opportunity to sign up for time-slots for the study. Students will receive either course credit or extra credit depending on the eligible course they are enrolled as compensation for your participation.

Sample size

Our target group will be 80.

Sample size rationale

We conducted a power analysis using G*Power. We set the power at .80 to detect a medium effect size of d as .50 at .20 alpha error probability. The sample size based off G*Power is 74. We choose 80 as it is the closest number to allow us complete the sequence of 8 versions of the tasks while also satisfying the sample size recommendation derived from G*Power.

Stopping rule

We will post participant sign-up slots by week. No more spots will be posted once we have reached our target sample size. However, we will complete all sessions and include all data from participants who have already signed up at the time we stop posting new spots, even if this results in a larger-than-planned sample size.

Variables

Manipulated variables

We manipulated the type of number being presented side by side in the rational number arithmetic section. In the same-notation condition, two arithmetic problems of the same number type (either both fraction or both decimal) were presented side by side for each trial. In the different-notation condition, two arithmetic problems of different number types (one fraction and one decimal) were presented side by side. In the different-notation condition, in each pair of problems presented side-by-side, one operand in one problem will be equivalent to one operand in the other problem (e.g., if one operand in the problem on the left is 1/2, one operand in the problem on the right could be 0.5). In both conditions, the pairs of problems presented side by side always involve the same arithmetic operation.

Measured variables

1. Accuracy on the main arithmetic task (proportion correct answers) 2. Proportion of trials on which participants converted to a different notation (e.g., converted fractions to decimals in a fraction problem, or converted decimals to fractions in a decimal problem) in the main arithmetic task 3. Accuracy on the mixed arithmetic task (proportion correct answers) 4a. Proportion of trials on which participants converted fractions to decimals in the mixed arithmetic task 4b. Proportion of trials on which participants converted decimals to fractions in the mixed arithmetic task

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

A 2 (rational number type: fraction or decimal) x 2 (operation: addition or multiplication) x 2 (condition: same-notation or different-notation) mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) with accuracy on the main arithmetic task as the dependent variable will be conducted to test Hypotheses 1-2. HYPOTHESIS 1. Accuracy on the main arithmetic task will be higher in the different-notation condition than in the same-notation condition. The presence of a main effect of condition on accuracy in the predicted direction would confirm this hypothesis. HYPOTHESIS 2. Accuracy on decimal problems relative to fraction problems will be higher for addition than for multiplication. The presence of a notation*operation interaction effect on accuracy in the predicted direction would confirm this hypothesis. To test Hypothesis 3, we will conduct ANOVA with proportion of trials in which participants converted to a different notation as the dependent variable. The model structure will be the same as in the previous ANOVA. HYPOTHESIS 3. On the main arithmetic task, participants will convert fractions to decimals or vice versa more often in the different-notation condition than in the same-notation condition. A main effect of condition in the predicted direction would confirm this hypothesis. To test Hypothesis 4, we will repeat the above ANOVA except using only data from participants in the different-notation condition. HYPOTHESIS 4. On the main arithmetic task, in the different-notation condition, the frequency of converting fractions to decimals or vice versa on addition relative to multiplication trials will be higher for fractions than for decimals. This hypothesis would be confirmed by the presence of a notation*operation interaction effect in the predicted direction on proportion of trials in which participants convert fractions to decimals or vice versa. To test Hypothesis 5, we will conduct two one-way ANOVAs with the same factors with operation (addition or multiplication) as a within-subjects factor. In one ANOVA, the dependent variable will be proportion of trials in which participants converted to fractions; in the other ANOVA, the dependent variable will be proportion of trials in which participants converted to decimals. HYPOTHESIS 5. On the mixed arithmetic task, the frequency of converting fractions into decimals will be higher on addition than multiplication problems, and the frequency of converting decimals into fractions will be higher on multiplication than addition problems. These hypotheses would be confirmed by main effects in the predicted directions in the two analyses.

Transformations

For the linear regression analyses performed in the exploratory analysis, the predictor (condition: same-notation vs. different-notation) will be centered. For the Math attitude scale included in the exploratory analysis, for the attitude with new techniques subscale, three items will be reverse coded in order to be included in the analysis.

Inference criteria

A two-tailed p-value of less than .05 will be used for all analyses to determine statistical significance.

Data exclusion

Outliers will be included. Data from participants who fail to complete a given task will be excluded from analyses involving that task.

Exploratory analysis

We will include the following measures in addition to the manipulated and measure variable in order to run exploratory analysis. Math attitude: The study generated a questionnaire consisting of 16 questions measuring participants’ math anxiety, math self-efficacy and their attitude towards trying new strategies. he math anxiety scale and math self-efficacy scale item were adopted from Hendy et al., (2014). Items that were not appropriate for the current context were removed. In total, 5 items measuring math anxiety and 5 items measuring math self-efficacy were kept. 6 additional items were created to capture students' attitude towards trying new techniques with math problems. Working memory: Alpha span is used to measure working memory (Craik et al., 2018). The participants were given a list of words and were asked to repeat back the words in alphabetical order. The length of the word list ranges from 2 to 8 words. There are two different word lists for the same length. Participants were given the word list with the length increasing. The overall percentage of accuracy recall was measured for working memory. Rational Number Conversion ability: The study created a task to measure this ability. The task included twelve items, six of the items were decimal numbers and six of the items were fraction, participants were asked to convert the decimal numbers into fraction forms and fraction numbers into decimal forms. The overall percentage accuracy were measured as their ability to convert rational numbers. We plan to conduct the following exploratory analysis: 1. A linear regression setting the condition (same-notation vs. different-notation) as IV and the accuracy on the main arithmetic task as DV, and working memory as the moderator. 2. A linear regression setting the condition (same-notation vs. different-notation) as IV and the accuracy on the main arithmetic task as DV, and math attitude as the moderator. 3. A linear regression setting the condition (same-notation vs. different-notation) as IV and the accuracy on the main arithmetic task as DV, and rational number conversion ability as the moderator.
