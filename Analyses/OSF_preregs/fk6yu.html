ESM mind wandering and the valence of thoughts

Study Information

Hypotheses

This study employs the Experience sampling method (ESM), in which participants receive prompts on their smartphone and are instructed to answer questions regarding their experience in the “moment” right before they responded to the prompt. Our confirmatory hypotheses are as follows: 1. First, we predict higher Well-Being for moments when people report being Present versus Not-Present (i.e., a main effect of what we refer to as “Attention State”). This confirmatory hypothesis serves as a replication of a 2010 study by Killingsworth &amp; Gilbert, although this previous study asked participants about their “thoughts” (which could include moments with or without inner speech), whereas the current study parses moments based on whether a participant reports experiencing “Inner Speech” vs. “NO Inner Speech” (with the latter divided into different types, e.g., Visual Imagery, Body Sensations). Our confirmatory hypothesis is for moments that contain Inner Speech, which allows for more specificity in analyzing type of thought (an advantage over the Killingsworth and Gilbert paper, which did not distinguish type of thought), and since this is what was analyzed in our pilot studies. 2. Second, using data from moments in which participants report having experienced Inner Speech, we predict that the above effect of Attention state on Well-Being) can be accounted for (i.e., mediated) by the self-reported “Objective Valence” of that inner speech. That is, being Present is predicted to be associated with more positive inner speech, and in turn, more positive inner speech is predicted to be associated with better Well-Being. As such, the effect of Attention State on Well-Being will be due entirely, or partially, to objective Valence. This mediation analysis will be run with and without using Clarity as a covariate because, in pilot studies, clarity absorbed a significant amount of variance in Well-Being. 3. Third, we predict that the degree of Reactivity (to one’s Inner Speech, on a scale from “No reaction at all” to “Extreme reaction”) will moderate the relationship between Objective Valence and Well-being, with the relationship being stronger in moments when people report higher Reactivity. Our exploratory questions are as follows: • First, we will explore whether the Five Factor Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) total, or one of the FFMQ subscale scores, further moderates the effect of the moderation of Reactivity and Objective Valence on Well-Being (i.e. a 3-way interaction). Note that because this is a general student sample unlikely to have extensive mindfulness training and/or meditation practice, the FFMQ total score will exclude the observing subscale. This is a standard practice when calculating a FFMQ total score, which will be calculated in this way whenever analyzed in the study. • Second, based on previous unpublished research by one of the authors (Jonathan Schooler), we will explore whether the “Interestingness” of the Inner Speech has a moderating effect on the relationship between Objective Valence and Well-Being, with the prediction that the relationship will be stronger when Inner Speech is less interesting. We also explore whether adding “Interestingness” to the models testing confirmatory analyses improves the model fit (as we predict will be the case by adding “Clarity”, see above). • Third, we will explore whether the distribution of Current Activity differs across the two Attention States (Present vs. Not Present). We will also see if including Current Activity as a fixed effect to the models testing confirmatory analyses improves the model fit (as we predict will be the case by adding “Clarity”, see above). • Fourth, we will explore the frequency/ratings of different experiences; Present vs. Not Present, Inner Speech vs. No Inner Speech (and the subtypes No Inner Speech experiences), Clarity, Interestingness, different Activities. And, we will explore whether Well-Being varies across different types of experiences not listed in our confirmatory hypotheses (e.g., Inner Speech vs. No Inner Speech). And, we will explore whether frequencies of types of experiences are associated with certain traits, like Gender, FFMQ or Personality (Big Five). • Fifth, the aforementioned analyses conducted for moments in which participants report “Inner Speech” will be explored for moments in which they report “No Inner Speech”.

Study design

There are three main independent variables for the confirmatory analyses in this study, all of which are captured in a “moment” (i.e., they are “state” and not “trait”): 1) Attention state, 2) Objective Valence, and 3) Reactivity. All variables are within-subject and are captured multiple times within and across days (up to 42 times per participant). The variables are orthogonal to one another.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited through the UCSD Diego (and potentially at UC Santa Barbara, if enrollment is judged to be too slow at UCSD alone) SONA participant pool, run by the Department of Psychology. To participate, participants must be over 18 years old and own a smartphone device. Participants will be compensated with research credits.

Sample size

Data will be collected from ~500 undergraduate students.

Sample size rationale

Rather than using a formal power analysis, we chose our sample size based on the pilot data, after taking into account the various differences in experimental design between the piloting and the current version. We aim to reach a total number of retained observations that is at least as high as the number of observations from the most recent pilot study. Our pilot study had an n of 760, which yielded a total of 10,617 moments, which was large enough to yield significant effects for our main hypotheses. Based on what we found in pilot studies, we expect to retain about 85% of participants for analysis, whereas about 15% will be excluded after data cleaning (described below). In the current study, we are more than doubling the number of “moments” we attempt to obtain from each participant (42 in the current study vs. 18 in the pilot studies); therefore we estimate that we will need fewer participants (500) in the current study (vs. 760 in as the pilot studies).

Stopping rule

Data collection will stop when the total number of participants equals the planned sample size. More participants will be tested if the sample size drops to below the planned sample size due to participants who admit that they did not comply with the instruction (see “failing check tests or suspicion probes”, below). Note that we do this data cleaning before we analytically test our hypotheses.

Variables

Manipulated variables

There are no manipulated variables.

Measured variables

There are three main independent variables for the confirmatory analyses: 1) Attention state, 2) Objective Valence, and 3) Reactivity. • Attention State is the first dichotomous variable measured; participants respond either 1) “PRESENT: My attention WAS related to my current activity, immediate surroundings, or inner experience”, which is internally coded as “Present”; or 2) “NOT-PRESENT: My attention was NOT related to my current activity, immediate surroundings”, which was internally coded as “Not-present”. • Objective Valence is measured as a continuous variable on a 7-point scale with three anchor points: 2 endpoints (-3 =Very Negative, and +3 = Very Positive) and one midpoint (0, Neutral). • Reactivity is measured as a continuous variable on a 7-point scale with with three anchor points: 2 endpoints (1 = No reaction at all, and 7 = Extreme reaction), and one midpoint (3, Moderate reaction) There are three main independent variables for the exploratory analyses in this study: • FFMQ, which is a validated trait measure of mindfulness, which is a between-participant measure. • Big Five Personality, which is a validated trait measure of personality, which is a between-participant measure. • Inner speech is measured as a dichotomous variable, where participants respond either 1) I was experiencing inner speech (internally coded as “inner speech”), or 2) I was NOT experiencing inner speech (internally coded as “inner experience”). If (2) inner experience is selected, participants choose from one of eight options for type of inner experience: o 1) BODY: “I was experiencing body sensations, e.g. hunger” o 2) EMOTION: “I was experiencing emotions, e.g. sadness” o 3) ENVIRONMENT : “I was noticing the environment, e.g. looking at the trees” o 4) MUSIC/SOUNDS : “I was listening to music/podcast, e.g. with my earphones in, or imagining music/sounds” o 5) VISUAL IMAGERY: “I was experiencing visual imagery, e.g. imagining my dog” o 6) ANOTHER PERSON: “I was experiencing another person, e.g. holding hands” o 7) BLANK MIND : “I was not thinking about anything at all– my mind was completely blank” o 8) OTHER: ‘Please type in” There is one DV, which is Well-Being and is a continuous variable on a 7-point scale with three anchor points: 2 endpoints (-3 =Very Bad, and +3 = Very Good) and one neutral midpoint (0, Neutral). Three further variables are included as possible covariates; these are Clarity, Interestingness, and Current Activity. Clarity and Interestingness are measured as continuous variables on a 7-point scale with 2 anchor points (1, Not at all clear/interesting, 7 = Extremely clear/interesting). Current Activity is measured by asking “Whether in-person or online, what type of activity were you primarily doing? (select one)”, and participants are given six options adapted from Oerlemans et al. (2011): o Social activity (e.g., being with family, friends, or peers; phone calls or messages; attending a party or group outing) o Physical activity (e.g., exercising, sports, walking, bicycling, hiking) o Restful activity (e.g., eating, resting, taking a nap, doing nothing, reading for fun, watching TV or videos, browsing the internet or social media) o Household activity (e.g., preparing meals, grocery shopping, household finances, cleaning or other chores) o Cognitive activity (e.g., studying, homework, attending lecture, learning something new, puzzle solving o Other activity (text entry)

Indices

All state versions of the predictor variables (reactivity, valence, clarity, interesting) are person-mean centered prior to the following analyses in order to capture the within-subject differences of that variable across prompt (as opposed to the average score across all prompts for each person, which would capture between-subject differences).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

First, using data from moments in which participants report having experienced “inner speech”, we predict higher Well-Being for moments when people report being Present versus Not-Present (i.e., a main effect of Attention State), in line with the results of Killingsworth &amp; Gilbert, 2010. For this analysis we use a multilevel model where the unit of analysis is “moment”, Attention State is the predictor variable, Well-Being is the dependent variable, and Participant ID is entered as a random effect. Second, using data from moments in which participants report having experienced Inner Speech, we predict the above effect of Attention State on Well-being can be accounted for (i.e., mediated) by the self-reported “Objective Valence” of that inner speech. That is, if being present is associated with more positive thought content, and more positive thought content is associated with better Well-Being, then the effect of Attention State on Well-Being may be due entirely, or partially, to Objective Valence,. As such, we will compare the effect of Attention State on Well-Being without Objective Valence (above) vs. with Objective Valence added to the model as a fixed effect. If the effect of Attention State is substantially reduced, we can conclude the effect of Attention State on Well-Being may be (simply) due to people having more positive Inner Speech when they are Present. (As explained above, this analysis rests on finding a significant zero-order correlation between Objective Valence and Attention State.) - This will be run with and without using state Clarity as a covariate because in pilot studies Clarity absorbed a significant amount of variance in Well-Being. Third, we predict that the degree of Reactivity (to one’s Inner Speech, on a scale from “No reaction at all” to “Extreme reaction”) will moderate the relationship between Objective Valence and Well-being, with the relationship being stronger in moments when people report higher Reactivity. This will involve adding an interaction term to the multilevel model.

Transformations

All state versions of the predictor variables (reactivity, valence, clarity, interesting) are person-mean centered prior to the analyses in order to capture the within-subject differences of that variable across prompt (as opposed to the average score across all prompts for each person, which would capture between-subject differences).

Inference criteria

We will use the standard p&lt;.05 criteria as well as confidence intervals.

Data exclusion

Participants will be deleted who have 0 inner speech prompts; beyond this, a response is required for all questions so we do not anticipate missing data.

Missing data

Participants will be deleted who have 0 inner speech prompts; beyond this, a response is required for all questions so we do not anticipate missing data.

Exploratory analysis

Exploratory Analyses These are five possible exploratory analyses for this study. First, we will explore whether the Five Factor Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ) total, or one of the FFMQ subscale scores, further moderates the effect of the moderation of Reactivity and Objective Valence on Well-Being. This will be tested via a series of 3-way interactions in the multilevel model. Second, we will explore whether Interestingness has a moderating effect on the relationship between Objective Valence and Well-being, via a 2-way interaction in the multilevel model. We will also explore whether adding Interestingness as a fixed effect to the models testing confirmatory analyses improves the model fit. Third, we will explore whether the distribution of Current Activity differs across the two Attention States (Present vs. Not Present) via a chi-square analysis. We will also see if including Current Activity as a fixed effect to the models testing confirmatory analyses improves the model fit Fourth, we will explore the descriptive frequency/ratings of different experiences; Present vs. Not Present, Inner Speech vs. No Inner Speech (and the subtypes No Inner Speech experiences), Clarity, Interestingness, different Activities. And, we will explore whether Well-Being varies across different types of experiences not listed in our confirmatory hypotheses (e.g., Inner Speech vs. No Inner Speech), via chi-square and ANOVA analyses. And, we will explore whether frequencies of types of moments are associated with certain traits, like Gender, FFMQ or Personality (Big Five), via chi-square and ANOVA analyses. Fifth, the aforementioned analyses conducted for moments in which participants report “Inner Speech” will be explored for moments in which they report “No Inner Speech”.
