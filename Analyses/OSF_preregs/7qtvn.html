Can strong retro-cues have an effect on memory performance in Constant &amp; Liesefeld's paradigm?

OSF ID: 7qtvn | Registered: 2023-11-29T14:15:06.352568

Study Information

Hypotheses

In this study, we will present a black tilted bar (cue) for a short duration after the memory display disappears. When valid, the cue will be a bar that shares its tilt and position with one of the (previously seen) to-be-remembered target and will indicate with a validity of 100 % that the corresponding target is the true target (i.e., the one participants will be asked to recall the color; also called the probe). When neutral, the cue will be a vertical bar presented centrally. We expect that the recall error should be lower (i.e., better) when the cue is valid compared to when it's neutral. We predict that when the cue is neutral, recall error for more salient targets should be lower than for less salient targets. Given data from previous spatial or feature pre-cue experiments, where salience still had an effect when the cue was valid, we predict that when the cue is valid, recall error for more salient targets should still be lower than for less salient targets. Overall, the expected recall errors are: valid 45° &lt; valid 28° &lt; valid 12° neutral 45° &lt; neutral 28° &lt; neutral 12° valid 45° &lt; neutral 45° valid 28° &lt; neutral 28° valid 12° &lt; neutral 12°

Study design

We have a within-subjects design with 2 factors: the degree of tilt (12°, 28°, 45°) and the cue validity (valid/neutral). We will use a task where participants have to memorize the color of three tilted bars in a dense array of 33 vertical colored bars (36 bars in total). - The memory display will appear for 350 ms. - 750 ms after the memory display disappears, a black bar (i.e., the retro-cue) will appear for 500 ms. It can either be vertical and appear centrally (neutral cue), or share its tilt and location with one of the targets that just appeared (100 % valid cue). - 750 ms after the cue disappears (totaling to 2 second since memory display disappeared), participants must recall the color of one probed tilted bar (probe) by clicking on a color wheel. There will be three tilted bars (12°, 28° and 45°) in each display. Instructions explicitly give the probability that the cue is valid, and this is reminded between each block. There will be 14 blocks of 36 trials for a total of 504 trials. This means there will be 84 valid and 84 neutral trials for each tilt. All other variables (e.g. colors, position of target and distractors) will be selected randomly from pre-set lists (e.g. 360 possible colors).

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

2 student assistants will conduct the experiment and recruit the participants from our standard students population at the University of Geneva. They will not be color-deficient and their vision will be normal/corrected. The experiment will last approximately 1h and they will be compensated with course credits.

Sample size

We will perform sequential testing with Bayes Factors (BF), following the recommendations by Schönbrodt &amp; Wagenmakers (2018). We will initially collect 10 participants and then continue testing and computing the Bayes factor until the a priori defined level of evidence: A BF of 6 in favor of the alternative or a BF of 3 in favor of the null hypothesis for each critical test (as specified in Statistical models part) is reached. For practical reasons (group testing; analysis-related time and effort), calculations will be performed after adding batches of data sets (2-5). If we do not reach the a priori defined level of evidence with our sequential testing procedure within the constraints of cost-effectiveness (here: 30 participants), we will stop testing (see Schönbrodt &amp; Wagenmakers, 2018). Note that the BF of studies that does not reach the a priori threshold can still be interpreted.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will conduct Bayesian t tests (planned comparisons) to test our hypotheses. For directional hypotheses (e.g., 28° neutral &lt; 12° neutral), directional tests will be used. The critical hypotheses that determine the stopping rule are: 1. The recall error for valid 12° should be higher than for valid 28°, the recall error for valid 12° should be higher than for valid 45°, and the recall error for valid 28° should be higher than for valid 45° (3 one-tailed t tests, expecting BF10 &gt;= 6) 2. The recall error for neutral 12° should be higher than for neutral 28°, the recall error for neutral 12° should be higher than for neutral 45°, and the recall error for neutral 28° should be higher than for neutral 45° (3 one-tailed t tests, expecting BF10 &gt;= 6) 3. The recall error for each neutral target should be higher than for the corresponding valid target (3 one-tailed t tests, expecting BF10 &gt;= 6), that is: neutral 12° &gt; valid 12°; neutral 28° &gt; valid 28°; neutral 45° &gt; valid 45°. The analyses will be made either with custom Python code, or with the latest available JASP version with default settings. To confirm with common practice in the literature, we will (additionally) run and report the respective traditional (frequentist) significance tests.

Inference criteria

A BF10 of 6 will be considered as sufficient evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis and a BF01 of 3 will be considered as sufficient evidence in favor of the null hypothesis. For frequentist tests, a p value below .05 will be considered as sufficient evidence. Both criteria (frequentist and bayesian) should be met to consider a result significant (e.g., p &lt; .05 AND BF10 &gt;= 6).

Data exclusion

The data of participants who do not follow instructions, evidenced by an average recall error of over 80° (slightly below chance level), will be excluded.
