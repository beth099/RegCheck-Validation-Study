How temporal discontinuities in the structure of events influence temporal compression in episodic memory.

OSF ID: 3s46f | Registered: 2021-03-17T10:49:55.490435

Study Information

Hypotheses

Our main hypothesis is that removing less informative parts of the video should mimic the process by which information from the continuous stream of experience is selected for representing the unfolding of events in episodic memory. Thus, the rate of temporal compression (i.e., the time needed to remember relative to the actual stimulus duration) should be lower when remembering edited compared to intact videos. In the two studies, hypotheses are centered on four measures of interest: (1) temporal compression rates, (2) the number of recalled actions, (3) the number of recalled actions per unit of the actual video duration and (4) estimates of the time needed to remember an action (i.e., the number of recalled of actions relative to the total duration of memory replay). Study 1: - (1) We predict that temporal compression rates will be smaller for memories of edited videos compare to memories of complete videos. - (2) Although the total number of recalled actions might be smaller for edited compared to complete videos, the number of recalled actions per unit of time (of the actual video duration) will be higher for edited compared to complete videos because they include proportionally more informative regions of the sensory stream. - (3) We expect that estimates of the time needed to remember an action will be similar between edited and complete videos (i.e., our manipulation should affect the total rate of temporal compression but not the representation of individual actions per se). Study 2: Study 2 will be conducted to control for differences in video duration that might in part account for the results of Study 1 (i.e., edited videos will be shorter than intact videos, which might in part explain differences in compression rates). Thus, we will use videos that include black screens instead of ellipses, such that the total duration of intact and edited videos will be equivalent. - (1) When excluding black screen durations in the estimation of temporal compression rates for edited videos, we predict that temporal compression rates will be smaller for memories of edited videos compare to memories of complete videos. However, when considering the entire stimulus duration (including back screens), temporal compression rates might be similar between the two types of videos. - (2) Although the total number of recalled actions might be smaller for edited compared to complete videos, the number of recalled actions per unit of time will be higher for edited videos compared to complete videos (note that in this analysis the unit of time is the total video duration for intact videos and the total video duration minus back screens for the edited videos). - (3) We expect that estimates of the time needed to remember an action (i.e., the number of recalled of actions relative to the total duration of memory replay) will be similar between edited and complete videos.

Study design

In both Study 1 and Study 2, the type of video (intact vs edited) is manipulated between subjects. Both studies involve a computer memory task in which subjects have to mentally replay a series of videos depicting people performing daily life activities. Study 1: Five videos depicting a sequence of actions performed by a person during daily life activities (e.g., gardening, grocery shopping) will be used (see Smith, Newberry &amp; Bailey, 2020). For each video, two film versions were made: a complete and an ellipse version. The two film versions depict the same activity performed by the same person. The only difference between the two versions is that ellipse videos involve temporal ellipses (parts of the movies that are not presented), such that some segments of the complete videos are not shown in the ellipse videos. For example, the complete video showing a person shopping begins with the person entering in the grocery, walking through the shelves and then picking a first item. In the ellipse version of this video, the action of walking through the shelves of the grocery is replaced by a temporal ellipse, such that the video illustrates the person entering in the grocery and, after a one second temporal ellipse, the person picking an item. The number and placement of ellipses in each video was determined a priori based on a pilot study assessing memory for the complete version of the videos. More precisely, for each video, actions that were reported by fewer than half of the subjects when they remembered the video were replaced by temporal ellipses. Thus, parts of the video that were removed were those that were the least frequently reported when remembering the video, thus corresponding to the hypothesized temporal gaps in memories. Temporal ellipses consisted of a 1 seconds (or 25 frames) black screen, with transitions between the movie and the ellipses being smoothed using a 0.4 seconds (10 frames) progressive smoothing. Study 1 involves a two-group between-subjects design in which half of the subjects will receive a memory task that involve five complete videos (complete condition) whereas the other half of the subjects will receive the exact same videos with temporal ellipses (ellipse condition). Each participant will be randomly assigned to either the complete condition or the ellipse condition. In the two experimental conditions, each trial will begin with the presentation of a fixation cross on the computer screen for a duration of 3 s. Then, the video will be presented on the screen and participant will be instructed to watch the video carefully and to pay attention to all actions that the person performs in the video. Once the video is finished, subjects will have to try to mentally re-experience the unfolding of the video in as much detail as possible (and not as quickly as possible). More precisely, they will be instructed to press the "SPACEBAR" to indicate that they start to mentally re-experience the video and once again when they finish to mentally relive the video. Finally, immediately after the mental re-experience of the video, subjects will be invited to describe, as accurately and precisely as possible, every actions that came to their mind during their mental replay of the video. To this end, a writing area will be presented on the computer screen and subjects will be asked to write down, in the most accurate and detailed way as possible, all the actions that came to their mind when they tried to mentally re-experience the unfolding of the video. They will be instructed to write each action on a different line by pressing the "ENTER" key to move from one line to the next. They will be asked to only report the actions that they had previously re-experienced and not additional actions. Then, they will have to press a button displayed under the writing area to begin the next trial. In the ellipse condition, two additional instructions will be provided. First, subjects will be instructed to ignore the temporal ellipses during the viewing of the videos. Second, they will be asked not to recall (and thus report) the temporal ellipses but only the actions that happened in the videos. In total, each subject will receive 5 trials (which correspond to the five videos), presented in random order. The task will be performed individually on the Gorilla online experience platform (for information, see https://gorilla.sc/), which provides a reliable video display and high-precision reaction time recording (for evidence supporting the effectiveness of the Gorilla platform in collecting online behavioral data, see Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020). Before starting the experimental trials, subjects will first complete one practice trial, with a different video (either complete or involving temporal ellipses depending on the experimental condition), in order to familiarize them with the entire procedure. Study 2: The experimental procedure of Study 2 will be similar to Study 1, with the following modifications. First, the ellipse condition will be replace by a black screen condition. In this latter condition, parts of the complete video will be replaced by a black screen of the same duration as the removed parts of the complete video. For example, if we go back to the example given earlier (see Study 1), the action of walking through the shelves of the grocery presented in the complete video during 15 seconds will be replaced by a black screen of 15 seconds in the black screen version of the video. It follows that the number and temporal position of black screens and temporal ellipses in their respective version of each video are exactly the same. The only difference between these two types of videos is that black screen durations depend on the durations of the corresponding (non-represented) actions whereas temporal ellipses are all fixed to 1 second. As a corollary, complete and black screen versions of the five videos have the same duration whereas ellipse versions are all shorter than the complete versions of the videos. A second difference with Study 1 is that the additional instructions that will be provided for the black screen condition will instruct subjects to ignore the black screens during the viewing of the videos, as well as during their mental replay. Study 1 and 2 will focus on 4 measures of interest: (1) temporal compression rates (the ratio of the duration of mental replay to the actual video duration), (2) the number of recalled actions, (3) the number of recalled actions per unit of the actual video duration and (4) the number of recalled of actions per unit of the remembering duration (see measured variables).

Randomization

Study 1: Each subject will be randomly assigned either to the complete condition or to the ellipse condition. In each condition, the five videos will be randomly presented. Study 2: Each subject will be randomly assigned either to the complete condition or to the black screen condition. In each condition, the five videos will be randomly presented.

Data collection procedures

Study 1: Human subjects aged between 18 and 30 with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorder will be recruited to complete this study. No payment will be provided for their participation. Study 2: Human subjects aged between 18 and 30 with no history of neurological or psychiatric disorder will be recruited to complete this study. No payment will be provided for their participation.

Sample size

Study 1: 80 subjects will be recruited in this study. Half of the subjects will be randomly assigned to the ellipse (40 subjects) or the complete (40 subjects) conditions. For multilevel analyses, we will use videos as level 1 units (i.e. 5 videos) and subjects as level 2 units (i.e. 80 subjects). Study 2: 80 subjects will be recruited in this study. Half of the subjects will be randomly assigned to the black screen (40 subjects) or the complete (40 subjects) conditions. For robust multilevel design, we will use videos as level 1 units (i.e. 5 videos) and subjects as level 2 units (i.e. 80 subjects).

Sample size rationale

For each study, we plan to include 40 subjects in each experimental condition (i.e., complete and edited videos), resulting in a total of 80 participants. Note that it was not possible to estimate the expected effect size for our between-condition comparison for temporal compression based on previous studies because this is the first study to assess the impact of edited movies on compression rates. Thus, the sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power 3 (Faul et al., 2007) in order to have a statistical power of 0.80 (with an alpha of .05, two-tailed) to detect between-group differences with an effect size d = 0.65, which corresponds to the effect size typically observed in previous studies comparing the temporal compression rates of different types of events (e.g., Jeunehomme &amp; D’Argembeau, 2019, 2020). Note also that this sample size provides sufficient statistical power (&gt; 0.80) for detecting differences in the recall of actions when comparing intact movies with movies in which segments that are considered informative had been removed, based on an effect size of d = 0.91 estimated from Schwan and Garsoffky (2004; comparison between comparison of “full-length version” and “non-breakpoint-keeping summaries”).

Variables

Manipulated variables

Study 1: Type of videos (complete VS ellipses) Study 2: Type of videos (complete VS black screens)

Measured variables

Study 1 and Study 2: (1) The time needed to remember the videos. The time to remember each video will be measured by pressing the SPACEBAR during the computer task when beginning and ending each mental replay. (2) The number of recalled actions will be measured based on the action descriptions that participants will write. For each video, the number of correctly recalled actions will be computed based on a list describing all the actions represented on the video.

Indices

Study 1 and Study 2: (1) Temporal compression rate: the ratio of the actual video duration to the time needed to remember the video. (2) The total number of recalled actions (3) The number of recalled actions per unit of the actual video duration. (4) The number of recalled of actions per unit of the remembering duration. (5) The number of ellipses (Study 1) and black screens (Study2) in the videos. This number is a priori predefined and is identical for the five videos presented in Study 1 and 2. Study 2 only: (6) Temporal compression rates when excluding black screen durations. Black screen durations will be a priori estimated for each video. (7) The number of recalled action per unit of the video duration when excluding black screen durations.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Study 1 and Study 2: Because the distribution of the measure of temporal compression was substantially skewed in our previous studies (Jeunehomme &amp; D’Argembeau, 2019, 2020), we will first examine whether the data are normally distributed or not. If temporal compression rates are normally distributed, we plan to examine differences in the number of recalled actions, temporal compression rates, the number of recalled actions per unit of the actual video duration and the number of recalled of actions per unit of the remembering duration, using independent samples t-tests. We will also examine whether recalled actions predict temporal compression rates using multilevel regression analyses (with videos as level 1 units and participants as level 2 units). However, if the data are not normally distributed, the robust versions of independent samples t-tests (using the 20% trimmed means) and of multilevel models will be used to analyze the data.

Transformations

Inference criteria

Data exclusion

Due to the online nature of the experiment, all experimental trials that will reveal a connection issue will be excluded from the analyses (e.g., incomplete or slow presentation of a video). In addition, experimental trials with a time of mental replay less than 5 seconds will be excluded from the analyses (i.e., to remove trials in which response times are too fast to correspond to the mental replay of the video). Finally, all subjects who will not fully complete the experimental task will be excluded from the analyses.
