Study Information

Hypotheses

We hypothesize that when a mating goal is made salient, people would perceive a potential mate as more attractive if the sought help is dependency-oriented (versus autonomy-oriented)

Study design

As a cover story, participants will be instructed to work on a study consisted of three unrelated parts alleged to examine online interpersonal interactions and consumption behaviors. First, participants will learn that they would be randomly paired up and cooperated with another participant to complete a task. To increase the credibility, they will be asked to fill out their demographic information (age and gender) and the shortened version of Big-Five Personality Inventory (Gosling et al., 2003) alleged to facilitate pairing. Then, participants will be told that the paring process should take a while, and therefore they are invited to evaluate an advertisement while they are waiting for the pairing results. In fact, the advertisement is used to manipulate participants’ mating motivation (Ko &amp; Suh, 2019; Sundie et al., 2011). Specifically, participants in the mating condition will be exposed to a clip from Tom Ford lipstick advertisement in which couples passionately exchanged kisses. In contrast, participants in the control condition will view a clip from a Nike advertisement showing scenes of people exercising. As a manipulation check, participants will be asked to indicate their levels of romantic and sexual arousal. The two items are: “I feel romantically aroused.” and “I feel sexually aroused.” (1= not at all, 9 = very much). As a manipulation check, the two items will be averaged to index their levels of mating motivation. To boost the credibility of the cover story and to prevent participants from knowing the true purpose, participants will also be asked to rate the products advertised on other dimensions, such as “To what extent you find this ad is aesthetically appealing?” “To what extent you find this ad is appropriate in its content?” and “To what extent you would like to purchase the product in this ad?” This part of data will not be analyzed. Participants will then be directed to the third part of the study ostensibly examining on-line teamwork. Adapted from Bareket et al. (2021), participants will be presented with a short profile of their assigned partner who appears to be a real person but is actually programmed. All participants will receive the same profile except that the gender of the partner will be deliberately made to be the opposite gender. The profile will contain information of the partner’s first name (i.e., for male participants: Mary; for female participants: James), age that is roughly matched with that of participants (i.e., 28 year-old for participants aged between 18-35; 42 for participants aged between 36-50; 60 for participants aged between 51-70), and personality (i.e., moderately extroverted and sometimes emotional). Participants will learn that this part of the study is to examine their expertise in dealing with everyday tasks. To complete the task, team members would be randomly assigned to either the “participant” role or the “assistant” role. The former needs to answer all the questions, while the latter will be provided with correct answers (i.e., hints and final answers) and offer help to the “participant” upon required. Importantly, all participants will be informed that they are assigned to be the “assistant” and their partner is assigned to be the “participant” by chance. Then, participants will be presented with both the hints and the final answers to all 8 questions. Later, participants will be notified that their partner asks for their help to solve 5 questions. In the dependency-oriented help condition, their partner asks for 5 final answers (i.e., James/ Mary wants you to answer the question for him/her). In contrast, those in the autonomy-oriented help condition, their partner asks for 5 hints (i.e., James/ Mary wants to get a hint from you so that she/he can answer the question himself/herself). Participants will then be instructed to provide help to their partner by sending either the final answers or the hints according to the condition they are in. After this, participants will be instructed to indicate their romantic feelings toward their partner (i.e., help-seeker) using three items: “I find my partner attractive,” “I find my partner desirable,” and “I would like to go on a date with my partner.” An overall romantic interests score will be calculated by averaging the scores, with higher scores corresponding to higher levels of romantic interests. As a control measure, participants will evaluate their partner on perceived competence using one item: “I feel that my partner is competent” (1 = Not at all; 7 = very much). In addition, we will measure participants’ sense of power during the pair work using two items: “I was in control during the task” and “I felt powerful during the task” (1 = Not at all; 7 = very much). An overall sense of power will be calculated by averaging the scores, with higher scores corresponding to higher levels of sense of power. Moreover, participants will be asked to rate their involvement in the task using the two items, including: “How much efforts have you put into these tasks?” and “How engaged were you in solving the problems?” (1 = not at all; 7 = very much). Finally, participants will report their demographic information and relationship history (the number of times they have been in a romantic relationship). In addition, participants will answer an open question to probe suspicions about the real purpose of the study. They will then be thanked and debriefed.

Randomization

In a 2 (condition: mating vs. control) by 2 (type of requested help: dependency-oriented vs. autonomy-oriented) between-subjects design, participants will be randomly assigned to one of the four conditions. The randomization would be automatically achieve via an on-line software.

Data collection procedures

A priori analysis using G*power calculator reveals that a sample of 220 participants is needed to detect the effect size obtained in Study 5A (f = 0.19; α = 0.05, 80% power; ANOVA, interaction). We will aim for 2 times of the sample size and will oversample in a single wave of data collection to account for those who can’t pass our attention check question. A total of 500 single heterosexual British participants will be recruited from Prolific to participate in this study. Sensitivity power analysis reveals that a minimum effect size of f = 0.13 could be detected (α = .05, 80% power). All participants in this study will be rewarded for a small monetary reward. Informed consent will be obtained from all participants prior to their participation. Those who successfully pass attention check questions would be included in the final analysis.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Help-Providers’ Romantic Interests. Data will be submitted to an ANOVA by treating condition (mating vs. control) and type of requested help (dependency-oriented vs. autonomy-oriented) as between-subjects factors. We expect that the interaction between condition and type of requested help to be significant. This effect is expected to remain significant after controlling for age, gender, relationship history, and perceived partner’s competence. In addition, when sense of power is entered as a covariate, we expect that the interaction effect between condition and type of requested help on help-providers’ romantic interests remains to be significant. In particular, we expect that when a mating goal is activated, participants would perceive the help-seeker as more attractive when dependency-oriented help, compared to autonomy-oriented help, is requested.
