The effect of negative valence on false memory formation in the DRM paradigm: A Paradigmatic Replication

OSF ID: wqfkh | Registered: 2022-01-14T15:15:25.701164 | Showing: original version (1 version(s) on OSF)

Study Information

Hypotheses

All hypotheses are stated for recall and recognition results separately as analyses will also be conducted for memory tests separately. Primary Focus We will examine whether there is a difference in false memory formation between neutral and negative valence. To this end, we will separately run two analyses to test the effect of valence on the two dependent variables: recall and recognition of non-studied critical words. 1. We hypothesize that negative valence will lead to more recalled non-studied critical words compared to neutral valence. (one-sided) 2. We hypothesize that negative valence will lead to more “old” responses to non-studied critical words compared to neutral valence. (one-sided) Secondary Focus We will examine whether the primary effect of valence on the dependent variable non-studied critical words may be the result of differential discriminability and/or response bias for valence conditions. These additional analyses will be conducted to preclude the interpretation that an identified primary effect is due to general tendencies towards negative valence (vs neutral). To this end, we will first run an additional analysis to test the effect of valence on the dependent variable ‘studied words’ in the recall test. A general response bias tendency in negative valence (vs neutral) would be evident from: 3. negative valence will show more recalled studied words compared to neutral valence. (one-sided) Second, we will calculate a response bias index for the recognition test. More liberal response bias to negative valence would be evident from: 4. negative valence will show a more liberal response bias index (i.e., lower response bias index score) compared to neutral valence. (one-sided) Third, we will calculate a recognition discriminability index to see whether discriminability differs per valence condition. Here, poorer discriminability in negative valence (vs neutral) would be evident from: 5. negative valence will show a lower recognition discriminability index compared to neutral valence. (one-sided) Arousal Addition: We will test whether the arousal level of lists has an effect on false memory formation within the negative valence condition as such that it increases false memories. This effect of arousal would be evident from: 6. high-arousal negative lists will show more recalled non-studied critical lures compared to low-arousal negative lists. (one-sided) 7. high-arousal negative lists will show more “old” responses to non-studied critical lures in the recognition test compared to low-arousal negative lists. (one-sided) Lastly, we will also analyze the primary hypotheses on the two groups neutral low-arousal and negative low-arousal (instead of neutral and negative valence generally, with the latter valence group including both low- and high-arousal) to see whether conclusions of the results change. This is done to preclude the explanation that evidence of a valence effect is instead only due to arousal differences. If conclusions of the results change, then arousal differences might (partly) underlie the primary effect.

Is there any additional blinding in this study?

This study forms a within-subject design. Thus, all participants will go through the same experimental conditions. Data will be collected online (i.e., no experimenter present). Participants will not be aware of the specific focus of the study, i.e., that we are testing primarily the effect of valence on false memory formation specifically.

Study design

The study represents a within-subject quasi-experimental design with list valence (levels: negative vs neutral), and negative list arousal (levels: low vs high) as the independent variables (within-subject), resulting in a total of three groups (low-arousal negative vs high-arousal negative vs low-arousal neutral). The three dependent variables are: reported studied words, non-studied critical words and non-studied unrelated words. This design will be tested and evaluated for the recall and recognition test separately.

Randomization

To avoid stimulus-specific effects (Wells &amp; Windschitl, 1999), there will be two sets of stimuli counterbalanced across participants, so participants either get set A or set B (by the program Inquisit). Data collection procedures

As prior research produced robust DRM effects online (even for non-naïve participants; Zwaan et al., 2018), data will be collected through the online portal Prolific.co. Participants will complete one session (approximately 30 minutes) and will be compensated monetarily (approximately 4.50 euros). The inclusion criteria for study participation are: first language being English, age between 18 and 45 (i.e., to minimize developmental or aging differences; Abichou et al., 2020; Otgaar et al., 2013), use of windows computer (i.e., as the graphics work best and most consistently with this operating system) with working audio and participants who are willing to download the Inquisit player software. Adherence will be assured by using self-report screening measures. There are three exclusion criteria. First, we will exclude all participants who have not completed the session’s necessary parts: learning phase, recall and recognition test. Second, we will exclude participants who fail the attention check administered during the learning phase. Third, participants will be excluded if they gave “old” responses to more than 95% or less than 5% of the trials in the recognition test as this likely indicates decreased attention by just “clicking through” (i.e., actual percentage of old responses in test = 37.5%).

Sample size

We will initially test the evidential value of the primary focus (i.e., effect of valence on the dependent variable ‘non-studied critical words’ in the recall and the recognition test separately) on N=100 participants (i.e., before exclusions). We will stop data collection if substantial evidence speaks for or against an effect of valence on the non-studied critical words in both the recall and recognition test (both BFs10&gt;5 or BF BFs10&lt;.2). If non-substantial, we will add batches of N=100 until we reach substantial evidence or N=300 of participants (i.e., before exclusions). The upper limit of N=300 is set for feasibility purposes.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Variable 1: Valence Valence will be manipulated within-subjects with the valence of lists: neutral vs negative. Variable 2: Arousal Arousal of the negative list will be manipulated within-subjects with the arousal of the lists: negative low arousal vs negative high arousal. We have three within-groups in total - low arousal negative lists vs low arousal neutral lists vs high arousal negative lists. All word lists were selected from existing lists (using 12 words out of each list pool), taking into account the need for non-overlapping words and to satisfy desired valence, arousal and backward associative strength ratings. The neutral lists (i.e., non-studied critical lures: mountain, stove, car, cold, lion, command, king, bitter) all stem from Roediger et al. (2001). The negative list with the non-studied critical lure ‘cry’ was extracted from Shah &amp; Knott (2018) and all other negative lists (i.e., non-studied critical lures: sick, trash, dead, thief, spider, anger, hurt) were extracted from Chang et al. (2021). Words will always be presented in decreasing order of backward associative strength. We used the data of Warriner and colleagues (2013) to look up valence and arousal measures for each word. The measures were acquired in Warriner et al. (2013) based on online self-report measures on 9-point Likert scale for both valence (after reverse-coding: ranging from 1 unhappy to 9 happy) and arousal (after reverse-coding: ranging from 1 calm to 9 excited). In their study, a total of 723 participants for valence and 745 participants for arousal rated words for their emotional properties. This work represents an extension of the Affective Norms for English Words (ANEW) by Bradley and Lang (1999), from 1034 to nearly 14,000 English lemmas. To make sure that our stimuli were appropriately selected, we conducted additional analyses on lists’ mean backward associative strength, valence and arousal ratings. The mean arousal and valence per list was computed as the mean properties of all 12 words of the respective list. If measures for specific words in the list were not available, the mean was calculated based on words for which the respective measure was known. There were no outliers (defined as more than 1.5 times the interquartile range above the third quartile or below the first quartile) in mean backward associative strength of lists. Across the 16 lists, there was convincing evidence that mean valence ratings differed between neutral and negative lists (t(13.14) = 12.21, p &lt; .001, BF10 = 819438.50) as such that negative lists were rated as more negative than neutral lists. Further, across the 16 lists, there was convincing evidence that mean arousal ratings differed between low-arousal and high-arousal lists (t(4.42) = -5.11, p = .003, BF10 = 273.49) as such that low-arousal lists were rated as less arousing than high-arousal lists. This distinction was even apparent (but to a lesser degree) within negative valence groups only. Across the 8 lists, mean arousal ratings differed between low-arousal negative and high-arousal negative lists (t(5.96) = -3.56, p = .006, BF10 = 4.63) as such that low-arousal negative lists were rated as less arousing than high-arousal negative lists. We note that for the latter comparison two conditions with each only 4 data points were compared, resulting in reduced power which may also explain the slightly lower Bayes factor.

bias index for the recognition outcome (for both: one computation including critical false alarms, and one computation including unrelated false alarms). These calculations will be conducted using standardized z-scores. We note that, as Z-scores cannot be calculated in the case of zeros, we may correct our data following the procedure recommended by Snodgrass and Corwin (1988). For the recognition discriminability, we will calculate two difference scores. We will calculate a difference score between the standardized z-score for correct “old” responses to studied words (i.e., hit rate) and the standardized z-score for the incorrect “old” responses to non-studied critical words (i.e., false alarm rate), per valence condition. We will also calculate a difference score between the standardized z-score for correct “old” responses to studied words (i.e., hit rate) and the standardized z-score for the incorrect “old” responses to non-studied unrelated words (i.e., false alarm rate), per valence condition. Thus, we will calculate both versions of the negative and neutral recognition discriminability index as follows: d’Critical, neutral = z(hit rate neutral) - z(critical false alarm rate neutral) d’Critical, negative = z(hit rate negative) - z(critical false alarm rate negative) d’ values close to zero indicate that the participant is unable to discriminate between studied words and non-studied critical words. Higher values indicate that the participant tends to (correctly) accept studied words and (correctly) reject non-studied critical words. d’Unrelated, neutral = z(hit rate neutral) - z(unrelated false alarm rate neutral) d’Unrelated, negative = z(hit rate negative) - z(unrelated false alarm rate negative) d’ values close to zero indicate that the participant is unable to discriminate between studied words and non-studied unrelated words. Higher values indicate that the participant tends to (correctly) accept studied words and (correctly) reject non-studied unrelated words. Lastly, the response bias index per valence condition will be calculated as follows for the computation including critical false alarms, and the computation including unrelated false alarms. cCritical, neutral =-[ z(hit rate neutral) + z(critical false alarm rate neutral)]/2 cCritical, negative =-[ z(hit rate negative) + z(critical false alarm rate negative)]/2 cUnrelated, neutral =-[ z(hit rate neutral) + z(unrelated false alarm rate neutral)]/2 cUnrelated, negative =-[ z(hit rate negative) + z(unrelated false alarm rate negative)]/2 Here, the neutral point (c = 0) indicates that no response is favored. A criterion on either side of the neutral point indicates a bias: a negative value corresponds to a liberal bias (responding “old” frequently) and a positive value corresponds to a conservative bias (responding “old” infrequently) (Stanislaw &amp; Todorov, 1999).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Exclusions We will exclude participants who did not complete necessary parts of the experiment (i.e., learning phase, recall and recognition test), gave “old” responses to more than 95% or less than 5% of the trials in the recognition test, or failed to accurately react to the attention check in the learning phase. Manipulation Check As a positive control, we will use the recognition test of the neutral lists to test for the original DRM effect, which has already been shown to be robust also in online studies (Zwaan et al., 2018). To test this, we will use a Bayesian Student one-tailed paired samples t-test to compare the null hypothesis (i.e., no difference in “old” responses to neutral non-studied critical words and neutral non-studied unrelated words) to a one-sided alternative (i.e., more “old” responses to neutral non-studied critical words in comparison to neutral non-studied unrelated words). Before testing the positive control effect, we will investigate and remove extreme outliers (i.e., difference score that is larger or smaller than the third and first quartiles, respectively, by three times of the interquartile range). Due to using a large sample size in the study, we will refrain from testing the normality assumption. If we do not find a positive control effect (i.e., DRM recognition effect in the neutral list; BF10 not larger than 5), confirmatory analyses will be discussed more tentatively. In case of a ceiling (correct response &gt; 95%) or flooring effect (correct response &lt; 5%) in the recognition test, interpretation of the confirmatory recognition results will be discussed more cautiously. Further, in case of a ceiling (correct studied words recall &gt; 95%, more than 91 words out of 96) or flooring effect (correct studied words recall &lt; 5%, less than 5 words out of 96) in the recall test, interpretation of the confirmatory recall results will be discussed more cautiously. Confirmatory Analysis Confirmatory analyses will be conducted on the final total sample after listwise exclusions. As above mentioned, prior to testing analyses on both outcomes, we will investigate and remove extreme outliers per analysis. We will report Cohen’s d effect sizes with their respective 95% confidence intervals. The first outcome of primary interest will be the non-studied critical words in the recall test. For this, we will conduct a Bayesian one-tailed student paired samples t-test to compare the null hypothesis (i.e., no difference in recalled non-studied critical words between neutral and negative valence) to a one-sided alternative (i.e., more recalled non-studied critical words in negative compared to the neutral condition). For the second outcome of primary interest, selected non-studied critical words in the recognition test, we will equally conduct a Bayesian one-tailed student paired samples t-test to compare the null hypothesis (i.e., no difference in “old” responses to non-studied critical words between neutral and negative valence) to a one-sided alternative (i.e., more “old” responses to non-studied critical words in negative compared to the neutral condition). For the secondary focus, four additional analyses will be conducted. In all analyses, we will conduct a Bayesian one-tailed student paired samples t-test to compare the null hypothesis (i.e., no difference between contrasts in secondary measure analyses) to a one-sided alternative. Inference criteria

For the Bayesian analyses, a standard JZS prior with scaling factor r = .707 will be used. Additionally, JASP will be used to conduct a Bayes factor robustness check for the primary analyses (i.e., to show the respective computed Bayes factor for a wide range of prior distributions, allowing to examine the extent to which conclusions depend on the prior specification). We will calculate Bayes Factors (BF01), which indicates how likely the data are under the null hypothesis, compared to if the alternative hypothesis is true (Jarosz &amp; Wiley, 2014). The inverse ratio allows one to speak to the likelihood of the alternative hypothesis compared to the null (Jarosz &amp; Wiley, 2014). For all analyses, we will classify BF10 &gt;5 as convincing evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis over the null hypothesis and BF10 &lt;0.2 as convincing evidence in favor of the null hypothesis over the alternative hypothesis. We want to note that if a Bayesian analysis shows a BF10 &lt;5 and BF10 &gt;0.2, we will not draw definite conclusions on the directionality of the respective effect.

Data exclusion

We will exclude participants who did not complete necessary parts of the experiment (learning phase, recall and recognition test), gave “old” responses to more than 95% or less than 5% of the trials in the recognition test, or failed to accurately react to the attention check in the learning phase.

Missing data

We will not have missing data for the confirmatory analyses when only including participants that completed necessary parts of the experiment.

Exploratory analysis

As an exploratory addition, we will conduct sensitivity analyses in which we will reanalyze all confirmatory analyses with extreme outliers included to see whether conclusions of the results change.
