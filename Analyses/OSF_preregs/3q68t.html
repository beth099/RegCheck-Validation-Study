Emotions as perception: A ratio-scale for feeling intensity.

OSF ID: 3q68t | Registered: 2020-12-15T13:31:25.763035 | Showing: original version (1 version(s) on OSF)

Study Information

Hypotheses

In this work, we rely on the analogy between emotions and perceptual decisions, so we hypothesize that emotional awareness behaves like conscious (“perceptual”) sensation and can be put on a ratio- scale, and also that it is obeys Weber's Law. An alternative hypothesis is that emotion resembles “action” related perception, and as such does not obey Weber’s Law.

Study design

In this study, we will run one experiment, which consists of two different tasks. The first task is the circle task. In this task, subjects will be exposed to differently sized circles, and their task will be to determine whether the circle presented to them in each step is larger or smaller than the standard circle. In the circles task, a fixation point will appear first for 500 milliseconds, followed by the standard circle that will appear for 500 milliseconds. After that, another fixation point will appear for 1500 ms. Finally, the circle to which the subject will respond will appear around the 2nd fixation. This circle will be presented until the response. After presenting the circle to which the subject will respond, a blank screen will appear for 500 milliseconds. The second task is the emotional task. In this task, subjects will be exposed to emotional stimuli with established norms (NAPS, Marchewka, urawski, Jednoróg, &amp;Grabowska, 2014). For every emotional stimulus, the subjects will need to determine whether this emotional picture is making them feel pleasant or unpleasant. Before the presentation of an emotional stimulus, a frame (used as fixation points) whose size is lightly larger than the photo will appear for 1500 milliseconds. The emotional picture will then be presented inside the frame. The image will appear on the screen for 4.5 seconds, regardless of the subject response. After the image, a blank screen will appear for 500 milliseconds. While viewing the: 2nd fixation point, the circle to which the subject will respond, the frame that used as fixation points, and also while viewing the emotional picture, the relevant keys will appear on the sides of the screen. All subject will response "big" and "pleasant " with their right hand, and "small" and "unpleasant" with their left hand. The purpose of those is to make the mapping intuitive, and to avoid response selection difficulty. Experiment 1 will consist of the circle and the emotional task with trials of the two tasks interleaved. Participants will be told that we are studying the influence of emotion on their circle perception; that we manipulate their emotion using pictures; and that we need to know how they personally feel in response to that picture. This experiment is a within subject's design, with three factors: circle sizes (8 levels), emotion eliciting photos intensity (6 levels), and valence (2 levels). The NAPS pictures were selected according to their valence scores, so that six groups were created. For the first group the valence values will be around the value 2, for the second group the valence values will be around the value 3, and so on. The group of neutral stimuli (valence around 5) will not be used in the present study. In this experiment, each emotional trial will be followed by a circle trial. After each experiment containing the emotional task, we will send the subjects an Inspiring video, and will ask them to answer some quotation related to this video. The purpose of this video is to dilute the effect of the difficult-to-view images, so that the subjects will leave the experiment in a positive as possible feeling. For the same purpose, the experiment will contain more positive than negative emotional stimuli, which might make the evaluation of the model parameters less stable for the negative stimuli. To summarize, in the experiment subjects will perform both the circle task and the emotional task, with trials of these two tasks interleaved. The experiment will begin with detailed instructions. In addition, the experimenter will provide a verbal explanation to ensure that the instructions are clear. The experiment will contain four practice blocks: a circle task practice block, emotional task practice block, keys only practice block, and an interleaved practice block. Also, at the end of every experiment, the experimenter will ask the subject three questions: 1. Please state in your words: what was your task in the experiment? 2. What strategy did you use in order to succeed in this task? 3. In your opinion, what was the goal of this experiment? In case that the first experiment will not produce the expected results (the SV parameter from the LBA model will not increase with circle size and emotional stimuli size), we will try another version of the experiment, this time instead of presenting the stimuli until the response is given\ for 4.5 seconds, the circle will disappear after 200 milliseconds. The rationale is that responding to a present stimulus might invoke the action system which is known not to behave according to Weber’s Law, whereas responding to a remembered stimulus activates the perception system, which behaves according to Weber’s Law (Ganel et al. 2008). If the new version will not produce the expected results as well, we will have to consider additional alternatives before running the second experiment.

Explanation of existing data

So far, we have collected data from two experiments. In the first experiment, we asked participants if the circle they are seeing is smaller or larger than a standard (fixed) circle. We encountered problems related to this study design, and decided to conduct another experiment instead. In the second experiment, subjects were exposed to different sized ellipse's, and their task was to determine whether the ellipse presented to them at each step is "thinner" or "fatter", compared to a perfect circle. The circles and ellipse's tasks were used to test whether Weber’s Law can be identified in a perceptual task using our method. In the circle experiment, the circle to which the subjects responded was displayed in a randomly chosen position on the screen (screen center or 150 pixels above/ below the screen center). We found that when the circle did not appear in the center of the screen, there was no evidence for Weber’s law, but evidence for Weber’s law did appear when the circle was displayed at the center of the screen. Subjects reported great difficulty in estimating the size of the circle when it did not appear at the center of the screen. We hypothesize that the position manipulation made the experimental results indecisive. It is possible that the fixation point (which appeared in the center of the screen regardless of the stimulus location) confused the subjects and made it difficult to estimate the size of the circles. On top of that, in retrospect, the problem with this task was that it was insufficiently analogous to the emotion task. For this reason, we decided to conduct the ellipses experiment. So far, we did not find any evidence suggesting Weber’s Law can be identified using the ellipse's experiment. Taking all the above into account, we decided to use the circle experiment in the current experiment. More information about the ellipse's experiment that was shelved can be found in the "Other" section, information about the experiment to replace it is detailed in this pre-registration.

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited through online experiment system of Ben- Gurion University. Participant must have intact / corrected vision. In addition, given the highly aversive nature of some stimuli, and as justifiably dictated by our Ethics committee, we will inform the participants that this study is not suitable for people who suffer from depression, phobia, or anxiety. Participants who will sign to the experiment will receive an invitation to fill a depression questionnaire. Only participants who will be within the normal range in the depression questionnaire outcomes, will participate in the experiment. Subjects whose depression scores are in the clinical range will: 1. Be compensated for their participation; 2. will be given an explanation regarding the meaning of this score; and 3. Will be offered to meet with a clinical psychologist for further explanation regarding possible directions to take. Participants will be ungraduated students of ben- Gurion University, who will be paid (~35 NIS per hour), or that will receive course credit for their participation.

We understand that this analysis is not well suited for the models that we are applying, but at present this gives a reasonable first guess. Our experience shows that similar Ns produce highly replicable (across experiments) results. Variables

Manipulated variables

First, we manipulate circle sizes. The radius of the standard circle is 225. There are four circles that are larger than the standard circle, and four that are smaller than it. Each circle area is 5% larger/ smaller than its previous circle in the series. In order to calculate the circle areas, we used radius squared. For example: if the radius squared of the standard circle is 22,500. If 5% are added, the radius squared will be 23,625, and the radius will be 153.704. The next circle will be 5% larger than the previous radius square (23,625), and will be 24,806.25, and its radius will be 157.5, and so on. Because we will run those studies online, we will create a standard circle radius for each group of screen sizes, so that each participant will see the same sized stimulus, regardless of her/his screen size. The 225 radius of the standard circle is suitable for 13.9"-14.1" sized screen. Second, we manipulate the emotional intensity of pictures. We will use emotion eliciting photos that have established norms. Each photo had been chosen based on its valence, intensity and category (animals, faces, etc.), So that every category will have a satisfactory representation in the study, that every value of intensity will have a satisfactory representation, and that each valence will have a satisfactory representation as well.

Measured variables

In the circle's manipulation, we will ask participants if the circle they are seeing is smaller or larger than the standard circle. We will record their answer to this question. In the emotional awareness manipulation, we will ask participants if the picture they are seeing is making them feel pleasant or unpleasant. In every manipulation we will record RT and response choice for each answer.

Indices

We will use an evidence accumulation model, the liner ballistic accumulation model (LBA) with a focus on DR (drift-rate) and the across-trial SD of drift-rate (SV).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

In this study we will use the method of fitting a hierarchical Bayesian evidence-accumulation model. This hierarchical method estimates parameters for each individual separately, but constrain these estimations with the population distribution (see Lin &amp; Strickland, in press, Appendix, for details). Follow-up analyses In this study we will use a method of model comparison to test some of our hypothesis. The fundamental idea in this method is to fit several different LBA models, and to decide which model fits the data best. To do so, we will create the null model (M1) with all its parameters set to be equal across different levels of the dependent variable in question. Then, we will create five more models (M2-M6), which in every one of those models a different parameter will set as a function of the dependent variable. The null model will serve as a comparison model to the other models. Because hierarchical modeling is a time-consuming process, we will use hierarchical modeling only for our main hypotheses. In addition, in order to interpret the data obtained from the hierarchical analysis, the Gelman-Robin index for each individual and for all subjects together must be less than 1.1. If we won't meet this requirement, or if the hypotheses under examination is not a main hypothesis, we will use simpler methods for estimating the model parameters. The simpler method treats the data as if they came from "one big subject". This approach is based on maximum likelihood estimation, and issues BIC values for comparative model fit assessment. This method will conduct using 'rtdists' or 'glba' R packages. Any model that will have a significant smaller DIC or BIC then M1, will account as a model that fit the data the best. This method will be used by us in the analyses of few of our hypothesis. To test our hypothesis that emotional awareness obeys Weber's law, we first need to show that the LBA model is suitable to find evidence to Weber’s law even for physical stimuli. We thus intend to show that the LBA model can detect Weber's law for the perception of circle size. We term the related hypothesis “tool-validity hypothesis” (TVH) to mark the fact that it is not a core hypothesis. To do so, we will use the model comparison method, and fit several different LBA models to circle responses. Model 1 (TVH-M1) is the null model with all its parameters set to be equal across different levels of the circle size. In Model 2 (TVH-M2), DR is a function of circle size. In model 3 (TVH-M3), SV will set as a function of the circle size. We expect that in TVH-M7, SV will increase linearly with stimulus intensity, while DR will increase as the circle size move farther away from the target circle. If TVH-M7 is shown to be superior over TVH-M2 and TVH-M3, this will support our prediction. In every one of the four remaining models (TVH-M4- TVH-M6), a different parameter (SP, non-decision, b) will set as a function of the different levels of the circle size. We will choose the best model to fit the data, based on the DIC or BIC differences between the models (significantly smallest DIC/BIC). In a case that less than 75% of the subjects will fit more to one model compared to the other model (can be deduced by the DIC differences for each subject in both models), we will perform another analysis. For this analysis we will randomly split the data into two separate data sets, so that each subject's trials will be equally distributed between the two sets of data. We than re-run the model with both new data sets, and examined whether the percentage of subjects to fit one model compared to the other model remain almost the same. If the percentage will remain quite like the percentages of the first analysis, we can conclude that the differences between both models are not coincidental. If our hypothesis is not supported by the data, we have two options: First, the LBA model is not suitable to express Weber's law. The second option is that something in the experimental procedure went wrong, then we can fix it and try again. Importantly, until (or whether) the tool-validity hypothesis is supported is a pre-requisite for testing the core hypothesis which is described next. In order to test our core hypothesis (that emotional awareness obey Weber's law), we will compare between several alternative models which are strictly analogous to the models we have described above, except for being applied to (un) pleasantness reports. The circle task is used for two purposes: (1) to create a validated reference-point to the emotional and gamble tasks. (2) To function as a disguise (subjects will be led to believe that we are examining whether emotional stimulation/ gamble outcomes affect perception of circles area). In order to create a validated reference-point, we will use the method of model comparison as described above, and apply it on the difference between tasks (circle, emotional). We expect to find that the model that will show to be superior to the other models, is the model that allows the SP parameter to vary between tasks. This hypothesis is in accordance with previous work conducted in our lab (Givon et al., 2019). We would also like to check for selective influence as means to validate the model and to replicate prior similar validation by Givon et al. (2019). To do so, we will once again use the method of model comparison, and compare between different levels of some of the depended variables (circle size, intensity, valance). We believe that the model that will have the smallest BIC for each of those variables, will be the model that allows the DR and the SV parameters to vary between different levels of the depended variables. If this prediction will be supported, this will serve as evidence for selective influence. As sanity check, we would also aspect to receive higher drift-rate for negative feeling, then for positive felling (as was found in previous work conducted in our lab). In this experiment, we will use the DR parameter, per emotional intensity photo, in order to put the emotional stimuli trials on a ratio scale. This will be possible if indeed the DR parameter will increase as the circle size move farther away from the target circle. The evidence-accumulation model that we will use is the liner ballistic accumulation model (LBA). In its more standard applications, this model contains of five parameters: drift rate (v), the drift rate standard deviation (SV), non-decision time, starting point (SP) and response threshold (b) (St0- the range of non-decision time, is usually fixed at zero, since adding it to the model substantially increases fitting time, and since usually there is no substantial question that relates to it).

Transformations

If the estimated parameters will be reasonable (i.e. non-decision time will be greater than 0, and more reasonably greater than .15, and between .5 to 1), no transformation will be needed. If not, then a transformation might be needed. If we use a transformation, this transformation will conduct in order to control individual differences in minimal RT and RT variance (see Pereg &amp; Meiran, 2019, Appendix, for details). This between-subject normalization procedure will only be applied in case that we do not use the hierarchical modeling.

Inference criteria

We intend to employ hierarchical Bayesian estimation (as instantiated in the ‘ggdmc’ R-package). This estimation method yields DIC estimated per subject. Thus, for each subject, we will determine which model fits the data the best (lowest DIC). We will use Bayesian Binomial test to compare subjects DIC score that obtained from different models, in order to show that one model in superior to the other not just by chance. For the simpler methods for estimating the model parameters, we will use Bayes Factor (BF) to determine which of the models fit the data the best. In order to calculate BF, we will use this function BF = e0.5*ΔBIC. We will treat the results as significant when BF will be greater than 5.

Data exclusion

First, we will exclude emotional stimuli whose responses do not fit their original norms. For example, if an emotional picture item that has a score 5 (neutral score) in the norms, but in this experiment most subjects have rated this picture as negative, then this item will be excluded. In addition, for every stimulus that according to the norms is on one side of the scale (negative or positive), we need that at least 60% of the subjects will classify it as such. For example, if a specific stimulus norm is negative, then at least 60% of the subject's will still have to classify it as negative in order for that stimulus to remain in the analysis. Second, we will exclude subjects for whom a technical or equipment failure resulted in loss of more than 50% of their data (we do not anticipate any such subjects). We will also exclude subjects who seem not to understand the instruction (as identified by the questions at the end of the experiment. This decision will be taken before peeking at the RT-accuracy results of that subject). We will also exclude subjects whose accuracy rates are 2.5 SD less than the mean accuracy of the entire sample. Third, we will exclude trials with RT greater than 7 sec, or shorter than 200 milliseconds. Following, we will exclude trials with RT greater than 3.5 SD above mean RT (as determined per condition within subject).

Missing data

In this study it is not possible to have missing data, only data that have been excluded.

Exploratory analysis

We may run post hoc tests that will come to mind after additional thoughts. These will be presented as such in publications, and will be followed by additional experiments to verify the robustness of the findings. We consider modeling using Drift Diffusion Model (DDM) instead of LBA, given that the units can be determined by the within-trial variance, which is hopefully completely unrelated to the JND (proportional to the between trial variance). The prediction for the DDM are strictly analogous to those made with respect to LBA.
