Study Information

Title

The Interplay of Interval Models and Entrainment Models in Duration Perception

Authors

Tzu-Han Zoe Cheng

Description

Entrainment has been proposed to be essential to listeners’ estimates of short time durations and prediction of onset timings. One of the most influential proposals is McAuley and Jones’ framework (2003). They demonstrated that listeners’ duration estimates were shifted from reality in opposite directions when to-be-judged durations occurred earlier than an entrained beat vs. later than an entrained beat, which is predicted by their entrainment models. However, neither their model nor the previous studies specify how long the entrainment persists after the cessation of external stimulation. We plan to explore entrainment decay in a duration estimation task which has 300 ms context tones with short (600 ms; 2 beats), medium (1200 ms; 4 beats) and long (2400 ms; 8 beats) delay versions. We have two goals in the current study. First, we want to test the persistence of the entrainment effect by observing the behavioral patterns across three designed delay lengths. Second, we plan to test different hypotheses of entrainment decrease by manipulating three delay lengths and compare it to our previous experiment, which had two absolute delay matches (1200 and 2400 ms) and two relative delay matches (2 beats and 4 beats).

Hypotheses

If the entrainment effect persists for more than a few beats, as suggested by McAuley and Jones’ framework, we should observe the same response pattern across different delay lengths. If the entrainment effect decreases across time, we will not observe the pattern in the longer-delay length condition. Moreover, comparing the results of the current experiment to our previous experiment allows us to test whether entrainment decreases after a certain amount of time (absolute delay) vs. a certain number of beats (relative delay). Our previous experiment had 600 ms inter-tone intervals and delays of 1.2 s (2 beats) and 2.4 s (4 beats). If the absolute delay account is true, we should observe entrainment decreases between 1.2 s and 2.4 s, as found in our previous experiment. If the relative delay account is true, we should observe entrainment decreases between 0.6 (2 beats) and 1.2 s (4 beats).

Study design

A within-subject 3 (Delay Length) × 3 (Comparison Onset) × 6 (Comparison Duration) repeated measures design will be used in this study. Short-delay, medium-delay and long-delay trials will be blocked and fully counterbalanced. To carefully control the response bias of responding by their dominant hand, we also plan to counterbalance the response keys across participants (half of participants responded F key as shorter, J key as longer; the other half, J key as shorter, F key as longer).

Randomization

We will randomize the presenting order of trials for each participant within each block.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited through the SONA website. Participants will receive 2 credits for agreeing to participate. Participants must be at least 18 years old.

Sample size

Our target sample size is 48 participants. We will attempt to recruit more until the sample size meets our target sample size.

Sample size rationale

The effects we planned to detect are the effect of comparison onset time (early, on-time, late relative to an expected beat) and the interaction of onset time and delay length. The effect of comparison onset time is crucial for verifying that entrainment effects are present at short delays. The interaction term is crucial for determining if there is a decrease in entrainment at the longer delay. We estimated a moderate effect size of h2p = .1. With this effect size, G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) indicates a sample size of 46 or higher is needed to achieve power of .80. We rounded this up slightly to 48 to reach a multiple of 4, given two block orders and two response key assignments. This criterion was used in our original study, so we maintain this criterion here.

Stopping rule

We plan to collect 48 participants. In the original experiment, we found that some participants showed aberrant points of subjective equality, which appeared to result from very low accuracy even on the easiest (shortest and longest) stimuli. In that study, we eliminated participants who scored below 60% accuracy on those “easy” trials in either or both blocks. We maintain this criterion in the current study, now as an a priori exclusion criterion. These low-accuracy participants will be replaced. Past this number, any additional participant subject-pool sign-ups will be tested for their own educational benefit, but their data will not be analyzed.

Variables

Manipulated variables

We have three independent variables. First, Delay Length with three levels: short, medium and long delay length. Second, Comparison Onset with three levels: early, ontime and late onset. Third, Comparison Duration with six levels: 275 ms, 285 ms, 295 ms, 305 ms, 315 ms, 325 ms.

Measured variables

We plan to measure participants’ trial-by-trial responses (i.e. shorter or longer), reaction time and their music backgrounds. Trial-by-trial responses are converted into three dependent variables: proportion short; proportion correct; point of subjective equality.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will use two-way within-subject 3 (Delay Length) × 3 (Comparison Onset) repeated measures ANOVAs to analyze the results of each of the three dependent variables: proportion short; proportion correct; point of subjective equality. As in McAuley and Jones (2003) and in our earlier experiment, we collapse over the factor Comparison Duration. Bonferroni-corrected t-tests will be used to discern the nature of any significant effects. For each DV, we expect a Delay Length x Comparison Onset interaction. 1. At shortest delay length, effect of Comparison Onset of all DVs. Proportion correct: significantly higher in on-time trials than early and late trials. Proportion short: significantly higher in early trials than on-time trials; significantly higher in on-time trials than late trials. Point of subjective equality: significantly longer in early trials than on-time trials; significantly longer in on-time trials than late trials. 2. At middle delay length, unknown if effect of Comparison Onset of all DVs. 3. At longest delay length, no effect of Comparison Onset of all DVs.

Transformations

We follow Macmillan and Creelman (1991, pp. 219–220) to calculate the points of subjective equality (PSE) from proportion short for each participant in each condition. The PSE is the time interval subjectively considered as equally long as the standard interval, as operationalized by being judged as shorter 50% of the time. We will first transform the proportions of responding “short” for each of the six comparison intervals to z-coordinates. Since z (1.00) is positive infinity and z (0.00) is negative infinity, we converted the 100 percent probability and 0 percent probability to 1-(1/2N) and 1/2N with N as the number of trials in each condition. Then we will fit a first order (linear) polynomial function on the six z-transformed points. The intercepts of the polynomial function represent the estimated PSEs for each condition.

Inference criteria

We will use p-values. For ANOVA effects and interactions, the criterion is p&lt;.05. For simple effects tests, p-values are Bonferroni-corrected.
