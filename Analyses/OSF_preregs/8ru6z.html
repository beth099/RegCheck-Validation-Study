Do congruent trials, incongruent trials, or both drive the congruency sequence effect? (Experiment 2)

OSF ID: 8ru6z | Registered: 2022-01-25T17:16:50.970816

Study Information

Hypotheses

We will first test whether – relative to previous neutral trials – previous congruent trials, previous incongruent trials, or both drive the CSE. If the congruency effect is greater after congruent compared to neutral trials, we will conclude that previous congruent trials drive the CSE. If the congruency effect is smaller after incongruent trials compared to neutral trials, we will conclude that previous incongruent trials drive the CSE. Based on the results of a pilot study (described in the sample size rationale section below), we expect that congruent trials alone will drive the CSE. In other words, we predict that the congruency effect will be larger after congruent trials relative to after neutral trials and that the congruency effect will be equivalent following neutral and incongruent trials. We also anticipate that -- after incongruent trials -- participants will only inhibit the current trial distractor. In other words, we predict that -- after incongruent trials -- congruent trial RTs will be slower than neutral trial RTs, and that neutral trial RTs will be equivalent to incongruent trial RTs.

Study design

The experiment will have a within-subjects design with two factors: previous trial congruency (congruent, incongruent, neutral) and current trial congruency (congruent, incongruent, neutral). There will be a single practice block of 36 trials, which will be divided into two sub-blocks of 18 trials. The task instructions will appear before each sub-block. Following the practice trials, there will be nine 72-trial test blocks (648 test trials in total). The task instructions will appear before each test block. In each block, incongruent, congruent, and neutral trials will occur equally often (12 times each in the 36-trial practice block; 24 times each in every test block). The trials will appear in a pseudo-randomized, first-order counterbalanced order wherein each trial type is preceded equally often by every trial type in the experimental design (congruent, neutral, and incongruent). We will avoid contingency learning confounds by presenting each prime-probe pair equally often. Further, we will avoid feature integration confounds by alternating between different stimulus sets (i.e., horizontal and vertical arrow and rectangle stimuli) in odd and even trials as in Schmidt &amp; Weissman 2014. We now describe the events in each block. First, a fixation cross will appear for 1.8 seconds. Second, a blank screen will replace the fixation cross for 0.2 seconds. Third, the trials will appear as described below. In each 2.7-second trial, participants will see a prime arrow or rectangle followed by a probe arrow. We will use white horizontal and vertical unfilled block stimuli. We will rotate a single block arrow and block rectangle stimulus to generate the stimuli for our horizontal and vertical stimulus sets. The prime and probe stimuli will subtend approximately the same degrees of visual angle. Further, the rectangles will be approximately the same size as the arrows. The approximate width and height of each stimulus in degrees of visual angle will be: left/right arrow 8.0° x 4.5°, up/down arrow 4.5° x 8.0°, horizontal rectangle 8.0° x 2.4°, vertical rectangle 2.4° x 8.0°. There are four possible congruent trials i.e. trials in which prime and probe arrows point in the same direction (left-left, right-right, up-up, down-down), four possible incongruent trials i.e. trials in which prime and probe arrows point in different directions (left-right, right-left, down-up, up-down), and four possible neutral trials i.e. trials with a rectangle prime (horizontal rectangle and left, horizontal rectangle and right, vertical rectangle and up, vertical rectangle and down). Odd trials will use horizontal arrow and rectangle stimuli. Even trials will use vertical arrow and rectangle stimuli. The timing of events within each trial will be as follows: (1) prime (133 ms), (2) blank screen (800 ms), (3) probe (133 ms), (4) blank screen (1434 ms), and (5) feedback (200 ms). Participants will be instructed to identify the direction in which the probe arrow points as quickly and accurately as possible. They will have 900 ms to respond. On a QWERTY keyboard, participants will press “f”(left middle finger), “g” (left index finger), “j” (right middle finger), or “n” (right index finger) to indicate that the probe points “left”, “right”, “up”, or “down”, respectively. If participants respond before the probe arrow appears, press the wrong key after the probe arrow appears, or take longer than 900 ms to respond, an error message will appear in white on the screen for 200ms (i.e., 1567 ms - 1767 ms after probe onset). Specifically, when participants press the wrong key or fail to respond, they will see the word “Error.” If participants respond after 900 ms, they will see the phrase “Too Slow.”

Randomization

The trials in each block will be presented in a pseudo-randomized, counterbalanced order. A new trial order will be generated before the 36-trial practice block and each of the nine 72-trial test blocks. For each participant, therefore, each block will contain a different, randomly-generated trial order.

Sample size rationale

1.0 Pilot Data To determine an appropriate sample size for our experiment, we conducted a pilot study and analyzed it as we propose below in our analysis plan. We collected data from 29 participants using the sampling method described above. The experimental procedure was the same as that described above with the exception that participants had 1567 ms to respond to the probe and were not told specifically how fast they would need to respond. As we discuss next, we analyzed these data to answer both of our research questions. We focused on the mean RT data as we did not observe a “standard” CSE in the mean ER data; that is, the interaction between previous trial congruency (congruent, incongruent) and current trial congruency (congruent, incongruent) was not significant. 1.1 Do congruent trials, incongruent trials, or both drive the CSE? We began by running a 3x3 repeated-measures ANOVA -- including all three levels of previous trial congruency and current trial congruency. This revealed a significant interaction F(4, 112) = 22.846, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.449. To further explore this interaction, we conducted a 3x2 ANOVA including all three levels of previous trial congruency (congruent, neutral, and incongruent) but only two levels of current trial congruency (congruent and incongruent). Restricting the analysis to the 3x2 ANOVA allowed us to focus on how the congruency effect (i.e. the difference between current incongruent and current congruent trial RT) changed as a function of previous trial congruency. The 3x2 ANOVA revealed an interaction between previous and current trial congruency, F(2, 56) = 34.003, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.548, which indicates that the magnitude of the congruency effect depended on the previous trial’s congruency. To determine the source of this interaction (i.e., whether congruent trials, incongruent trials, or both drive the CSE), we conducted three further analyses. First, we conducted the standard 2x2 repeated-measures ANOVA with previous trial congruency (congruent, incongruent) and current trial congruency (congruent, incongruent) as factors. As expected, we found a significant interaction (i.e., a CSE), F(1, 28) = 40.254, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.590, because the congruency effect was larger after congruent trials (16 ms) than after incongruent trials (-25 ms). Second, we conducted a 2x2 repeated-measures ANOVA with previous trial congruency (congruent, neutral) and current trial congruency (congruent, incongruent) as factors. We found a significant interaction, F(1, 28) = 45.094, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.617, because the congruency effect was larger after congruent trials (16 ms) than after neutral trials (-24 ms). This pattern suggests that congruent trials drive the CSE. Third, we conducted a 2x2 repeated-measures ANOVA with previous trial congruency (incongruent, neutral) and current trial congruency (congruent, incongruent). In contrast to the other 2x2 ANOVAs, we did not find a significant interaction, F(1, 28) = 0.047, p = 0.829, partial η2 = 0.002, because the congruency effect was equivalent following neutral trials (-24 ms) and incongruent trials (-25ms). This pattern suggests that incongruent trials do not drive the CSE. 1.2 Does inhibition, activation, or both explain the negative congruency effect after incongruent trials? As anticipated, our analyses revealed a negative congruency effect after incongruent trials: that is, congruent trial RTs (M = 487 ms) were slower than incongruent trial RTs (M = 462 ms). We then compared RTs in congruent, neutral, and incongruent trials that followed incongruent trials. We conducted three paired-samples t tests to evaluate each pairwise comparison. Congruent trial RTs were significantly slower (M = 487 ms) than incongruent trial RTs (M = 462 ms), F(1, 28) = 14.677, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.344, (2) congruent trial RTs were significantly slower (M = 487 ms) than neutral trial RTs (M = 466 ms), F(1, 28) = 11.655, p = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.294, and (3) neutral trial RTs (M = 466 ms) were not significantly different from incongruent trial RTs (M = 462 ms), F(1, 28) = 0.477, p = 0.495, partial η2 = 0.017. This pattern of results suggests that participants inhibited the current trial distractor following incongruent trials, making them slower on congruent trials than on neutral and incongruent trials. The results do not indicate that participants activated the response opposite of that cued by the distracter. In this scenario, incongruent trial RTs would have been faster than neutral trial RTs, rather than equivalent. This outcome implies that participants only inhibited the current trial distractor after an incongruent trial. 2.0 Results of Experiment 1: 2.1 Exploratory Analyses of the data from Experiment 1 In the study following our pilot, we replicated the answer to our first question (i.e. which previous trial type(s) drives the CSE) but not to our second question (i.e. how do control processes modulate responses following incongruent trials). For our first question, we found that the congruency effect was larger (11 ms) after congruent trials than after neutral trials (-10 ms), F(1, 39) = 21.915, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.360, but did not differ between incongruent (-18 ms) and neutral trials (-10 ms), F(1, 39) = 2.888, p = 0.097, partial η2 = 0.069. Consistent with our pilot data, this result suggests that only previous congruent trials drive the CSE. However, the results for our second question differed from what we observed in the pilot study. Here, neutral trial RTs (M = 463 ms) were between that of congruent (M = 472 ms) and incongruent RTs (M = 454 ms) following incongruent trials and did not significantly differ from either congruent, F(1, 39) = 2.409, p = 0.129, partial η2 = 0.058 or incongruent trials, F(1, 39) = 2.251, p = 0.142, partial η2 = 0.055. This is inconsistent with our pilot data, and did not support our hypothesis that control processes only inhibit the response cued by the distractor. Based on observations of the subject-specific mean RTs, we hypothesized that our failure to find the “inhibition only” pattern in our study was caused by especially slow responses to neutral trials in at least some of the subjects. To investigate this possibility, we conducted exploratory analyses wherein we removed all trials that included responses greater than 900 ms. When we excluded responses greater than 900 ms, we replicated both of the effects we found in our pilot study. First, we found that only previous congruent trials drove the CSE. That is, the congruency effect was larger after congruent trials (13 ms) than after neutral trials (-12 ms), F(1, 37) = 33.485, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.475 and equivalent after incongruent (-17 ms) and neutral trials (-12 ms), F(1, 37) = 1.576, p = 0.217, partial η2 = 0.041. Second, we found that participants only inhibited the distractor response following an incongruent trial. That is, after an incongruent trial, RTs were slower in congruent trials (M = 460 ms) than neutral trials (M = 445 ms), F(1, 37) = 13.599, p &lt; 0.001, partial η2 = 0.269 but equivalent in incongruent (M = 443 ms) and neutral trials (M = 445 ms), F(1, 37) = 0.364, p = 0.550, partial η2 = 0.01. Since excluding trials with longer response times reduced the overall accuracy of 2 subjects below our 75% criterion, these exploratory analyses are based on 38 subjects rather than the 40 total subjects in our main analyses from the same study. Sample Size Rationale After conducting this series of analyses, we used the results of the pilot study to perform power analyses. We used the results of the pilot study to guide our power analyses, because excluding trials and subjects from our exploratory analyses in Experiment 1 likely reduced statistical power. We entered effect sizes for each significant statistical test in G*power, calculating the sample size necessary for finding each effect with 95% power. The power analyses revealed that we would need a sample size of 35 to find our smallest significant effect (partial η2 = 0.294) with 95% power. Thus, we decided to collect data from 40 participants.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will conduct an omnibus repeated-measures ANOVA with previous trial congruency (congruent, neutral, incongruent) and current trial congruency (congruent, neutral, incongruent) as factors. We will also conduct a 3x2 repeated-measures ANOVA with previous trial congruency (congruent, neutral, and incongruent) and current trial congruency (congruent, and incongruent) as factors. The most critical analyses for answering our first research question (“which trial types drive the CSE?”) will be a series of three 2x2 within-subjects ANOVAs on mean RT. The first ANOVA will include two levels of previous trial type (congruent, incongruent) and current trial type (congruent, incongruent). The second ANOVA will include two levels of previous trial type (congruent, neutral) and two levels of current trial type (congruent, incongruent). The third ANOVA will include two levels of previous trial type (incongruent, neutral) and two levels of current trial type (congruent, incongruent). We will conduct analogous ANOVAs on the mean ER data to assess any speed-accuracy tradeoffs. For our second research question (i.e., what mechanism drives response modulation after incongruent trials?), we will evaluate the three possible pairwise comparisons among congruent, incongruent, and neutral trials after incongruent trials: congruent vs. incongruent, congruent vs. neutral, and incongruent vs. neutral. We will use three paired t-tests or three equivalent one-way repeated-measures ANOVAs for these comparisons.

Transformations

Inference criteria

We will use significant 2-tailed p-values for the repeated-measures ANOVAs and t-tests. We will consider p-values (2-tailed) less than 0.05 to be significant.

Data exclusion

We will exclude subjects whose overall accuracy during the nine blocks of test trials is lower than 75%. If this occurs, our final sample size will be larger than 40 subjects. However, we will only include data from the first 40 subjects with usable data (i.e., overall accuracy greater than 75%) in the final analyses. In the analysis of mean probe RT, we will exclude (1) trials with errors or omitted responses, (2) trials in which subjects take longer than 900 ms to respond, (3) trials following trials with errors, omitted, or slow responses, and (4) outliers in the current trial. We will define outliers as trials with RTs greater than 3 Sn, calculated separately for each of the nine possible combinations of previous trial congruency and current trial congruency. Finally, we will also exclude practice trials and the first trial of each test block. In other words, we will classify a response as being correct only if subjects pressed the correct key within 900 ms and the trial was not an outlier. Using this classification, this is what we will require in the previous and current trial in order to analyze mean probe RT in the current trial. Mean Probe RT will be calculated for only the following trials: Previous trial: prime (no response), probe (correct) Current Trial: prime (no response), probe (correct). In the analysis of mean probe ER, we will exclude the same trials with the exception of (1) above. This criterion refers to errors and response omissions when responding to the probe in the current trial, which forms the dependent measure. This corresponds to the following. Mean Probe ER will be calculated for only the following trials: Previous trial: prime (no response), probe (correct) Current Trial: prime (no response), probe (correct, incorrect, or omission)

Missing data

We will replace each participant who performs the task with less than 75% overall accuracy.
