The Relationship Between Natural Emotion Vocabularies, Emotion Differentiation and Depressive Symptoms in an Adolescent Sample

OSF ID: ndkzv | Registered: 2023-03-19T12:30:34.300995 | Showing: original version (1 version(s) on OSF)

Study Information

Hypotheses

H1: Replicating previous work examining EVs (Vine et al., 2020), we anticipate that larger negative EVs will be negatively associated with the following text-derived variables: positive emotional tone, we-words and leisure words. We expect negative EVs to be positively associated with the following text-derived variables: cognitive processing, negative emotional tone, general vocabulary size, illness words, I-words, affiliation words and achievement words. We anticipate that negative EVs will be positively associated with self-reported neuroticism, and that negative EVs will be higher among females. We anticipate that larger positive EVs will be negatively associated with the following text derived variables: cognitive processing &amp; we-words. We anticipate that positive EVs will be positively associated with the following text-derived variables: positive emotional tone, general vocabulary size, illness words, affiliation words, achievement words and leisure words. We expect positive EV to be negatively associated with self-reported neuroticism and age. As a test of robustness, we will also examine these associations when controlling for general vocabulary size and emotional tone. H2: We will test two competing hypotheses when examining the association between EVs and ED: 1) The emotion lexicon hypothesis suggests that larger negative and positive EVs will be associated with higher negative and positive ED respectively. 2) The emotion complexity hypothesis suggests that larger negative and positive EVs are associated with lower negative and positive ED. As a test of robustness, we will also examine these associations when controlling for general vocabulary size and emotional tone. As a second test of robustness, if either ED variables show significant associations with EV variables, we will control for emotional intensity corresponding to the valence of the ED variable (i.e., controlling for positive emotional intensity if the positive ED is significant, and negative emotional intensity if negative ED is significant). H3: We hypothesize that more depressive symptoms will be associated with larger negative EVs and lower depressive symptoms with smaller positive EVs. As a test of robustness, we will also examine these associations when controlling for general vocabulary size and emotional tone. H4: We hypothesize that more depressive symptoms will be associated with larger negative EVs and lower depressive symptoms with smaller positive EVs, above and beyond the relationship between negative and positive ED and depressive symptoms. As a test of robustness, we will also examine these associations when controlling for general vocabulary size and emotional tone.

Explanation of existing data

The current study will utilize data that have already been collected at the University of Rochester as a part of a longitudinal study examining predictors of adolescent emotional health (Starr et al., 2017).

Data collection procedures

The present study will utilize previously collected data. A community sample of adolescents, age 14-17 years, was recruited from a midsized metropolitan area of the northeastern United States. Exclusion criteria included lack of fluency in English, evidence of a pervasive developmental disorder, prior diagnosis of bipolar or psychotic disorder, and any major physical or neurological disorder. Participants and their primary caregivers attended an in-person lab session during which they completed a modified version of the UCLA Life Stress Interview for use in adolescents (LSI;(Hammen et al., 2000; Shih et al., 2006), the Schedule for Affect Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children-Revised Epidemiologic version for the DSM-IV (K-SADS;(Orvaschel &amp; Puig-Antich, 1987), and questionnaires (see measures). Adolescents were given instructions regarding the completion of the weeklong daily survey procedure, which included the questions needed to compute ED. Starting the day after the in-person lab session, adolescents were asked to complete surveys four times a day for seven days on either their smartphones, computers, or a study-provided tablet. Surveys were scheduled to be completed at approximately 4–6-hour intervals, with one survey prompt in the morning, two survey prompts midday and one survey prompt before bedtime. Participants earned up to $160 in compensation depending on survey compliance.

Sample size

The full sample consists of 241 adolescents. We will use maximum likelihood estimation to account for missing data (see analysis plan).

Measured variables

Emotion Vocabularies (EVs). EVs will be calculated from LSI transcripts using Vocabulate (Vine et al., 2020). Audio recordings of the LSI were automatically transcribed using NVivo (Edhlund &amp; McDougall, 2019), and manually checked for accuracy. Vocabulate quantifies the size of an individual’s EV by counting the frequencies of unique emotion words. This approach is intended to count words associated with emotional states (happy, annoyed, sad) as opposed to emotionally themed words (e.g., victory, idiotic, fight). Importantly, varied inflections of the same word (e.g., sad, sadness, sadly) will be counted as the same emotion word. In line with previous work (Vine et al., 2020), we will set Vocabulate to utilize the same finalized list of 92 negative emotion and 53 positive emotion words. While the difference in size between the negative and positive emotion word lists is notable, Vine et al. have argued that this discrepancy should not impact group level effects from natural language samples. English, like many languages, contains more negative than positive emotion words in its lexica (Bednarek, 2008; Jackson et al., 2019). Therefore, even if the positive emotion word list were expanded to include an equal number of terms, in practice it would not affect sample wide relationships as the rates of nuanced words used to describe positive emotions is lower than for negative emotions. Scores will be computed separately for Negative and Positive EVs. EV scores will be controlled for word count to eliminate the confounding effect of transcript length with the use of the below formula. Therefore, EV scores will represent the number of unique emotion words as a percentage rate of the total word count per transcript. Emotion Vocabulary (EV)=((# Unique emotion words)/(Total word count)) ×100. General Vocabulary Size. As EV may be an artifact of vocabulary size, we will estimate the general vocabulary size of each transcript. We will use the same procedure as Vine et al. (2020) by using Vocabulate to compute the number of unique words divided by the number of total words in the transcript. General vocabulary size, like EV, is expressed as a percentage, with higher values indicating larger vocabulary size. To compute a relevant general vocabulary size, emotion words included in the EV computations and function words will be excluded (pronouns, prepositions, articles, etc.). Emotional Tone. Negative and positive emotional tone will be calculated from the LSI transcripts using traditional linguistic approaches using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC(Pennebaker et al., 2015). Past studies have demonstrated LIWC’s accuracy in reflecting affective traits and states (Newell et al., 2018; Saxbe et al., 2013). Additional Text-derived Variables. To replicate the hypotheses posed in H1, we will derived the additional text-derived variables (cognitive processing, illness words, I-words, we-words, affiliation words, achievement words and leisure words) using traditional linguistic approaches using Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC(Pennebaker et al., 2015). Neuroticism. Neuroticism was assessed using the 8-item Neuroticism subscale from the 40-item Big 5 Mini-Marker Scale (Saucier, 1994). The Mini-Marker scale was developed from Goldberg’s set of 100 unipolar adjective markers of the Big-Five personality factor structure (Goldberg, 1992). The Mini-Marker subset has demonstrated good psychometric properties including reliabilities (Saucier, 1994). Emotion Differentiation. ED will be derived from daily survey data, which participants completed on a mobile device four times a day for one week. This is the gold standard approach to deriving ED (Hill &amp; Updegraff, 2012; Tugade &amp; Fredrickson, 2007). As a part of each survey prompt, participants rated their current emotions on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 5 (extremely). Negative emotion items were drawn from the 15-item version of the Profile of Mood States (POMS-15;(Ebesutani et al., 2012). The POMS assesses negative emotions across five domains with three items for each domain: anxious mood (anxious, on edge, uneasy); depressed mood (sad, hopeless, discouraged); anger (angry, resentful, annoyed); and fatigue (fatigued, worn out, and exhausted). Positive emotion items were drawn from the positive affect subscale of the POMS-15 and included five items: happy, proud, cheerful, lively, and joyful. NED and PED will be calculated by taking the average intraclass correlation (ICC) for all negative or positive emotion items across all completed survey prompts. To maintain consistency with past literature, ICCs will be subtracted from 1 to create NED and PED variables where higher values reflect greater differentiation. This procedure is well established and has been validated across several studies (Hill &amp; Updegraff, 2012; Tugade &amp; Fredrickson, 2007). Consistent with past methods, ED will be calculated using the average intraclass correlation of all available items (Tugade &amp; Fredrickson, 2007). In line with past work in this sample, participants with fewer than three surveys will have ED scores derived using FIML instead of from the EMA data (Starr et al., 2020). As covariates, emotion intensity items will be derived by averaging all NE items across all time points (for NE intensity), and all PE items across all time points (for PE intensity). Depression. The present study will utilize a model comparison approach to identify a latent measure of depression from the following variables. The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI; Beck et al., 1996) is a widely used 21-item self-report measure that assesses the presence and severity of depressive symptoms. Studies have demonstrated the BDI’s excellent psychometric properties as well as sensitivity and specificity for detecting depression in adolescent samples (Ambrosini et al., 1991; Beck et al., 1988). The BDI can be divided into two subscales, the affective symptoms subscale (8-items) and the somatic symptoms subscale (13 items; Whisman et al., 2000). The Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (DASS) depression subscale is a 7-item self-report measure that assesses depressive symptoms with strong discriminant validity (Lovibond &amp; Lovibond, 1995). Studies support the strong psychometric properties of the DASS (Clara et al., 2001). The DASS has been validated for use in adolescent samples (Szabó, 2010). The Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; Goodman, 1997) is a brief, 25-item self- report measure that assesses children’s externalizing and internalizing behaviors. The scale is comprised of five subscales of five items each. Adolescents completed a self-report and a caregiver reported on the adolescent’s symptoms using the parent version of the SDQ. The present study will utilize the emotional problems subscale from both adolescent and caregiver report. Clinical diagnostic interviews were completed separately with the child and their caregiver using the Schedule for Affect Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children-Revised Epidemiologic version for the DSM-IV (K-SADS; Orvaschel &amp; Puig-Antich, 1987). The K-SADS is a widely-used semi-structured interview assessing past and present clinical disorders and has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties (Kaufman et al., 1997). A dimensional numeric coding system was used to capture interview assessed ratings of major depressive episodes. Symptoms were rated on a five-point scale: 0 = no symptoms; 1 = mild symptoms; 2 = moderate, subthreshold symptoms; 3 = DSM-IV criteria met; 4 = DSM-IV criteria met with high severity and impairment. The present study will utilize the maximum score of either current major depressive disorder or dysthymia.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Analyses will be completed in Mplus. Hypotheses 1 &amp; 2 will utilize Pearson’s correlations and Pearson’s partial correlations. Hypotheses 3 &amp; 4 will utilize two step structural equation modeling. 1. Measurement Model We will develop a measurement model of depression using all available multimethod data and a model comparison approach. We will specify a latent depression factor three ways, and compare relative model fit using absolute fit indices and chi square difference tests. 1. KSADS, BDI (somatic symptoms), BDI (affective symptoms), DASS (depression subscale), SDQ (child report: emotional problems), SDQ (parent report: emotional problems) 2. KSADS, BDI, DASS (depression subscale), SDQ (child report: emotional problems), SDQ (parent report: emotional problems) 3. KSADS, BDI (somatic symptoms), BDI (affective symptoms), DASS (depression subscale) We will use multiple indexes of global model fit (Brown, 2015). Firstly, a non-significant χ2 likelihood ratio test indicates good fit. CFI and TLI values indicate acceptable fit if they are larger than .90, and good fit if they are larger than .95. RMSEA values below 0.10 indicate acceptable fit and values below 0.06 indicate good fit. If the model fit of the best-fitting model remains unacceptable, we will consult modification indices to consider only theoretically justified modification (e.g., correlating BDI-derived scores). If model fit remains unacceptable, we will proceed with a just-identified latent depression model with the three most established indicators only (e.g., KSADS, BDI, DASS). We will consider a just identified measurement model to have acceptable fit if the factor loadings are significant and greater than .4. If model fit is still poor, we will forego the measurement model and use the BDI as a manifest measure of depression and test the below hypotheses using multiple regression. 2. Structural Models If we retain a latent depression variable as described above, we will assess model fit of structural Model 1 which will examine whether negative and positive EVs predict the latent depression variable. Model 2 will examine if this association holds when controlling for ED. We will use multiple indexes of global model fit (Brown, 2015). Firstly, a non-significant χ2 likelihood ratio test indicates good fit. CFI and TLI values indicate acceptable fit if they are larger than .90, and good fit if they are larger than .95. RMSEA values below 0.10 indicate acceptable fit and values below 0.06 indicate good fit. If global model fit is poor, we will consider modification indices which are theoretically justified, erring on the side of conservatism to avoid overfitting our models. As a test of robustness, we will assess if the associations in both models hold when controlling for general vocabulary size and emotional tone as a test of robustness.
