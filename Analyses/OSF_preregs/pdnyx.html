Study Information

Hypotheses

Replicating Past Work: 1) People will update their injunctive beliefs (injunctive belief_new) to a greater extent for the fairness vignettes than for the new harm vignettes but will not update their injunctive beliefs more for the fairness vignettes than the old harm vignettes used in the previous study (Study 4b). For the injunctive belief_old DV, we expect that people will update more for the fairness vignettes than either set of harms vignettes. 2) People will update their stated likelihood of engaging in the behavior to a greater extent for the fairness vignettes than both sets of harm vignettes. 3) People will update their personal moral beliefs more for the fairness vignettes than for both sets of harm vignettes but there will be no differences in updating for other people’s moral beliefs. Primary Predictions: 4) Vignette fairness and convention ratings will positively predict injunctive norm updating while harm ratings will negatively predict injunctive norm updating and preference ratings will not predict updating. Secondary Predictions: 5) The fairness vignettes will be rated the highest on fairness followed by the old harm vignettes, then the new harm vignettes, then the conventional vignettes, with the lowest fairness ratings for the preference vignettes. 6) Both sets of harm vignettes will be rated the highest on harm followed by the fairness vignettes, then the conventional vignettes, with the lowest harm ratings for the preference vignettes. 7) The preference vignettes will be rated the highest on preference followed by the conventional vignettes, then the fairness vignettes, with the lowest preference ratings for the harm vignettes. 8) The new harm vignettes will be rated the highest on severity followed by the old harm vignettes, then the fairness vignettes, then the conventional vignettes, with the lowest severity ratings for the preference vignettes. 9) Both sets of harm vignettes will be rated the highest on impacting others followed by the fairness vignettes, then the conventional vignettes, with the lowest other impact ratings for the preference vignettes.

Study design

Participants will be assigned to one of two descriptive norm conditions (weak, strong) between-subjects (see Manipulated variables). Participants will first answer the DVs for each behavior without the descriptive norm information to assess their priors, after which they will complete a short filler task consisting of 10 unrelated trivia questions before receiving the behaviors again with either the weak or strong descriptive norm information (updating task; see Measured variables). Additionally, participants will see all 5 vignette type conditions (fairness, harm-new, harm-old, conventional, preferences) within-subjects in a randomized order and will see 2 negatively valenced vignettes per vignette type (as used in the last two previous studies). Participants will also complete ratings of 10 vignettes (vignette rating task; 2 harm-new, 2 harm-old, 2 fairness, 2 preferences, 2 conventional) on several dimensions (described below, see Measured Variables) in a randomized order.

Randomization

Participants will be randomly assigned to one descriptive norm condition (weak, strong) between-subjects and will see five vignette type conditions (fairness, harm-new, harm-old, conventional, preferences) within-subjects in a randomized order. The order of the two vignettes within each vignette type will also be randomized. We will counterbalance the order in which participants receive the updating task and vignette rating task such that half of the participants will randomly be assigned to complete the updating task followed by the vignette ratings and vice versa.

Data collection procedures

We will collect data online using Amazon Mechanical Turk.

Sample size

We will aim to collect a total N of 400. These numbers are approximate due to our recruitment via Amazon Mechanical Turk and the possibility that we will need to top up our sample due to exclusions (see Exclusions). Our top up procedure (see Other) will ensure that we have a minimum of N = 200 per descriptive norm condition (weak, strong).

Sample size rationale

The sample size is based on previous work using the same platform. A power analysis w/ 5,000 simulations found that we’d have 98.64% power with N=643 (~200 per vignette cell) to detect an effect of the interaction (d = 0.18) between condition (weak, strong) &amp; vignette type (fairness, harm) on injunctive differences (injunctive norm_old DV) from prior to post norm information (one of our key model of interest for this study). Effect size was taken from the same interaction term from a previous study (Study 4a).

Variables

Manipulated variables

Participants will be assigned to one of two conditions (descriptive norm: weak, strong) between-subjects and will see all five vignette types (fairness, harm-old, harm-new, conventional, preferences) within-subjects. In the weak descriptive norm condition, participants will be told that 20% (2/10, or 4/20) people in the scenario engaged in the relevant behavior. In the strong descriptive norm condition, participants will be told that 80% (8/10 or 16/20) people in that scenario engaged in the relevant behavior. In this study, we only use the negatively valenced vignettes used in previous studies.

Measured variables

Updating Task: 1) Injunctive norm_new: To what extent do other people think you should [behavior] (0-”not at all” to 100-”A great deal”) 2) Injunctive norm_old: In general, would most people approve of [the behavior]? (anchored from 0-’Definitely not approve’ to 100-’Definitely approve’) 3) Injunctive certainty: You answered [response to injunctive norm_new DV]. How certain are you in that response? (0-’Extremely uncertain’ to 100-’Extremely certain’) 4) Descriptive norm: In general, how many people would [engage in the behavior]? (0-’No one’ to 100-’Everyone’’) 5) Morality-Self: How moral do you personally think it is for someone to [engage in the behavior]? (0-’Extremely immoral’, 100-’Extremely moral’) 6) Morality-Other: How moral do you think other people think it is for someone to [engage in the behavior]? (0-’Extremely immoral’, 100-’Extremely moral’) 7) Likelihood: How likely would you be to [engage in the behavior]? (0-’Extremely unlikely’ to 100-’Extremely likely’) 8) Behavior: If you were in this situation, what would you do. (Binary: yes, do behavior; no, not do behavior) Vignette Rating Task: 1) Fairness - To what extent does [behavior] relate to fairness? (0-”Not at all” to 100-”Entirely”) 2) Harm - To what extent does [behavior] involve harming others? (0-”Not at all” to 100-”Entirely”) 3) Preference - To what extent is [behavior] a personal preference? (0-”Not at all” to 100-”Entirely”) 4) Conventional - To what extent is [behavior] a social convention? (0-”Not at all” to 100-”Entirely”) 5) Severity - How bad is it to [behavior]? (0-”Not bad at all” to 100-”Extremely bad”) 6) Self-Other impact - To what extent does [behavior] impact others as compared to yourself? (0-”Only impacts yourself” to 100-””Only impacts others”) 7) Fair-Harm Scale - Where does [behavior] fall on a scale from unfair to harmful (0-”Unfair” to 100-”Harmful”) Exploratory Variables: 1) We will ask participants to self-report how they define four of the terms used in the vignette rating task (fairness, harm, personal preference, social convention) in an open response question: “how do you define [term]?”

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

For all models described below, we will run linear mixed effects models (LMEM) with subject and vignette identity as random effects. Replicating Past Work: Model 1a: To determine whether descriptive norm condition differentially influences injunctive norm belief updating across vignette type, we will run a LMEM with injunctive norm_new difference scores as the response term and descriptive norm condition, vignette type (categorical: fairness, harm, conventional, preference), and their interaction as predictors. Model 1b: To determine whether descriptive norm condition differentially influences injunctive norm belief updating across vignette type, we will run a LMEM with injunctive norm_old difference scores as the response term and descriptive norm condition, vignette type (categorical: fairness, harm, conventional, preference), and their interaction as predictors. Model 2: To determine if descriptive norm condition differentially influences whether participants update their likelihood of engaging in the behavior across vignette type, we will run a LMEM with likelihood difference scores as the response term and descriptive norm condition, vignette type, and their interaction as predictors Model 3a: To determine if descriptive norm condition differentially influences whether participants update their personal moral ratings across vignette type, we will run a LMEM with self-moral difference scores as the response term and descriptive norm condition, vignette type, and their interaction as predictors. Model 3b: To determine if descriptive norm condition differentially influences whether participants update their ratings about others’ moral beliefs across vignette type, we will run a LMEM with other-moral difference scores as the response term and descriptive norm condition, vignette type, and their interaction as predictors. Primary Predictions: Model 4: To determine whether fairness, harm, preference and convention ratings predict descriptive norm updating effects on injunctive beliefs, we will run a LMEM with injunctive norm difference score as the response term and vignette type, descriptive norm condition, fairness ratings, harm ratings, preference ratings, convention ratings, severity ratings, self-other ratings, and the interaction between vignette type and condition as predictors. Secondary Predictions: Model 5: To determine whether the fairness vignettes are rated as higher in fairness than the other vignette types, we will run a LMEM with fair ratings as the response term and vignette type (fair, harm-new, harm-old, preference, conventional) as a predictor. Model 6: To determine whether the harm vignettes are rated as higher in harm than the other vignette types, we will run a LMEM with harm ratings as the response term and vignette type (fair, harm-new, harm-old, preference, conventional) as a predictor. Model 7: To determine whether the preference vignettes are rated as more preference-like than the other vignette types, we will run a LMEM with preference ratings as the response term and vignette type (fair, harm-new, harm-old, preference, conventional) as a predictor. Model 8: To determine whether the new harm vignettes are rated as more severe than the other vignette types, we will run a LMEM with severity ratings as the response term and vignette type (fair, harm-new, harm-old, preference, conventional) as a predictor. Model 9: To determine whether the harm vignettes are rated as impacting other people more than the other vignette types, we will run a LMEM with self-other ratings as the response term and vignette type (fair, harm-new, harm-old, preference, conventional) as a predictor. For models 1-4, we will run them twice, once with the old harm vignettes and again substituting in the new harm vignettes. We will also run exploratory analyses including only the harm vignette data with vignette type (harm-new, harm-old) as a 2-level categorical predictor to compare updating between the different sets of harm vignettes. For models 1-3, 5-9, we will run two multivariate multiple regression models predicting all dependent variables of interest by condition and vignette type in one model to ensure our univariate models for each dependent variable are robust to multiple comparisons. For model 5, we will run it twice, once with the injunctive norm_new DV and again with the injunctive norm_old DV.

Inference criteria

Data exclusion

We will exclude participants who answer the attention check questions incorrectly (“If you're paying attention to this HIT, select 'Somewhat disagree'; “If you’re paying attention to this HIT, slide the scale all the way to ‘Extremely likely’). We will exclude any participants who respond with 5 or higher to the question asking how many questions they answered with little or no thought put into them (this criteria is more lenient than in previous studies in this project to account for the increased number of questions participants will answer).

Missing data

Exploratory analysis

We may run exploratory analyses on the open response question asking participants to define harm, fairness, preferences, and conventions using text analysis or coding.

Other

Other

We plan on soft launching our survey with n=40 to ensure that our survey and display logic are working correctly. If there are no processing errors in the survey, we will include these data points in our final sample. Top up procedure: to ensure we have at least N = 400, we will top up our sample by re-running the study with the smallest batch possible based on the exclusion rate from the first launch.
