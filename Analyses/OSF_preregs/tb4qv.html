The influence of age on stress, worry, and mood during the COVID-19 pandemic

OSF ID: tb4qv | Registered: 2020-07-03T17:06:07.898699 | Showing: original version (1 version(s) on OSF)

Study Information

Hypotheses

Specific Aims- (1) To examine the relationship between age and mood, stress, worry, and depression symptoms within the context of the current chronic stressors associated with COVID19 both during the early stages of wide spread stay at home orders and in the days prior to the relaxation of these orders. (2) To determine the potential moderating effect of exercise and self-reported experience of social connectedness on the relationship between age and mood, stress, worry, and depression symptoms within the context of the current chronic stressors associated with COVID19 both during the early stages of wide spread stay at home orders and in the days prior to the relaxation of these orders. Hypothesis: (1a) During the first two weeks of data collection (March 20-April 4, 2020), age will itself be positively associated with positive mood (as measured by the positive affect scale of the PANAS) and negatively associated with measures of depression, stress, and worry, and negative mood (as measured by the negative affect scale of the PANAS, modified PHQ-9 without the suicidality question, and COVID-related stress and worry questions), a prediction that is based on a large literature suggesting that adults become able to regulate negative emotions with age (Mather, 2012). This hypothesis will be tested against the alternate hypothesis that the benefits of age typically seen are reduced in the context of the stress of the COVID pandemic. (1b) During the final two weeks of wide spread stay at home orders (April 16-May 1, 2020), we expect that following the chronic threat and anxiety produced by COVID19, and in the context of society opening up more but in ways that benefit younger adults more than older adults who are still urged to stay home, the protective factors associated with age will become less apparent, such that age will show a lesser advantage to measures of mood, depression, stress, and worry than it did during the early weeks of the pandemic. This hypothesis will be tested against the alternative hypothesis that the benefits of age persist throughout the time of the stay-home orders and into these final weeks prior to reopening. (2a) During both the early and later time windows, we expect age to be negatively associated with exercise and self-reported social connectedness, and each of these factors to be positively associated with mood. As noted in #1, we expect the effects of age to dominate in the first phase of data collection (Mar 20-Apr 4, 2020). However, by the final two weeks of wide spread stay at home orders (April 16-May 1, 2020), we predict that exercise and self-reported social connectedness will be the primary drivers of effective coping. Thus, if there are reduced effects of age on wellbeing in the final two weeks as compared to the first two weeks (Hypothesis 1b), this age x time interaction may be mediated by older adults’ reduced exercise and reduced subjective experience of social connections during the pandemic.

Study design

Our study is a mixed design. It has been established as a longitudinal within-subject design to determine the impact of COVID19 and response measures on participants over time. We have also collected important demographic information (age, race, sex, gender, LGBTQ status, socioeconomic status, essential/healthcare workers, etc) that may be used either as continuous or dichotomous variables for between-subject comparisons.

Explanation of existing data

We have been collecting the data since March 21, 2020 in order to longitudinally track the impact of COVID19. Our data has been stored primarily on REDCap since that time, and only Dr. Cunningham and Dr. Kensinger had access to it for a majority of that time. Dr. Eric Fields was recently granted access to assist with data cleaning. We are currently in the process of cleaning the data set, starting with the daily surveys. We ran a very preliminary analysis looking at just the first two weeks of data collection. This was done prior to the start of data cleaning (and as such should be considered unreliable at best) and does not cover the extent of the research question presented here.

Data collection procedures

Adults (18+ years old) are permitted to enroll in the study. Participants sign up for the study using a Boston College (BC) IRB-approved online consent form. After consenting, they provide their email address, which is the only identifiable information directly requested in the study. The email addresses are transferred to a password protected sheet on a secure BC server, and a unique and de-identified Subject ID is generated for each participant. Participants are then sent an anonymous survey link through Redcap (a secure, password-protected web application for building and managing online surveys and databases) via email to complete the initial demographic form. Once the demographic form is complete, each participant receives the Full Version (see Variables tab for description) of the daily survey for three consecutive days. Following the third day of receiving the Full Version, the participants are added to an email listserv with other participants in their time zone that is set to deliver daily anonymous survey links between 7-9am. From March 20, 2020 through May 20, 2020, participants on the listservs received the Full Version of the survey 2x per week, and the Short Version (see Variables tab for description) of the survey 5x per week. From May 21, 2020 through June 23, 2020, participants on the listservs only received the Full Version of the survey 2x per week. From June 24, 2020 until the end of the study period, newly enrolled participants will only receive the Full Version of the daily survey for the three consecutive days after they enroll, and the listservs will no longer be active. In addition to the longitudinal collection of daily surveys, we are releasing three rounds of one-time assessments for our participants to complete (Rounds 1-3) to increase the applicability of the daily survey data and give us a more comprehensive understanding of the impact of COVID19 on factors related to mental health and well-being (see Variables tab for description). Invitations and reminders for Rounds 1 and 2 are sent through REDCap, and as such email addresses, Study ID, and responses are stored together on the secured site. At the completion of the assessment period, the responses to the assessments are downloaded and stored on our secure BC database. Round 3 asks for further personal and health information. Given the need for increased security of this data, this round is collected through the use of email and anonymized survey links. Experimenters manually cross reference the completed surveys with the full participant list in order to determine which participants need reminders. The Participant ID will be the only means of syncing this data with previously collected data, and it has no direct link to email information. The compensation structure for the one-time surveys is also separate (see Compensation). To maximize the security and confidentiality of each participant, entering their unique and de-identified Subject ID at the beginning of every survey is the only way to sync all of their data. These Subject IDs are not included in the open-access database (new IDs have been randomly generated). At the completion of the study, the data will be downloaded and stored on our secure BC database. INCLUSION CRITERIA: All adults over the age of 18 were invited to participate in the study COMPENSATION: All participants that complete the initial demographic survey receive one entry into a drawing for one of 10 $50 Amazon gift cards. Additionally, for every 5 days of surveys that a participant fills out, they will receive one additional entry into that raffle. As compensation for each of the additional one-time assessments (Round 1-3), participants receive raffle entries into a raffle for one of 30 $50 Amazon Gift cards. Additionally, given the length and importance of the additional demographic questions queried in the Round 3 assessment, completion of that assessment is worth two entries into this final raffle. This raffle will be separate from the one described in the daily surveys, so in total 40 $50 Amazon gift cards will be raffled.

Sample size

Through a mixture of IRB-approved recruitment techniques (e.g. contact with previous participants, dissemination through professional networks, social media, etc.), we have recruited a large network of participants (approximately 1950) that enrolled into our study, with approximately 1500 of those enrolled completing the required onboarding procedures to begin receiving our daily surveys and one-time assessments. As recruitment has been rolling since March 20,2020 and participation in each of the surveys is optional, each survey will have different numbers of responses.

Sample size rationale

Participation was open globally to all adults 18+ to try to get as large and diverse a sample as possible to better understand the worldwide impact of COVID19. The primary constraint was our ability to effectively spread the word to interested and willing participants.

Stopping rule

We have elected to stop the daily assessments of our participants as we believe we have captured enough data to provide some understanding of the initial wave of COVID19, which was our primary goal. We have at least one follow-up survey planned at a later date, and reserve the right to attempt to restart data collection depending on the progression of the pandemic.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

For testing Hypothesis 1, we will average across all responses in each time bin for each variable. These averaged variables will be analyzed with a linear mixed model with a random intercept for subject. Each DV of interest will be analyzed in a separate model with age as a continuous predictor and time bin as a categorical predictor. For example, using syntax of lme4 R package: stress ~ age*time_bin + (1|sub_id) To test hypothesis 2, average self-reported social connectedness and proportion of days exercised within each time bin will be added to the model. For example: stress ~ age*time_bin*exercise*social + (1|sub_id)

Transformations

We do not intend to transform any variable in our primary analyses, with the exception of linear transformations common in regression (e.g., centering a variable around the mean). We do plan to transform some variables in some exploratory analyses which we describe in detail below.

Inference criteria

Inferences will be made based on hypothesis tests and confidence intervals for the coefficients in the regression model with alpha = 0.05. Type III sums of squares will be used for hypothesis testing.

Data exclusion

Analyses will be performed on the subset of survey respondents who live in the United States and for survey responses for which the reference date (the date that responses refer to) is unambiguous. Most variables used in these analysis are bounded (e.g., a likert scale from 1 to 7) and thus not subject to extreme outliers. For variables this does not apply to (e.g., steps and minute socializing in the exploratory analyses), a criteria will be determined for excluding implausible values.

Missing data

When averaging across observations in each time bin, missing values will be ignored. Linear mixed models naturally handle missing data, so no additional measures will be taken to deal with variables that are missing across all responses for a subject in a given time bin (i.e., missing values after averaging).

Exploratory analysis

As part of an exploratory analysis, we will also examine the impact of age on the complete sum of all worry questions as well as each of the worry questions separately to determine if COVID19 has had any age-related effects in other domains. We will also investigate the utility of using questions of virtual socializing and encountering others “face-to-face” as measures of social connectedness, although we believe both questions have significant limitations. These variables will be log transformed before being used in regression models. This reflects the expectation that these variables are more likely to have a logarithmic than linear relationship to mood outcomes—i.e., there will be a benefit of socializing and contact with people, but there will be diminishing returns to the benefit of these variables as the amount increase. In addition, the log transformation also reduces the significant positive skew and outliers in these variables. As such, our primary analyses are limited to their self-reported experience of social isolation/connectedness. We will also investigate the utility of using step count as a measure of exercise, although this is limited to participants that owned a step counter during the assessment period and reported it (~40% of our data). To reduce the impact of a few large outliers in the step variable, we plan to exclude any observations more than three median absolute deviations from the median. This is a common outlier detection rule (Leys, Delacre, Mora, Lakens, &amp; Ley, 2019).
