Temporal heterogeneity of eye-movements as a marker of prior information exposure

OSF ID: zgtvx | Registered: 2019-06-07T15:20:03.228851

Study Information

Title

Temporal heterogeneity of eye movements as a marker of prior information exposure

Authors

Michal Krol, Magdalena Ewa Krol

Description

Existing research shows that the order in which evidence arrives can bias its evaluation and the resulting decision in favour of information encountered early on, in a phenomenon known as the pre-decisional distortion of information. In many situations, people are supposed to evaluate a choice option based solely on a given set of information rather than any prior knowledge – for instance, when evaluating job applicants, or when acting as a juror in a trial. In those cases, existing research has shown that people deliberately fail to disregard to-be-ignored information. Our aim is to investigate if it is therefore possible to 'diagnose' a bias induced by prior information based on the way in which new information has been processed, and particularly on how it was visually examined.

Hypotheses

We hypothesize that, based on participants' eye movement sequences registered while viewing sets of opinion words describing investment prospects, it will be possible to predict, with accuracy significantly above chance, if the participants in question have received prior information about whether a transaction fee (or, alternatively, a positive premium) would be payable when choosing to invest. Specifically, based on the results of a pilot study, we predict that participants' temporal eye movement patterns will be more heterogenous when prior information was provided than when it was not. Accordingly, it should be possible to predict if a set of decision trials by different participants was preceded by prior information based on the similarities between the corresponding scanpaths and the extent to which they were 'atypical' within the whole set of scanpaths registered in the study.

Study design

Subjects evaluate a series of 90 investment opportunities based on historical New York Stock Exchange data. Each time, they are first told if, in case they choose to invest, they will pay a transaction fee ['fee condition'], receive a positive premium ['premium condition'], or that the fee / premium information will not be disclosed until later ['hidden information condition']. Next, they see a set of 35 most common expert opinion words from articles about the given stock (scraped from the largest information portal for investors, referred to as SA). Finally, they choose whether or not to invest. The subjects are informed that words from articles of those experts who are generally more positive about stocks are placed higher up on the screen. Exactly one-third (30) trials, randomly ordered, will correspond to each condition, and the presence of the fee / premium is independent of stock returns and opinions. To ensure that subjects are aware of this and trust that this is indeed the case, before beginning the study each subject draws an envelope with her lucky symbol: either circle or square. The result of the draw is not entered into the experimental software, but the subject knows that she will receive the premium in trials marked with her lucky symbol, and pay the fee in those marked with the other symbol. When choosing to invest, apart from the fee / premium, subjects receive a number of points proportional to the actual return of the stock in the given time period. Points are converted into a monetary award upon completion of the study. The full experimental schedule of all trials as randomized for each subsequent subject is attached (Wolfram Mathematica data file).

Sample size

approximately 100 subjects

Sample size rationale

We based our sample size on the analysis of a pilot study with 20 subjects. For each of those subjects, and separately for trials with hidden prior information, disclosed negative (fee) information, and disclosed positive (premium) information, we calculated the average ScanMatch similarity between the scanpaths of the given subject during the given type of trials and those of all other subjects during the same type of trials (data available on the OSF project page). We then conducted two paired two-tailed Wilcoxon tests: (1) of the difference between the average similarity between trials with hidden vs. disclosed negative information; and (2) of the difference between the average similarity between trials with hidden vs. disclosed positive information. Using GPower 3.1.9, for effect (1), we obtained an effect size of d = 0.359; and for effect (2) an effect size of 0.80. Based on the weaker of the two effects (1), two-tailed alpha = .05, and power = 0.90, we obtained a required sample size of 88. Accordingly, we will aim to recruit a number of subjects slightly above this value, i.e. approximately 100 subjects, so that the final sample after potential no-shows or exclusions would not fall below this threshold.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

In the first part of the analysis, we will calculate pairwise ScanMatch similarities between all scanpaths (constructed by matching fixations to 35 AOIs, i.e. the fixed locations of words displayed on the screen). Next, we will compute the average similarity between every scanpath and those of other subjects registered in the same experimental condition (hidden information / fee / premium). We will use this as the outcome variable in a linear mixed model with random subject effects, the experimental condition as the main independent variable, and scanpath length and trial number as controls, to test if scanpath similarity is increased when the information is hidden. We will aim to replicate the results by using two alternative dependent variables: (1) a one-dimensional t-SNE transformation of the scanpath similarity matrix (based on the MATLAB t-SNEp implementation); (2) the 'rarer-probability' measure (obtained using the RarerProbability function embedded in Wolfram Mathematica 12.0) describing the extent to which a given 2-dimensional t-SNE representation of the scanpath is typical among all scanpaths registered in the study (we expect to obtain larger values when prior information is hidden than when it is not). In the second part of the analysis, we will randomly split all scanpaths into two groups (the training sample and the testing sample), and then repeatedly draw scanpath subsets ('training examples') of a given size (20, 40, or 60), registered during a given experimental condition, from the training sample. For each training example, we will calculate the average ScanMatch similarity between all scanpaths in the subset, as well as the average values of the rarer-probability and one-dimensional t-SNE variables described above. Using the drawn training examples, we will train a machine-learning algorithm (Gradient Boosted Trees and a benchmark standard Logistic Regression) predicting the experimental condition from the three input variables. We will then evaluate the trained algorithm on the testing sample, and then reverse the process (training on what used to be the testing sample and evaluating on the training sample). We will repeat this two-fold cross validation process 5 times, and evaluate if predictions are significantly above chance (and above those given by a benchmark model trained on average reaction times) using the 5-2 cross validation test and a permutation test. We will conduct this testing procedure for two cases: 1) distinguishing between the hidden condition and the fee condition; and 2) distinguishing between the hidden condition and the premium condition. The associated data analysis code (in the Wolfram Mathematica language) that we will use to carry out the procedures described in this section, taking a given raw eye-data set as input, is attached.
