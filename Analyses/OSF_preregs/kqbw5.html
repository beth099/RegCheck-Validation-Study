Phases of Reappraisal Motive

Study Information

Hypotheses

We hypothesize that in the hedonic condition, positive emotion (PE) positively predicts people's implementation choice and is a better predictor than reappraisal plausibility. H1: When having the motive to feel better, people are more likely to implement a reappraisal when feeling more positive after generation. H2: When having the motive to feel better, PE is a better predictor of people's implementation choice than plausibility. We also hypothesize that in the understand condition, reappraisal plausibility positively predicts people's implementation choice and is a better predictor than PE. H3: When having the motive to understand the stressor, people are more likely to implement a reappraisal when it's more plausible. H4: When having the motive to understand the stressor, plausibility is a better predictor of people's implementation choice than PE.

Study design

This study will use a within-subject design and will be hosted through Qualtrics. Participants will first be introduced to reappraisal and the study procedure. The study consists of the following parts: 1. Stressor description Participants will be asked to think of one ongoing, negative stressor, briefly describe it, and report the intensity of their current emotions, their positive emotions, and their negative emotions. 2. Motive assignment Participants will be randomly assigned one of the two motives for the first stressor: to make themselves feel better about the stressor (i.e., pro-hedonic motive) or to better understand the stressor (i.e., understand motive). The order of motive assignment will be counterbalanced (i.e., pro-hedonic motive first and understand motive second vs. understand motive first and pro-hedonic motive second). 3. Reappraisal task For each stressor, participants will generate and write down four reappraisals, one at a time. The stressor description will be presented every time they try to generate a reappraisal to remind them of the negative experience. Following each reappraisal generation, participants will rate their positive emotions, negative emotions, and the reappraisal's plausibility and creativity (1 - Not at all, 7 - Very). They will also make a choice on whether to use the reappraisal later (No = 1, Yes = 2). 4. Implementation task One of the four reappraisals participants generated will be randomly selected for them to follow through their choice. If participants chose to use the selected reappraisal, they will be exposed to it for 15s while elaborating on it (i.e., implementation). If participants chose not to use the selected reappraisal, they will be exposed to the stressor description written by themselves for 15s and count the number of a certain letter in it (i.e., distraction). Participants will then rate their positive and negative emotions (1 - Not at all, 7 - Very). If they chose not to use the reappraisal, they will additionally report the number of the letter they counted. The same process (i.e., steps 1-4) will then be repeated once, but participants will be asked to write down another stressor and be assigned a different emotion regulation motive. 5. Reappraisal Scale Participants will complete the reappraisal scale from the emotion regulation questionnaire (Gross, J. J., &amp; John, O. P., 2003). Participants will rate to what extent they agree with each statement (1 - Strongly disagree, 4 - Neutral, 7 - Strongly agree). 6. Post-task survey and debrief Following the main task, participants will complete a post-task survey, which includes questions about the instructions, task compliance, and demographics. Finally, they will be debriefed and compensated.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited from Prolific. Eligible participants must be 18-64 years old. Participants will be included if they are from the US and fluent in English. Participants will receive $6 for compensation. They will not receive compensation if choosing to end participation during the session.

Sample size

We aim to recruit 225 participants.

Sample size rationale

We used R to conduct a power analysis. With 180 participants, we should be able to obtain &gt; 80% power to detect an odds ratio of 1.57 (the effects of positive emotion on implementation choice in our previous study) at the standard .05 alpha error probability. We aim to recruit 225 participants in total to account for potential exclusions.

Stopping rule

We will stop when we have obtained our full sample size.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Emotion regulation motive is manipulated as to feel better about the stressor vs. to better understand the stressor.

Measured variables

Positive emotion: how positive people feel, ranging from 1 (Not at all positive) to 7 (Very positive). Participants will rate their positive emotions after reflecting on the stressor, generating a reappraisal, and using a reappraisal or completing the letter counting task. Negative emotion: how negative people feel, ranging from 1 (Not at all negative) to 7 (Very negative). Participants will rate their negative emotions after reflecting on the stressor, generating a reappraisal, and using a reappraisal or completing the letter counting task. Regulatory choice: participants' choice to use a reappraisal (= 2) or to distract themselves by counting the number of a letter in the stressor description (= 1). Plausibility: how plausible the reappraisals are, ranging from 1 (Not at all plausible) to 7 (Very plausible). Creativity: how creative the reappraisals are, ranging from 1 (Not at all creative) to 7 (Very creative).

Indices

For descriptive analysis, we will average within phases (generation, implementation) and within motive condition (e.g., pro-hedonic motive) as well as within motive conditions contingent on choice (e.g., pro-hedonic trials in which participants choose implementation vs. distraction).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

To test H1: When having the motive to feel better, people are more likely to implement a reappraisal when feeling more positive after generation We will run a multilevel binomial regression model (Model 1) for trials in which people are assigned a motive to feel better. PE from generation will be entered as a Level 1 predictor to examine whether they predict the choice to implement a reappraisal. Participants will be included at Level 2 and their average PE from generation will be included as a covariate at Level 1. To test H2: When having the motive to feel better, PE is a better predictor of people's implementation choice than plausibility. We will additionally run two binomial regression models for trials in which people are assigned a motive to feel better. In Model 2, plausibility will be entered as a Level 1 predictor. Participants will be included at Level 2 and their average plausibility ratings will be entered as a covariate at Level 1. In Model 3, both PE from generation and plausibility will be entered as Level 1 predictors. Participants will be included at Level 2 and their average PE from generation and average plausibility ratings will be included as covariates at Level 1. We will then run two likelihood ratio tests comparing the fit of 1) Model 1 and Model 3, and 2) Model 2 and Model 3. To test H3: When having the motive to understand the stressor, people are more likely to implement a reappraisal when it's more plausible We will run a multilevel binomial regression model (Model 4) for trials in which people are assigned a motive to understand the stressor. Plausibility will be entered as a Level 1 predictor to examine whether they predict the choice to implement a reappraisal. Participants will be included at Level 2 and their average plausibility ratings will be included as a covariate at Level 1. To test H4: When having the motive to understand the stressor, plausibility is a better predictor of people's implementation choice than PE. We will additionally run two binomial regression models for trials in which people are assigned a motive to understand the stressor. In Model 5, PE from generation will be entered as a Level 1 predictor. Participants will be included at Level 2 and their average PE from generation will be entered as a covariate at Level 1. In Model 6, both PE from generation and plausibility will be entered as Level 1 predictors. Participants will be included at Level 2 and their average PE from generation and average plausibility ratings will be included as covariates at Level 1. We will then run two likelihood ratio tests comparing the fit of 1) Model 4 and Model 6, and 2) Model 5 and Model 6.

Transformations

Intensity, PE (positive emotion), NE (negative emotion), plausibility, and creativity scores will be centered. Participants' choice will be recoded: 1 = Distraction, 2 = Implementation.

Inference criteria

p = .05, two-tailed

Data exclusion

We will exclude data based on the following criteria: 1. Participants who answer both practice questions incorrectly will be excluded. 2. Participants who 1) described at least one motive incorrectly during the instruction section AND 2) failed to choose the two assigned motives at the end of the study will be excluded. 3. Participants who describe the same stressor twice or report making up one or both stressors will be excluded 4. Participants who are unable to briefly describe in writing the distraction/implementation instructions (if they ever distracted/implemented) or report low task compliance in the post-task manipulation check will be excluded.

Missing data

Trials missing PE scores will not be used in analyses entering PE as an IV or DV. Trials missing NE scores will not be used in analyses entering NE as an IV or DV. Trials missing plausibility data will not be used in analyses entering plausibility as an IV or DV. Trials missing creativity data will not be used in analyses entering creativity as an IV or DV.

Exploratory analysis

1. We will first examine the effects of order (pro-hedonic motive first vs. understand motive first) on implementation choice. If order does not predict choice, we will combine samples across orders. Otherwise, we will add order as a covariate and/or factor. 2. We will explore whether emotional intensity influences people’s implementation choice. If emotional intensity predicts choice, we will include it as a covariate in follow-up analyses. 3. We will examine participants' variations in choices between implementation and distraction (i.e., whether it's significantly different from 50%). 4. We will examine whether participants' PE after implementation is significantly different from PE after distraction when having the motive to feel better. 5. We will explore whether participants' PE after implementation is significantly different from PE after generation when having the motive to feel better. 6. We will examine whether people are more likely to use a reappraisal that is more creative for each motive condition separately and combined. 7. We will examine whether people who frequently use reappraisal are more likely to choose implementation overall. 8. We will examine whether 1) people have higher PE or lower NE after generation when having pro-hedonic (vs. instrumental) motives and 2) whether people generate more plausible reappraisals when having instrumental (vs. pro-hedonic) motives. 9. We will examine whether higher stressor intensity predicts lower PE after generation, higher NE after generation, and lower reappraisal plausibility. 10. We will also examine 1) whether positive emotions will increase the likelihood of implementation more when people have the motive to feel better (vs. understand the stressor) and 2) whether plausibility will increase the likelihood of implementation more when people have the motive to understand the stressor (vs. to feel better).
