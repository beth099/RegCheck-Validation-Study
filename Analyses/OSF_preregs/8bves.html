Zero-Sum Mindset and Hostile Attributions in Zero-Sum and Non-Zero-Sum Situations - A Pre-Registered Replication

OSF ID: 8bves | Registered: 2023-10-31T23:48:02.174921

Study Information

Hypotheses

Hypothesis 1: Zero-sum configurations of success promote hostile attributions. That is, when competitive situations are structured as zero-sum, individuals are more likely to attribute hostile intent to other competitors than when competitive situations are structured as non-zero-sum. (i.e., main effect of zero-sum situation on hostile attributions) Hypothesis 2: Zero-sum mindset predicts stronger hostile attributions (even in objectively non-zero-sum situations). That is, across competitive situations, individuals who hold a stronger zero-sum mindset are more likely to attribute hostile intent to other competitors than individuals who hold a weaker/non-zero-sum mindset (i.e., main effect of zero-sum mindset on hostile attributions). Hypothesis 3: Zero-sum mindset predicts greater intention to retaliate against the perceived hostility across situations, including in objectively non-zero-sum situations. That is, across competitive situations, individuals who hold a stronger zero-sum mindset are more likely to intend to retaliate against other competitors than individuals who hold a weaker/non-zero-sum mindset (i.e., main effect of zero-sum mindset on retaliation intentions).

Study design

This is a between subjects experimental design in which participants are randomly assigned (simple randomization) to one of two conditions: a zero-sum configuration of success condition or a non-zero-sum configuration condition. In both conditions, participants are presented with four vignettes (modeled after Dodge et al., 2015) describing four different kinds of competitive situations: athletics, academics, politics, and the job market. For each domain, participants read a story (told in the 2nd person) describing the competitive situation (e.g. a job interview), the reward (e.g., a desirable job), the reward structure, zero-sum or non-zero-sum depending on assignment (e.g., only one job available vs. variable number of jobs available), followed by manipulation check questions (ratings of zero-sum beliefs about the situation, e.g. "In this scenario, if I am successful, it means that other job candidates will be unsuccessful"), a description of an ambiguous incident (e.g., another candidate spills coffee on your shirt) and ratings of perceptions of hostile intent in the ambiguous situation, and their intention to retaliate. Importantly, the competitive situations (e.g., interviewing for a job), the objective outcomes of success (e.g., getting the job), and the ambiguous situations (e.g., coffee spills on your shirt), and all measures, are identical across the conditions. The only feature that varies between conditions is the description of the reward structure, that is, whether success is zero-sum (e.g., only one job available) or non-zero-sum (e.g., variable number of job positions to be filled by qualified candidates). Lastly, participants complete measures of zero-sum mindset followed by demographic variables (age, sex, race, education, income).

Randomization

Simple randomization with participants randomly assigned to one of two conditions (approximately equal in size) using the Qualtrics randomizer.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited using the Prolific platform and paid $1.22 (USD $12/hr) for completing the 6-minute study. To examine predictions regarding the relationship between zero-sum mindset and hostile attributions, the sample must have sufficient variability in zero-sum mindsets (in US Prolific Samples, means are usually right-skewed). To help mitigate this, we plan to prescreen participants to ensure adequate representation of demographic criteria known to be associated with a stronger zero-sum mindset (male, conservative) and which are underrepresented in Prolific samples.

Sample size

Target sample size is 1000 participants. Participants who fail a straightforward attention check will be excluded from analysis.

Sample size rationale

This sample size is powered to detect an effect as small as d = .18 in a comparison of means (two-tailed independent samples t-test with alpha = .05 at 80% power), and a Cohen’s f as small as .10, in a in a multiple regression with two predictors (alpha = .05 at 80% power), and as small as f = .09, in a one-tailed test of the interaction between zero-sum mindset and condition.

Stopping rule

Data collection stops automatically when the Prolific target of 1000 completes has been met (Note: Participants will not be blocked from completing the study and receiving compensation because of a failed attention check, but will be excluded from subsequent analysis.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Manipulated variables In this experiment, we manipulate the structure of success in four competitive scenarios so that they take either a zero-sum structure or a non-zero-sum structure. Participants assigned to the zero-sum condition read about zero-sum structures for success (see below) and participants assigned to the non-zero-sum condition read about non-zero-sum structures. All other text and measures are identical across conditions. Example: Marathon Scenario Zero-Sum Structure: "You are a competitive marathon runner hoping to qualify for a prestigious race. In order to qualify for this prestigious race, you must first finish the pre-race (the qualifying race) among the top 3. After a long, hard run you are nearing the finish line of the qualifying race. Looking ahead you can see that you are currently in fourth place with another runner just ahead of you. To qualify you just need to pass them." Non-Zero-Sum Structure: "You are a competitive marathon runner hoping to qualify for a prestigious race. In order to qualify for this prestigious race you must first finish the pre-race (the qualifying race) under a certain time. All runners who can finish under this time will qualify. After a long, hard run you are nearing the finish line of the qualifying race. Looking at your watch you can see that you are almost on pace to make it in time with another runner just ahead of you. To qualify you just need to pick up your pace a little bit.”

Measured variables

Manipulation Check (Zero-Sum Beliefs About the Situation): Each situation is followed by two items that measure zero-sum beliefs about the situations just described (e.g., "In this scenario, in order for the other runner to win, I have to lose.") rated on a scale from 1 - Strongly disagree to 7 - Strongly agree, for a total of 8-items. Hostile Attributions: After reading each description, participants rated their agreement with a hostile interpretation of the other competitor’s behavior (e.g., “I think they spilled coffee on me on purpose to hurt my chances for the job”) as well as a reverse-scored benign attribution (e.g., “I think it was an accident. They probably didn’t mean to spill coffee on me.”) for a total of eight items rated on a scale from 1- Strongly disagree to 7 - Strongly agree. Retaliation Intentions: After each scenario, participants indicate whether they would try to retaliate (“If I had the opportunity, I would try to retaliate in some way”) on a scale from 1- Strongly disagree to 7 - Strongly agree. The same item is measured after each vignette for a total of four items. Zero-Sum Mindset: Zero-sum mindset will be measured using the seven-item Likert-style scale (e.g., "Life is such that when one person gains, someone else has to lose.") with responses rated on a scale from 1 - Strongly disagree to 7 - Strongly agree. Demographic Variables. Participants also report their age, race, sex, income, and education. See attachment for all item text in the study export.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Before we test our main hypotheses, we will first examine whether the scenarios describing zero-sum competitions do actually promote zero-sum beliefs about those competitive situations (manipulation check) by comparing mean zero-sum belief ratings. Comparing these means in an independent samples t-test, we expect to find stronger zero-sum beliefs in the zero-sum condition than in the non-zero-sum condition. Next we test our main hypotheses. Hypothesis 1: Zero-sum configurations of success increase hostile attributions. We test this hypothesis using an independent samples t-test comparing mean levels of hostile attribution bias between conditions (expecting higher mean hostile attributions in the zero-sum condition). Hypothesis 2: Zero-sum mindset predicts stronger hostile attributions (even in objectively non-zero-sum situations). We primarily test this hypothesis using multiple regression analyses to predict hostile attribution bias from zero-sum mindset when controlling for the effect of condition. We hypothesize that stronger zero-sum mindset will predict stronger hostile attribution bias even when controlling for the effect of the objective situations (i.e., the experimental condition). As a secondary test of this hypothesis, we will also examine whether zero-sum mindset moderates the effect of the "objective situation" on hostile attributions by examining the interaction of zero-sum mindset and condition. We predict that when zero-sum mindset is stronger, the effect of the "objective situation" (i.e., the expected negative effect of a non-zero-sum configurations on hostile attributions) will be weaker (i.e., a negative interaction term coefficient when zero-sum condition is the reference level). However, we also note that the effect-size of this moderation observed in an earlier study was relatively small compared to the main effect of zero-sum mindset and main effect of condition on hostile attributions, and therefore, may require a larger sample size than that which is available for this study to detect at the power and significance thresholds applied to our main hypothesis tests. Given these constraints and the directional nature of the hypothesis, this secondary hypothesis will be tested using a one-tailed test. Hypothesis 3: Zero-sum mindset predicts greater intention to retaliate against the perceived hostility across situations, including in objectively non-zero-sum situations. We test this hypothesis using multiple regression analyses to predict retaliation intentions from zero-sum mindset when controlling for the effect of condition. We hypothesize that a stronger zero-sum mindset will predict stronger hostile attribution bias even when controlling for the effect of the objective situations (i.e., the experimental condition). Transformations

Reverse-key Likert items will be recoded before computing mean scores. Since all likert questions are rated on a scale from 1 to 7, they will be recoded by subtracting the raw score from 8 (e.g. 8 - 7 = 1; 8 - 1 = 7; 8 - 4 = 4) Continuous variables in multiple regressions will be standardized to provide interpretable beta coefficients.

Inference criteria

All main hypothesis tests will be two-tailed with a significance threshold of p-value &lt; .05. p-values between .05 -.10 will be considered marginal, and p-values above .10 as non-significant. Given the aforementioned power limitations for detecting small interaction effects, for secondary tests of the interaction between zero-sum mindset and condition, we plan to test a directional hypothesis implementing a one-tailed test (interaction term p &lt; .10).

Data exclusion

Participants who fail an attention check will be excluded from analysis.

Exploratory analysis

We have robust evidence that zero-sum mindset predicts zero-sum thinking and domain-specific zero-sum beliefs across a variety of domains (e.g., zero-sum beliefs about the job market in general). However, in this study we can also observe (in the non-zero-sum condition) potential zero-sum thinking in a situation that has been explicitly described as non-zero-sum. Presumably the explicit descriptions should leave far less room for zero-sum interpretation compared other studies examining zero-sum beliefs where no such explicit descriptions are provided. However, despite this reduced scope for interpretation, we may still find that even in this explicitly non-zero-sum situation, those with strong zero-sum mindsets are more likely to tether their beliefs about success in the situation to their internal model (zero-sum mindset) rather than to descriptions of the external situation provided. So, in addition to our manipulation check test (the main effect of condition on situationally-specific zero-sum beliefs), we will also examine whether people with a zero-sum mindset have stronger situationally-specific zero-sum beliefs even when those situations are explicitly described as non-zero-sum. Therefore, we will examine whether zero-sum mindset predicts situationally-specific zero-sum beliefs in two ways: By examining only participants in the non-zero-sum condition, we examine whether a stronger zero-sum mindset will predict stronger situationally-specific zero-sum beliefs. And in the full sample, we examine the interaction between zero-sum mindset and condition predicting situationally-specific zero-sum beliefs. Note: This hypothesis is exploratory because a significant relationship between zero-sum mindset and the manipulation check variable is not essential to the main hypothesis that zero-sum mindset predicts hostile attributions (even in non-zero-sum situations). This is because we have reason to expect these tendencies of interpretation (hostile bias) and response (retaliation) to be generally habitualized (see study examining implicit hostile bias). That is, even if a situation is “strong” enough to potentially attenuate situationally-specific zero-sum beliefs for those with a zero-sum mindset, the responses that follow from the mindset may be automatically activated without being sufficiently influenced by one’s updated situational beliefs. Finally, as in the previous study, we will also compare willingness to retaliate between conditions, and will examine the interaction between zero-sum mindset and condition when predicting willingness to retaliate.
