The Date/Delay Effect in Intertemporal Choice: An Eye-Tracking Study

OSF ID: a3u54 | Registered: 2021-12-06T10:53:46.764669

Study Information

Hypotheses

(1) We expect to replicate the date/delay effect in intertemporal choice (LeBoeuf, 2006; Read et al., 2005) in a within-subject design (also see Dshemuchadse et al., 2013). Specifically, we hypothesize that a version of the MCQ in which time until the receipt of smaller-immediate or larger-later reward is framed as a date (“date condition”, e.g., on May 31, 2022) will lead to lower discount rates k (i.e., to less discounting of the smaller-immediate reward) than a version of the MCQ in which time is framed as a delay (“delay condition”, e.g., in 176 days). (2) We expect that attentional mechanisms will differ between the date condition and the delay condition: (2a) We expect that, due to higher cognitive demands, the date condition will lead to more deliberation than the delay condition (cf. Dshemuchadse et al., 2013). Specifically, we hypothesize that in the date condition, participants will show (i) greater response times, (ii) a higher total number of saccades, and (iii) a larger total number of fixations compared to the delay condition. (2b) We hypothesize that in the date condition, participants will more strongly follow a comparative search strategy (rather than an integrative one) as compared to the delay condition, ultimately leading to less temporal discounting (cf. Amasino et al., 2019; Fisher, 2021; Reeck et al., 2017). This is thought to be reflected by relatively more attribute-wise (horizontal) than option-wise (vertical) eye movements during the choice process, i.e., we expect that the proportion of attribute-wise, horizontal transitions (relative to option-wise, vertical saccades) in the date condition will be higher than in the delay condition (diagonal transitions, i.e., rewardA-to-delayB-, rewardB-to-delayA-, delayA-to-rewardB-, and delayB-to-rewardA-saccades, as well as other saccades not linking the attributes excluded) (cf. Arieli et al., 2011; Kurokawa &amp; Ohtake, 2013). (2c) Additionally, we expect that the date condition, as compared to the delay condition, will lead to shifts in attentional focus (monetary reward attributes vs. time attributes). Since the attention-focusing hypothesis (Read et al., 2005) and the hypothesis of stronger deliberation in the date condition (Dshemuchadse et al., 2013) imply different predictions (attentional hypothesis: stronger focus on monetary reward attributes; deliberation hypothesis: stronger focus on time attributes), we formulate an undirected hypothesis: We hypothesize that attentional focus will shift towards either monetary reward attributes or time attributes in the date condition (as compared to the delay condition). More specifically, we expect this to be represented by longer fixation durations either on reward attributes or on time attributes in the date condition, as compared to the delay condition. (3) We hypothesize that eye movement data (transitions and/or fixation durations) are correlated with (and thus might explain) interindividual differences in the date/delay effect.

Study design

The study will use a within-subject design. Specifically, participants will complete two versions of the MCQ in two separate blocks (delay vs. date condition; within-subject design with one factor and two conditions). In one version, time until receipt of rewards will be framed as dates (e.g., 22 Euro on December 6, 2021 [= today] vs. 25 Euro on April 21, 2022). In the other condition, it will be presented as delays (e.g., 22 Euro in 0 days vs. 25 Euro in 136 days). Participants will give answers to both versions of the questionnaire (54 choices in total). After each version, they will be asked questions regarding their perception of the task and their feelings during completion (mental demand, frustration, fatigue, worry; items from Hart &amp; Staveland, 1988; Hockey, 1997). Additionally, participants will complete a questionnaire including demographic data (gender, handedness, age, and education), a German version of the UPPS-P (Lynam et al., 2006; translation of the UPPS-subscales from Schmidt et al., 2008, translation of the Positive Urgency subscale by the research group of the authors from the University of Bonn), and a German version of the ZTPI (Reuschenbach et al., 2013). For further details, please see the information below as well as code and examples attached to this preregistration.

Randomization

Presentation order of the version blocks of the MCQ will be counterbalanced. Additionally, in each block, the 27 trials of the MCQ will be presented in random order. In each trial, all attributes (two rewards, two delays) will be separated on the screen to ensure clear separation of eye movements. Rewards will always be presented on the top left and top right, the corresponding delays on the lower left and lower right of the screen (e.g., option A reward: top left; option A delay: lower left; option B reward: top right; option B delay: lower right). The positions of the smaller-immediate and larger-later options on the left and right side of the screen will be counterbalanced between trials (positions will be random for trials within a condition but same for corresponding trials across conditions; positions will be the same for each participant, as they will be fixed before the start of the study).

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

The inclusion criteria for the study are as follows: • Age 18–40 years • Right-handed* • Non-smoker* • Normal or corrected vision (glasses or contact lenses) • No current physical or mental illness (acute) • Not taking any medication (exceptions: Contraceptives, nutritional supplements, thyroid medication). * If these exclusion criteria considerably complicate recruitment, preventing the data collection to be finished in a reasonable amount of time (i.e., if data collection is not finished until 31/03/2022), left-handed people and smokers will also be allowed to participate. As the study will be conducted in an oculomotor laboratory of the Department of Psychology of the University of Bonn, participants will mainly be students from the University of Bonn. The study will be advertised through personal contacts as well as mailing lists, online forums, etc. Psychology students from the University of Bonn will be rewarded with hourly credit for their studies. Additionally, all participants will enter a lottery in which three of the participants will be randomly selected and paid according to the amount and delay of one choice they made in the experiment (minimum 11 Euro, maximum 85 Euro). The choice will be randomly selected.

Sample size

Based on our power analysis (see below), we will aim for a sample size of N = 54 participants in total, who fully completed the study and did not have to be excluded for any reasons (e.g., not meeting the inclusion criteria specified above, technical errors, etc.).

Sample size rationale

We conducted a power analysis with G*Power (Faul et al., 2009) to determine the sample size needed to replicate the date/delay effect (within-subject t-test), given an effect size of dRM = 0.45 (cf. Hedge’s g according to Rung &amp; Madden, 2018), an alpha level of .05 (two-tailed), and a power of 90%. The analysis yielded a target sample size of 54 participants.

Stopping rule

Generally, we will aim for a sample size of 54 participants. As soon as this target number will be reached, data collection will be stopped. However, if data collection is not finished until 31/03/2022, we will also allow smokers and left-handers (see inclusion criteria above) to participate in order to obtain a sufficiently large sample.

Measured variables

At the beginning of the study, participants will be given information about our study and will have to provide informed consent. After creating a pseudonymous code to ensure a correct matching of data and successfully calibrating the eye tracker, participants will be presented with the following tasks/questionnaires (in order of presentation): 1. Experiment (see tictac_20211129.ebz): a. German version of the MCQ (Kirby et al., 1999), conducted in two versions (date vs. delay condition); choice behavior (choices, reaction times) as well as eye movement data (i.e., saccades, fixations, velocities, etc.) will be acquired b. After each version of the MCQ (Kirby et al., 1999), participants will be asked to answer four questions about their performance. More specifically, they will be asked about the amount of mental demand required and the frustration level felt during the task (from NASA task load index [NASA-TXL], German version; Hart &amp; Staveland, 1988) as well as about fatigue and worry felt during the task (from Hockey Scales in German version; Hockey, 1997). Questions will be presented as continuous scale ranging from “very low [...]” to “very high [...]” (with twenty visual separators; see items_nasa-txl_hockey_scales.pdf) 2. Questionnaire (without eye-tracking) involving the following subjects (see survey.tictac.xml): a. Demographic data. This involves questions about gender (male, female, diverse), handedness (left, right; as control of inclusion criteria), age (in years), and education (German equivalents according to the International Standard Classification of Education [United Nations Educational Scientific and Cultural Organization Institute for Statistics, 2012], i.e.: Early childhood education [e.g., pre-primary education], Primary education [e.g., primary schools], Lower secondary education [e.g., grades 5-9/10 in special needs, grammar or comprehensive schools], Upper secondary education [e.g., grades 10-13 in grammar or comprehensive schools], Post-secondary non-tertiary education [e.g., evening grammar schools, adult education colleges], Short-cycle tertiary education [e.g., Master craftsman training with &lt; 880 h preparation], Bachelor’s or equivalent level [e.g., Bachelor's degree at universities, Master craftsman training with ≥ 880 h preparation], Master’s or equivalent level [e.g., Master's or diploma degree at universities], Doctoral degree, No other classification [e.g., mentally handicapped pupils]) b. German version of the UPPS-P (Lynam et al., 2006; translation of the UPPS-subscales from Schmidt et al., 2008, translation of the Positive Urgency subscale by the working group of the authors); 59 items on a five-point Likert scale (cf. Kämpfe &amp; Mitte, 2009) c. German version of the ZTPI (Reuschenbach et al., 2013); 56 items on a five-point Likert scale

Indices

Discount rates: We will estimate different discount functions based on the complete decision making data using hierarchical Bayesian estimations (e. g., Vincent, 2016). Specifically, we will do estimations assuming (a) an exponential, (b) a hyperbolic, and (c) a quasi-hyperbolic discounting function. To determine which function has the best fit, we will compare the models based on the Bayes factor. For analyses, we will estimate the date/delay effect based on the function with the best fit (date premium; see below) and do further analyses with the estimates of the discount rate and the date premium. Eye-tracking data: To obtain measures of comparative vs. integrative search strategies, summarized over each condition, we will compute the mean value of horizontal and vertical eye movements between areas of interest (i.e., changes of the overt visual attention on areas of interest) per trial in each condition (diagonal eye movements excluded, as they form part of both strategies). Areas of interest are defined rectangular areas around the different attributes. Additionally, we will compute measures of relative attention to reward vs. time attributes: First, we will check for differences in blink rates between conditions (i.e., we will compare the number of blinks per second between conditions with a within-subject t-test). Depending on the result, we will calculate indices for eye-movement data: If the blinking comparison is not significant, we will compute mean values of fixation duration on the reward attributes and time attributes, respectively, per trial in each condition. If the blinking comparison is significant, we will calculate mean values of fixation duration correcting for blinks (i.e., examine trials subtracting the duration of blinks). Questionnaires: For each of the five UPPS-P subscales (Negative Urgency, Lack of Premeditation, Lack of Perseverance, Sensation Seeking, Positive Urgency) as well as of the ZTPI subscales (Past-Negative, Past-Positive, Pres-Hedonistic, Present-Fatalistic, Future), a sum score will be calculated.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

Generally, we will present a table displaying the (Pearson) correlations between all relevant measures. For the hypotheses, we will conduct the following statistical analyses: Hypothesis 1: To test whether the date/delay effect is replicated, we will estimate a discount rate of the delay version (MCQdelay-k) and estimate a date premium (MCQdate-k = MCQdelay-k + date-premium). The posterior distribution for the date premium will be used to determine if (and with which probability) the effect is different from zero, both on an individual- and a group-level. For completeness, we will also carry out a within-subject t-test (two-tailed) of the two point estimates of the mean discount rates in the two conditions (see power-analysis). Hypothesis 2: (a) To test whether (i) response times, (ii) number of saccades, and (iii) number of fixations differ between conditions, we will conduct three separate within-subject t-tests. Due to multiple testing, the p-value of the t-tests will be Bonferroni-Holm corrected. (b) To test whether the frequency of saccades into horizontal vs. vertical directions differs between conditions, we will conduct a 2 × 2 within subject ANOVA (factor 1: date vs. delay; factor 2: horizontal vs. vertical direction). We expect this analysis to yield a significant interaction effect, revealing more horizontal saccades than vertical saccades, with this difference being stronger in the date than in the delay condition. If significant, this interaction effect will be visually inspected in a plot and tested with Bonferroni-Holm corrected post-hoc within-subject t-tests. (c) To test whether the date condition shifts the attentional focus (i.e., fixation durations) between monetary reward attributes vs. time attributes, we will conduct a second 2 × 2 within subject ANOVA (factor 1: date vs. delay; factor 2: reward attributes vs. time attributes; dependent variable: fixation durations in ms). We expect this analysis to yield a significant interaction effect, either revealing longer fixation durations on reward attributes relative to time attributes or longer fixation durations on time attributes relative to reward attributes in the date condition (as compared to the delay condition). If significant, this interaction effect will be visually inspected in a plot and tested with Bonferroni-Holm corrected post-hoc within-subject t-tests. Hypothesis 3: To test hypothesis 3, we will use the individual date premium (see above) as estimate of the date/delay effect. The date premiums will be regressed on the difference of the proportion of horizontal saccades (in all horizontal and vertical saccades, i. e.: prop_hor = hor / [hor + ver]) in the two conditions (diff_prop_hor_DDE = prop_hor_date – prop_hor_delay) and on the proportion of fixation durations on reward attributes (relative to fixation duration on reward and time attributes, i. e.: prop_ fixdur_rew = fixdur_rew / [fixdur_rew + fixdur_time]) in the two conditions (diff_ fixdur_rew_DDE = prop_ fixdur_rew_date – prop_ fixdur_rew_delay). We expect that diff_prop_hor_DDE will positively predict the date/delay effect and diff_fixdur_rew_DDE will either positively or negatively predict the date/delay effect.

Transformations

As the literature shows that MCQ-k typically is highly skewed (e.g., Kirby et al., 1999), we will use a log-transformation to transformed discount rates k and conduct the analyses with these transformed values where appropriate.

Inference criteria

Generally, p-values will be compared to an alpha error rate of .05 (two-tailed). Whenever necessary, we will report p-values corrected for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni-Holm correction; see, e.g., hypothesis 2a).

Data exclusion

Only participants with complete data (i.e., experiment with choice and usable eye-tracking data for all choices, complete questionnaire data) will be considered for analysis. Specifically, if participants have completed all tasks but eye-tacking data is not usable for analysis, they will be excluded. All participants for whom the inclusion criteria (see above) do not apply will be excluded. Additional exclusions depend on task performance: Specifically, (i) participants whose mean response times in the intertemporal choice tasks (summarized over all trials) deviate at least 3 SD from the sample mean, as well as (ii) participants for whom an estimation of discount rates k is not possible, will be excluded. Furthermore, trials with response times below 300 ms or above 20 seconds will be excluded, as theses outliers suggest that either no decision process was initiated or the decision process was somehow disrupted. If (other) outliers occur, we will run all analyses with and without outliers.

Missing data

As participants are not allowed to proceed without giving an answer to the questions displayed, missing data cannot occur. If it occurs due to technical errors and is essential for the specified analyses, participants will be excluded.

Exploratory analysis

Associations of the date/delay effect with personality measures: In exploratory analyses, we will investigate whether temporal discounting and, more specifically, the date/delay effect covary with personality measures of impulsivity and time perspective (Pearson correlations between log-transformed discount rates of each version and the measure of the date/delay effect on the one hand with the different subscale scores of the UPPS-P and ZTPI on the other). As associations between personality and temporal discounting measures tend to be weak (Cyders &amp; Coskunpinar, 2011; MacKillop et al., 2016), we suspect to find – if any – effects of rather small size, revealing relationships between the date/delay effect and impulsivity (e.g., positive correlation with the lack of premeditation subscale; cf. Klapproth, 2012, who found stronger effects of the manipulation in individuals with substance use disorder than in controls) or future time perspective (e.g., negative correlation with the future subscale). Thus, more impulsive and less future-oriented individuals could profit more strongly from the date/delay manipulation. Response times in the date/delay effect: As additional exploratory analysis, we will investigate the response times in the two conditions more thoroughly. While generally, shorter response times in intertemporal choice tasks seem to be associated with stronger comparative search strategies (Reeck et al., 2017) and more patient decision making (Amasino et al., 2019), the date condition is, perhaps counterintuitively (as it is associated with a more comparative strategy and less discounting behavior), expected to yield higher response times than the delay condition, due to more deliberation (potential higher cognitive demands to compare dates; see hypothesis 2a). Consistently, higher difficulties have also been associated with stronger comparative search strategies (Arieli et al., 2011). Thus, between-subjects, response times are expected to be shorter when individuals show stronger comparative strategies and lower discount rates (Pearson correlations between response times, proportion measures of comparative strategy, and discount rates; separated for each condition). Within-subjects, however, the date condition is expected to lead to higher response times than the delay condition (within-subject t-test), which could be interpreted as evidence for higher cognitive demands of the date condition in general (ultimately leading to more comparative search and less discounting within-subject). This assumption will also explicitly be tested by comparing the self-reported mental demand during the task versions. Additionally, frustration, fatigue and worry during the task versions will be explored. Pupil dilatation: In line with our expectations of higher cognitive demand in the date compared to the delay condition, we will also explore whether the two conditions differ in terms of pupil dilatation. Specifically, given that pupil dilatation has been shown to reflect cognitive effort (Goldwater, 1972; van der Wel &amp; van Steenbergen, 2018), the date condition is expected to lead to larger pupil diameters than the delay condition. Vigor of saccades before decisions: Reppert et al. (2015) found that vigor (saccadic peak velocity) was similar for saccades made towards the two intertemporal choice options during most of the choice process. However, around 0.5 seconds before decision, it became greater for saccades towards the preferred option. Thus, in our study as well, vigor of saccades ~0.5 s or less before decision should be higher when directed towards the preferred option than when directed towards the unpreferred option (in both conditions). Given that difference in vigor is associated with the difference in subjective value of two options (Reppert et al., 2015) and assuming that the subjective value of larger-later options in the date condition might be moderately higher than in the delay condition, this vigor effect could be particularly strong in the date condition (in trials in which the larger-later reward is chosen). We will explore this assumption in our study.
