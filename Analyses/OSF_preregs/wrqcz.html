Assessing Crossmodal Intensity Processing with a Speeded Discrimination Task - Replication of Marks (1987)

OSF ID: wrqcz | Registered: 2021-06-13T12:49:58.378104

Study Information

Hypotheses

Based on the findings of Marks (1987), we conjecture the following: (1) Human performance in the brightness classification task should depend upon whether there is a match or a mismatch between the visual target brightness and auditory distractor loudness. That is to say, responses to a bright (dim) visual target should on average be faster and more accurate when the simultaneously presented auditory distractor is loud (soft) than when it is soft (loud). This hypothesis will be tested with two ANOVAs performed separately on the mean response time (RT) and response accuracy data from the brightness classification task. This crossmodal correspondence between brightness and loudness should be reflected in both of the ANOVAs as a statistically reliable interaction effect between the two nominal predictors “brightness” and “loudness”. (2) Performance in the lightness classification task should on the other hand be independent of the intensity pairing between the visual target and the distractor tone. We therefore expect no Lightness × Loudness interaction in the analysis of the lightness classification data. Testing this hypothesis will involve two separate ANOVAs to be performed on the mean RT and error rates from the lightness classification. (3) As implied by hypotheses (1) and (2), joint ANOVAs conducted on the combined data from the lightness and brightness classification tasks should further reveal Mode × Visual Intensity × Auditory Intensity interaction effects for both the speed and accuracy of performance. That is, there should be a statistically reliable three-way interaction effect between the mode of visual appearance (brightness/lightness), the intensity of the visual signal and the loudness of the auditory distractor on both RT and accuracy of performance. (4) A within-subjects t-test will be performed on RT and accuracy rates from matching vs mismatching trials types to confirm that the crossmodal correspondence interaction effects are in the expected direction (i.e., faster and more accurate performance on matching type trials).

Study design

Three independent variables will be orthogonally varied in a fully factorial design, namely the intensity of the visual target, the loudness of the auditory distractor, and the manipulated mode of appearance of the visual target (i.e., the type of classification to be performed; either brightness or lightness). By and large, the study aims to follow the protocol employed by Marks (1987) as closely as possible. Each trial will begin with the presentation of a fixation cross at the center of the screen for 500 milliseconds to indicate the start of a new trial. Subsequently, a blank screen will be displayed for a variable foreperiod. This foreperiod will be composed of a constant latency of 1 second plus a variable latency component sampled from an exponential distribution with rate λ = 1. Thereafter, an imperative visual stimulus measuring 1.2 cm wide × 1.6 cm tall will be presented at the center of the screen. Concurrent with the presentation of this visual target, an accessory auditory stimulus will be delivered via loudspeakers. Participants will be tasked to classify the intensity of the visual target as either ‘weak’ or ‘strong’ by manually pressing one of two possible response keys. The presentation of both stimuli will end as soon as it is registered that the subject has pressed either response button. If the response was incorrect, or if the response time exceeded 1000 milliseconds, the subject will receive visual feedback saying that the response was incorrect or too slow (this feedback message will remain on the screen for 1 second before disappearing). In the brightness classification task, the visual target will be either dim (80 cd/m2) or bright (320 cd/m2) presented against a dark (0 cd/m2) background. In the lightness classification task, the role between background and dim targets will merely be switched, such that a target that is either dark (0 cd/m2) or light (320 cd/m2) is presented against a background which is middle gray (80 cd/m2). In both the brightness and the lightness classification task, the auditory distractor will be a sawtooth wave presented at either a soft 53 dB(A) or at a loud 80 dB(A) volume. Within every block, each of the four possible stimulus combinations belonging to the currently ongoing type of classification task will be repeated 24 (6) times per experimental (practice) block. The resulting 96 (24) trials per experimental (practice) block will be presented in random order. Two introductory practice blocks and eight experimental blocks will be cycled through for every participant, yielding a total sum of 48 practice trials and 768 experimental trials. If response time on a given trial exceeds 1 second, then this trial will be repeated by inserting it randomly into the further course of trials within the same block. Hence, the actual number of trials in a block may be higher, depending on the subject’s tendency to respond rapidly enough. After completion of all eight experimental blocks, participants will be asked to match the auditory and visual stimulus intensities according to their perceived similarity. First, they should match loudness and lightness levels by indicating whether they perceive the soft (loud) sound as being more similar to the dark (light) visual stimulus or to the light (dark) visual stimulus. Then, the same procedure will be repeated for matching the loudness and brightness levels.

Randomization

All three independent variables will be varied within-subjects; loudness and visual intensity within-blocks, and the mode of visual appearance between-blocks. Randomization of trial types and blocks is realized through a computerized pseudo-random number generator. See the study design above for a more elaborated description.

Data collection procedures

Subjects will mainly be recruited from the undergraduate student pool at the department of psychology at University of Tübingen. Recruitment will occur via internal emails, posters, and word of mouth. All subjects will be reimbursed for their participation in a single one-hour session, either monetarily with 8€ or with mandatory course credit. The only inclusion criteria will be german language proficiency, being above 18 years of age and possessing normal or corrected-to-normal vision and hearing. The experiment will be conducted in a sound- and light-attenuated booth.

Sample size

A sample size of N = 20 participants should suffice for 95% power for all confirmatory tests. Ten extra participants will be added to this figure for the sake of good error margins (e.g., in the case that some data has to be excluded due to poor overall accuracy) and because our testing resources allow it. This yields a total sample size of N = 30 participants which will be the stopping rule for data collection.

Sample size rationale

The stated target sample size was reached by conducting an a priori power analysis based on iterative Monte Carlo simulation. Statistical power was calculated by simulating and fitting mean RT data to the confirmatory ANOVA models 10,000 times per incremental step of sample size. The objective of the simulation was to determine the sample size necessary to achieve 95% frequentist power to reject the null hypothesis stating 1) no three-way Mode × Loudness × Visual Intensity interaction in the joint RT ANOVA, and 2) no two-way Loudness × Brightness interaction in the brightness RT ANOVA. The simulation was informed by extensive pilot testing of the experimental paradigm.

Stopping rule

N = 30 participants.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Three independent variables will be manipulated, namely (I) the intensity of the visual target (either low or high), (II) the intensity of the auditory distractor (either low or high), and (III) the mode of appearance of the visual stimulus (either dim/bright or dark/light).

Measured variables

The two dependent variables are (I) response time (the time interval from stimulus onset to the registration of the subject’s response) and (II) response accuracy (correct or incorrect).

Transformations

Inference criteria

The alpha threshold of statistical significance will be p &lt; .05 for all confirmatory tests.

Data exclusion

Participants exhibiting &lt; 85% overall accuracy of performance will be excluded from all confirmatory analyses. Response times shorter than 200 milliseconds will be deemed anticipatory, and responses slower than 1,000 milliseconds will be considered attentional lapses. These individual outlier responses (anticipations and lapses) will be disregarded from the confirmatory analysis of error rates and mean RT. Incorrect responses will further also be discarded from the analysis of mean RTs.

Missing data

If a subject does not complete the entire session of the experiment, the data collected from that subject will be discarded from all analyses and that subject will be replaced by a new subject.

Exploratory analysis

(1) Standardized effect sizes for the crossmodal mapping manipulation on mean RT and error rates will be computed by taking the within-subjects difference scores for congruent and incongruent type trials, and then bootstrap estimating the associated confidence intervals for the Hedges' g effect size index for repeated measures deisgn. This within-subjects contrast will also involve a t-test conducted on the difference between congruent and incongruent mean RT. (2) Graphical devices such as delta plots (De Jong, Liang, &amp; Lauber, 1994) or shift functions (Rousselet, Pernet, &amp; Wilcox, 2017) might be utilized to gain a more fine-grained picture of the RT distributions. (3) Potential speed-accuracy trade-offs might be explored by regressing mean RT for each participant against their accuracy score. (4) Supplementary analyses might be performed by recoding the congruency mapping between visual and auditory stimuli in accordance with each participant’s perceived similarity ratings provided during the end of the experiment. (5) More elaborate stochastic modeling of the latent cognitive mechanisms behind the loudness-brightness crossmodal correspondence might also be conducted.
