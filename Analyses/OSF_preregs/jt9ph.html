The relation between WM capacity and the temporal structure of events in episodic memory: An individual differences approach.

OSF ID: jt9ph | Registered: 2021-03-29T14:47:28.153509

Study Information

Hypotheses

Our hypotheses focus on three measures of interest: -temporal compression rates (i.e., the ratio between the original duration of the video and the time taken by the subject to mentally replay it). -the number of recalled actions (a proxy of remembered experience units). -estimates of the time needed to remember an action (i.e., the total duration of memory replay divided by the number of recalled actions). If the formation of experience units constituting the memory representation of events is facilitated by the perception of EBs, subjects should recall more actions for videos containing many EBs compared to videos containing few EBs. TCE should be negatively associated with the number of actions recalled and thus be lower for videos with a high density of EBs compared to videos with a low density of EBs. We expect that the formation and retrieval of experience units rely (at least in part) on WM processes, and thus are dependent on WM capacity. Therefore, we hypothesize that WM capacity is positively associated with the number of recalled actions. Regarding the relation between WM capacity and TCE, we formulate two hypotheses: (1) our main hypothesis is that a higher WM capacity would enable the remembering of many experience units, leading to longer mental replay durations and thus lower TCE rates. (2) nevertheless, it is possible that people with better WM abilities are faster at retrieving EUs in episodic memory (which could reduce the time needed to remember videos and thus lead to higher TCE rates). To disentangle these two hypotheses, we will not only focus on the relation between WM capacity and TCE (i.e., the ratio between video duration and the duration of its mental replay), but also on estimates of the time needed to remember an action (i.e., the total duration of memory replay divided by the number of recalled actions). If hypothesis 2 is correct, this should be reflected in a negative association between WM capacity and the time needed to remember an action. We also expect to observe an interaction between the density of EBs and WM capacity. In other words, we hypothesize that the magnitude of the difference between high and low EBs density videos (both in terms of TCE and recalled actions) should be positively associated with WM capacity. More specifically, in so far as EBs facilitate the creation of experience units through WM-driven processes, we hypothesize that the effect of EBs on our measures of interest (TCE and recalled actions) should be greater for individuals with higher WM capacity.

Study design

This study will include two independent variables, the global WM score of each subject and the segmentation density of videos (high density of EBs vs low density of EBs; manipulated within-subject). This experiment will take place in two parts (with a 30-minute break between them): Complex span tasks: Participants will have to complete three complex span tasks in the following order: (1) operation span (OSpan; for more detail, see Kane et al., 2004; Unsworth et al., 2005). (2) symmetry span (SymSpan; Kane et al., 2004; Unsworth et al., 2009). (3) rotation span (RotSpan; Harrison et al., 2013; Kane et al., 2004). The structure of these three tasks is similar. Participants are presented with a sequence of items that they have to remember (i.e., the storage part of the task). In addition, participants also have to perform a distraction task between the presentation of each item in the sequence (i.e., the processing part of the task). The number of to-be-remembered items vary unpredictably from trial to trial. The length of the sequences of to-be-remembered items varies between two and five or seven (depending on the task) (Foster et al., 2015). To reduce the time needed for participants to complete the study (and thus to limit tiredness and concentration losses), we will use the shortened complex span tasks developed by Foster et al. (2015). In these shortened versions, participants see each sequence length only once (instead of three times in the classical versions). This modification reduces the time needed to complete the three WM tasks by 28% without unduly affecting the psychometric qualities of the measures (for more detail on how these shortened versions were developed and their psychometric qualities, see (Foster et al., 2015). TCE task: This task will contain eight trials. Each trial will start with a fixation cross (3s) followed by a video. Participants will have to carefully watch this video. After that, they will have to mentally replay the video (the time needed to mentally replay the video will be measured by asking participants to press the spacebar to indicate the start and end of their mental replay) and then to describe, as accurately and precisely as possible, every action that came to their mind during their mental replay. The instructions are adapted from previous studies on TCE (Folville et al., 2020; Jeunehomme et al., 2020; Jeunehomme &amp; D’Argembeau, 2019, 2020). Eight 40-s videos will be used, with half of them including a high density of EBs and the other half a low density of EBs. These videos correspond to segments extracted from eight films commonly used in event segmentation studies (e.g., Bangert et al., 2019; Eisenberg &amp; Zacks, 2016; Kurby &amp; Zacks, 2011; Sargent et al., 2013; Zacks et al., 2011). Each of these movies shows a person performing a daily life activity (e.g., preparing breakfast, gardening, and so on). We edited our video stimuli following a procedure inspired by that of (Bangert et al., 2019). For each film, we used segmentation data from previous studies (Eisenberg &amp; Zacks, 2016; Kurby &amp; Zacks, 2011; Sargent et al., 2013) in order to compute the number of normative EBs for each possible temporal window of 40 s. For half of the films we extracted the 40-s interval containing the highest number of EBs, for the other half, the 40-s interval containing the lower number of EBs. Before starting the experimental trials, in order to familiarize them with the entire procedure, participants will see a tutorial video (filmed from a first person point of view) summarizing what they need to do in order to perform the task properly. Then they will have to perform one practice trial. References: Bangert, A. S., Kurby, C. A., &amp; Zacks, J. M. (2019). The influence of everyday events on prospective timing “in the moment”. Data collection procedures

Human subjects aged between 18 and 35 will be recruited through Prolific (https://www.prolific.co/) (Palan &amp; Schitter, 2018). To be eligible, participants must not be taking any medication that could affect their ability to concentrate or have a history of psychiatric, psychological or neurological disorders. Participants will receive monetary compensation in line with hourly rate recommended by Prolific (£7.50). Participants will receive an URL (on their prolific account) allowing them to take part in the experiment through Gorilla ((https://gorilla.sc/), a platform for running behavioral experiments online (Anwyl-Irvine et al., 2020). At the beginning of the experiment, participants will be asked to perform the task in a quiet and distraction-free environment and will be informed that they must complete all tasks (in their entirety) to receive the monetary compensation. The complex span tasks will be run on the E-Prime Go software (https://pstnet.com/eprime-go/)*. During the first part of the experiment, Gorilla will consecutively display three URLs on the screen. Each of them will allow the participants to download the E-Prime GO application to complete one of the complex span tasks. Once they have completed the three complex span tasks with E-Prime Go, participants will need to return to Gorilla to complete the TCE task and thus finish the experiment. * These tasks were originally developed on the E-Prime software (https://pstnet.com/products/e-prime/) by the Georgia Tech Attention and Working Memory Lab. They gently shared with us the E-Studio files necessary to create an “online” version of each task (through the E-Prime Go platform). E-Run files for the three tasks are available for download from their website (http://englelab.gatech.edu). References: Palan, S., &amp; Schitter, C. (2018). Sample size

Our target sample size is 78 participants. If a participant did not complete all tasks properly (see data exclusion), they will be replaced by another participant.

Sample size rationale

The sample size was estimated a priori using G*Power 3 (Faul et al., 2007) in order to have a statistical power of 0.90 (with an alpha of .05, two-tailed) to detect a significant relationship (with an effect size of r = 0.35; see Miller et al., 2019) between individual differences in WM capacity and our measures of interests (TCE rates and number of recalled actions). Measured variables

For each complex span task (for more detail, see Conway et al., 2005; Redick et al., 2012): -processing score: number of processing problems correctly answered. -processing accuracy errors: total number of errors made on the processing task. -processing speed errors: number of processing problems for which no response was given before the time limit. -storage absolute score: sum of items correctly recalled in the correct serial position (among trials for which all items were recalled correctly). -storage partial score: sum of items correctly recalled in the correct serial position (among all trials, regardless of whether all items of the trial were correctly recalled or not). TCE task: -the time needed to remember the videos. During the task, participants must press the “spacebar” to indicate the beginning and end of their mental replay. The time spent between these two presses will therefore correspond to the time taken by the person to mentally replay the video. -the number of remembered actions will be measured through the descriptions that participants will write. Participants’ scores on the three complex span tasks will be transformed into z-scores. Then, these three z-scores will be averaged. Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will analyze data using mixed-effects models. Fixed effects predictors will be WM composite scores, the density of EBs (high vs low), and their interaction. Three mixed-effects models will be run, with the following outcome variable: TCE rates, the number of recalled actions, estimation of the time needed to remember an action. We will also run another mixed-effects model evaluating if the number of recalled actions predict TCE (as shown in previous studies; Folville et al., 2020; Jeunehomme et al., 2020; Jeunehomme &amp; D’Argembeau, 2020) and will further explore whether this interacts with WM capacity. In two previous (unpublished) studies in which we used the same videos as in the present project, we observed that the distribution of temporal compression rates was substantially skewed. If this is the case, we will use robust statistical methods. References: Folville, A., Jeunehomme, O., Bastin, C., &amp; D’Argembeau, A. (2020). Data exclusion

Concerning complex span tasks: Participants whose processing accuracy is below 85% will be excluded from the analysis (Conway et al., 2005; Unsworth et al., 2005, 2009). Regarding the TCE task: -all experimental trials for which there is a technical issue with the video presentation (e.g., an abnormal duration of presentation) will be excluded from the analyses. - experimental trials with a time of mental replay less than 2 seconds will be excluded from the analyses (i.e., to remove trials in which response times are too fast to correspond to the mental replay of the video). -If more than half of trials of one type (high or low density of EBs) have to be excluded for a given participant, the entire participant will be excluded. - If a subject does not complete properly any of the four tasks (Ospan, Symspan, Rotspan, and TCE task), that subject will be excluded.
