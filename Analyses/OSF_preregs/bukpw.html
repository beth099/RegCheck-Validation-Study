Study Information

Title

Development of Global and Domain-Specific Self-Esteem in Adulthood

Authors

Katrin Rentzsch, Mareike B Müller, Michela Schröder-Abé

Description

In the present study, we want to investigate the longitudinal development and stability of global self-esteem and domain-specific self-esteem over a 6-year time span. The present investigation is based on a previous publication by Rentzsch and Schröder‐Abé (2018) in which we investigated stability and change in domain-specific self-esteem and global self-esteem by analyzing 2-year longitudinal data. Analyses using a latent variable approach revealed that the domains of self-esteem were relatively stable in terms of rank order and mean levels. In fact, the size of the stability coefficients was comparable to that of other trait measures that have been reported in the literature and paralleled the stability observed for global self-esteem. Our results did not provide support for either top-down or bottom-up effects between domain-specific and global self-esteem. The assessment took place in 2013 (Wave 1) and 2015 (Wave 2). In the present study, we want to investigate whether our previous results also hold when analyzing 6-year longitudinal data. At the time of this preregistration, the longitudinal data set includes data from 3 Waves (i.e. 2013, 2015, and 2017). In the following of this preregistration, we will field the 4th Wave (2019). We have written and uploaded this document to the Open Science Framework on July 17th 2019 before data collection of the 4th Wave started. The preregistration will be time‐stamped and fixed, so that any later amendments are identifiable as such. Our hypotheses include all 4 Waves. If the covariance coverage between waves is less than 0.10, then we will use data from the remaining waves only. Data from the 1st Wave have been analyzed and reported in Rentzsch and Gross (2015) and in Müller, Schütz, and Rentzsch (2019), and data from the 1st and the 2nd Wave have been analyzed with regard to self-esteem and reported in Rentzsch and Schröder‐Abé (2018). Data from the 3rd Wave have been collected prior to this preregistration, but they have not yet been analyzed except data on the self-regard subscale of the Multidimensional Self-Esteem Scale (MSES) and income.

Hypotheses

1. Stability of global and domain-specific self-esteem When comparing the stability of domain-specific vs. global conceptualizations of self-esteem, domain-specific self-esteem should be less stable than global self-esteem following the theoretical perspectives by Shavelson, Hubner, and Stanton (1976) or Brown and Marshall (2006). Other perspectives suggested that domain-specific self-esteem should be as stable as global self-esteem (Fleming &amp; Courtney, 1984; Janis &amp; Field, 1959). We will test those conflicting hypotheses against each other by examining rank-order stability of self-esteem. Differences in rank-order stability between measures of self-esteem are interpreted as relevant when the confidence intervals of the respective rank-order stability parameters do not overlap. If the perspective suggesting that domain-specific self-esteem is as stable as global self-esteem is true then domain-specific self-esteem should exhibit comparable trait variance as global self-esteem. When comparing unidimensional vs. multidimensional conceptualizations of global self-esteem, unidimensional global self-esteem should be less stable than multidimensional global self-esteem following the perspective by Shavelson et al. (1976), who predicted that subordinate factors of self-esteem such as emotional self-esteem would be less stable than the higher-order factor of global self-esteem. Differences in rank-order stability between unidimensional and multidimensional global self-esteem are interpreted as relevant when the confidence intervals of the respective rank-order stability parameters do not overlap. We expect that rank-order stability in global self-esteem and in domain-specific self-esteem will be smaller when considering larger retest-intervals. 2. Development of global and domain-specific self-esteem We will test conflicting hypotheses with regard to mean-level change building on the same theoretical perspectives as described above: Following Shavelson et al. (1976) or Brown and Marshall (2006), domain-specific self-esteem should be less stable in terms of mean-level change than global self-esteem. Following Fleming and Courtney (1984) or Janis and Field (1959), domain-specific self-esteem should be as stable as global self-esteem. Differences in mean-level change between measures of self-esteem are interpreted as relevant when the confidence intervals of the respective mean-level change parameters do not overlap. We expect that mean-level change in global self-esteem and in domain-specific self-esteem will be larger when considering larger retest-intervals. We expect that mean-level change in physical self-esteem over 6 years will be significantly different from zero. 3. Top-down vs. bottom-up When investigating bottom-up vs. top-down approaches on the influence between domain-specific and global self-esteem, level/change in domain-specific self-esteem should predict level/change in global self-esteem over time following the bottom-up approach (Shavelson et al., 1976) whereas level/change in global self-esteem should predict level/change in domain-specific self-esteem over time following the top-down approach (Brown, Dutton, &amp; Cook, 2001). In addition, following the bottom-up approach, state variance in domain-specific self-esteem should predict state variance in global self-esteem over time whereas state variance in global self-esteem should predict state variance in domain-specific self-esteem over time following the top-down approach.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

In order to investigate the stability of self-esteem, we will run latent longitudinal analyses based on confirmatory factor models. In order to investigate multidimensional global self-esteem, we will establish a second-order model consisting of six first-order factors representing each subscale of the MSES and a second-order factor reflecting multidimensional global self-esteem in accordance to Shavelson et al. (1976). In order to investigate unidimensional global self-esteem, we will establish a first-order model representing the latent factor of the self-regard subscale of the MSES. In order to investigate specific domains of self-esteem, we will establish first-order models for each of the domain-specific subscales of the MSES (i.e. social contact, social criticism, performance, physical appearance, physical ability). The planned analyses will include all 4 Waves. If the covariance coverage between waves is less than 0.10, then we will use data from the remaining waves only. Measurement invariance Every measurement model for each of the measures of self-esteem (i.e. multidimensional global self-esteem, unidimensional global self-esteem, and each of the domains of self-esteem) will be based on at least partial strong measurement invariance (Widaman, Ferrer, &amp; Conger, 2010) across the 4 time points. For each model, four latent factors are allowed to correlate: 1) the latent factor of self-esteem for the first measurement in 2013 (Time 1), the latent factor reflecting the second measurement in 2015 (Time 2), the latent factor from the third measurement in 2017 (Time 3) and the latent factor from the fourth measurement in 2019 (Time 4). We will use the same three manifest parcels as indicators for each latent factor of self-esteem as reported in Rentzsch and Schröder-Abé (2018). Furthermore, we will include correlated residuals to account for the use of the same indicators across time. Rank Order Stability In order to investigate rank-order stability, we will run latent regression analyses for each measure of self-esteem separately. All analyses are based on the respective measurement model. One set of analyses will include autoregressive effects between the latent self-esteem factors of neighboring time points (i.e. Time 2 – Time 1, Time 3 – Time 2, Time 4 – Time3). Another set of analyses will include autoregressive effects between the latent self-esteem factors at Time 4 and Time 1 (and Time 3 – Time 1) but no autoregressive effects between the other time points in order to investigate rank-order stability over 6 years (4 years). Latent rank-order stability will be computed as the standardized regression coefficient (the autoregressive effect). Mean Level Change In order to examine mean-level change, we will run True Intraindividual Change Models (TICM; Steyer, Eid, &amp; Schwenkmezger, 1997) for each measure of self-esteem separately. In these models, change is measured directly through latent difference variables. Latent difference variables represent individual differences in true intra-individual change over time corrected for random measurement error (Steyer et al., 1997). All TICM are based on the respective measurement model of each measure of self-esteem. One set of analyses will include latent difference variables between the latent self-esteem factors and the first time point (i.e. Time 2 – Time 1, Time 3 – Time 1, Time 4 – Time 1). All latent self-esteem factors load on an initial latent factor at Time 1. Every self-esteem factor later than Time 1 loads on a latent difference factor indicating latent change between Time 1 and the measurement point of the specific self-esteem factor. All loadings are fixed to one. The intercepts of the reference indicators are set to 0 to identify the latent means. The latent difference factors are allowed to correlate. The intercepts and variances of the latent self-esteem factors are set to 0 for purposes of identification. Latent mean-level change will be assessed as the mean of the latent difference factor. A significant latent difference factor mean indicates that the mean change between Time 1 and the other time point is significantly different from zero; a positive sign on the latent difference factor mean indicates that the latent self-esteem scores have increased between the respective time points. The variance of the latent difference factor reflects individual differences in the latent change scores, indicating inter-individual differences in intra-individual change. Another set of analyses will include latent difference variables between the latent self-esteem factors of neighboring time points (i.e. Time 2 – Time 1, Time 3 – Time 2, Time 4 – Time3). In case the TICM exhibits unacceptable model fit, we will use 1) Latent Growth Curve Models, 2) Dual Change Score Models (McArdle, 2009) or 3) Multilevel Growth Curve Models depending on model fit in the described order. Top-Down vs. Bottom-Up To analyze the effects of change in domain-specific self-esteem on change in global self-esteem (i.e. bottom-up) and the effects of change in global self-esteem on change in domain-specific self-esteem (i.e. top-down), we will simultaneously utilize two True Intraindividual Change Models (Steyer et al., 1997): one for the respective measure of global self-esteem and one for the respective measure of domain-specific self-esteem. Here, we use neighbor change models in which latent difference factors indicate change in self-esteem between neighboring time points (i.e. Time 2 – Time 1, Time 3 – Time 2, Time 4 – Time 3). The initial latent factors of domain-specific self-esteem and of global self-esteem at Time 1 are allowed to correlate with each other and they are allowed to correlate with both latent difference factors at Time 2. All latent difference factors are allowed to correlate with the other latent difference factor at the same time point and with the other latent difference factors at the subsequent time point. In addition, we will investigate whether domain-specific self-esteem at a previous time point predicts global self-esteem at a subsequent time-point (i.e. bottom-up), we will utilize latent regression analyses for each single predictor of domain-specific self-esteem and for each criterion of unidimensional and multidimensional global self-esteem separately. In order to investigate whether global self-esteem at a previous time point predicts domain-specific self-esteem at a subsequent time point (i.e. top-down), we will utilize latent regression analyses for each of the two global self-esteem factors and for each criterion of domain-specific self-esteem separately. It will be analyzed whether domain-specific self-esteem at a previous time point predicts mean-level change/ rank-order stability in global self-esteem at a later time-point (bottom-up). Furthermore, it will be investigated whether global self-esteem at a previous time point predicts mean-level change/ rank-order stability in domain-specific self-esteem at a later time-point (top-down). Latent State vs. Latent Trait To analyze the trait- and state-variance in the respective measures of self-esteem, we will use the Latent Trait-State-Occasion Model (TSO; Cole, Martin, &amp; Steiger, 2005). The TSO allows to estimate trait-specific variance and state-specific variance that is specific for the measurement occasions. We will use the same measurement model as in the previous analyses described above. We will run TS0 models for each measure of self-esteem separately.

Transformations

Covariates will be transformed as necessary for the respective analyses. For example, age will be coded in 10 year units (i.e. divided by 10) and mean-centered in order to avoid very small values. Sex will be coded as -1 for males and 1 for females.

Inference criteria

For non-directional hypotheses we will use p smaller than .05 and 95% confidence intervals. For directional hypotheses we will use p smaller than .10 and 90% confidence intervals. Model fit will be assessed with the χ2-test statistic, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root-Mean-Square Residual (SRMR): The CFI ≥ .90, RMSEA ≤ .08, and SRMR ≤ .11 reflect an acceptable fit to the data. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI) are also reported. To conduct the invariance testing, a hierarchically arranged set of analytical steps will be carried out. A series of increasingly restrictive models will be compared against each other to examine invariance (Meredith, 1993). In accordance with Cheung and Rensvold (2002), a value of ΔCFI smaller than or equal to .01 between nested models will be regarded as indicating a nonsignificant decrement in fit between the models. We will not account for multiple hypothesis testing.

Data exclusion

Missing data

All models will be estimated based on the full information maximum likelihood estimator.

Exploratory analysis

We will investigate whether time-invariant and time-varying covariates influence the level and the development of self-esteem. In this regard, we will analyze whether time-invariant covariates, such as age and gender, predict the initial levels, rank-order stability, and mean-level change of the respective measures of global self-esteem and domain-specific self-esteem. With respect to age, linear, quadratic and cubic effects between age and self-esteem are to be investigated. Furthermore, we will analyze whether change in time-varying covariates is correlated with change in self-esteem. We will further test whether time-varying covariates predict the level and change in the respective measures of self-esteem or whether the respective measures of self-esteem predict the covariates. We will also investigate whether the relation between domain-specific covariates and the respective domain of self-esteem is particularly strong by utilizing bifactor models on self-esteem.
