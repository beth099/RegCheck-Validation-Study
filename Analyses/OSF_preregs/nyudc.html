Perceived Contribution to Society of Volunteering and Unpaid Assistance to Close Others

OSF ID: nyudc | Registered: 2023-07-10T17:04:57.492903

Study Information

Hypotheses

Hypotheses: Our primary goal is to investigate how people perceive the contribution to society of unpaid assistance of close others in comparison to volunteering. We expect that compared to volunteering, unpaid assistance to close others (both hands-on assistance and information-based or remote care) will be perceived as: - Less of a contribution to society and - Reflecting less choice to help, In general (e.g., “In general, how much do hours spent providing hands-on unpaid assistance to close others contribute to society?” and “In general, how much do hours spent providing information-based or remote care (i.e., not hands-on) unpaid assistance to close others contribute to society?” vs. “In general, how much do hours spent doing formal volunteer work contribute to society?”) and Among people who engage in these helping behaviors (e.g., “How much do the hours you spend providing hands-on unpaid assistance to close others contribute to society?” and “How much do the hours you spend providing information-based or remote care (i.e., not hands-on) unpaid assistance to close others contribute to society?” vs. “How much do the hours you spend doing formal volunteer work contribute to society?”). Exploratory: - Mediating role of choice and obligation in perceived contribution: We will explore whether the perceived choice or obligation to help mediates the effect of the type of help (unpaid assistance to close others vs. volunteering) on perceived contribution to society in general and among people who engage in these helping behaviors. - Social class differences in helping behaviors: We will explore social class differences in unpaid assistance to close others and volunteering. -- We expect people in higher social class contexts to report higher rates of volunteering than people in lower social class contexts. -- We expect people in lower social class contexts to report higher rates of unpaid assistance to close others than people in higher social class contexts or to observe no significant difference between rates of unpaid assistance to close others by social class context. -- We will explore any potential differences between hands-on vs. information-based or remote care unpaid assistance. - Social class disparities in self-perceived social contribution and in relation to helping behaviors: We will explore social class differences in self-perceived social contribution and how self-perceived social contribution is related to engaging in unpaid assistance to close others and volunteering, perceptions of the contribution of these helping behaviors and the perceived choice or obligation to help for each of these helping behaviors. -- We expect people in lower social class contexts to report lower self-perceived social contribution than people in higher social class contexts. -- We expect volunteering (e.g., engaging in volunteering, perceptions of the contribution to society of volunteering, and perception of the amount of choice or obligation to volunteer) to be more strongly related to self-perceived social contribution than unpaid assistance to close others (e.g., engaging in unpaid assistance to close others, perceptions of the contribution to society of unpaid assistance to close others, and perception of the amount of choice or obligation to engage in unpaid assistance to close others). -- We will also explore how volunteering (e.g., engaging in volunteering, perceptions of the contribution to society of volunteering, and perception of the amount of choice or obligation to volunteer) versus unpaid assistance to close others (e.g., engaging in unpaid assistance to close others, perceptions of the contribution to society of unpaid assistance to close others, and perception of the amount of choice or obligation to engage in unpaid assistance to close others) relates to the social class disparity in self-perceived social contribution. -- We may explore if certain variables moderate the effect of other variables on self-perceived social contribution (e.g., perception of volunteering moderating the effect of hours spent volunteering). -- We may also explore how measures related to volunteering and unpaid assistance to close others predict the perception of the contribution of those helping behaviors (e.g., hours spent volunteering predicting the perceived value of those hours spent volunteering) and if any measures of volunteering and unpaid assistance to close others moderate the effect of other measures on the perceived contribution of volunteering or unpaid assistance to close others. - Association between self-perceived social contribution and measures of well-being, respect, and societal integration: We will explore how participants’ self-perceived social contribution relates to measures of their well-being, sense of respect, and societal integration, expecting self-perceived social contribution to be positively related to greater well-being, a greater sense of respect, and more societal integration. -- We may also explore social class disparities in measures of well-being, respect, and societal integration and whether self-perceived social contribution mediates any social class disparities in these outcomes. - Disparities in the perceived contribution of social groups: We will explore how people rate the contribution of income- and education-based social groups (e.g., “those making $26,000 or less per year”, “those with a high school degree or less education”). -- We will explore ratings reflecting participants’ own opinions as well as their ratings of what they think other people think (i.e., meta-perceptions). -- We expect that the distribution of contribution to society (e.g., 100% divided between five income or three education groups) will not be evenly distributed. For the five income groups, perceived contribution would be evenly distributed if each group is rated as contributing 20% to society. For the three education groups, perceived contribution would be evenly distributed if each group is rated as contributing 33% to society. -- We will explore if participants’ political party and/or ideology are associated with these ratings. - Associations between perceived contribution of social groups and support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans: We will explore how the perceived contribution of income- and education-based social groups relate to support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans. Specifically, we will investigate how the ratings of the lowest and highest income- and education-groups predict redistribution and political engagement. -- We expect that ratings of perceived contribution to society for the lowest income- and education-groups will be positively related to support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans (i.e., higher ratings of contribution to society for the lowest income- and education-groups will be related to increased support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans). -- We expect that ratings of perceived contribution to society for the highest income- and education-groups will be negatively related to support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans (i.e., higher ratings of contribution to society for the highest income- and education-groups will be related to reduced support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans). -- We may include participants’ political party and/or ideology are associated with the outcomes or moderate any effect of perceived contribution on the outcomes.

Study design

This is a correlational study. Participants will complete an online survey in which they will respond to a series of questions. Some of the questions will only be presented to participants who indicated that they engaged in specific helping behaviors. For example, participants who respond that they engage in volunteering will be asked a few questions about their volunteer work whereas participants who do not report volunteering will not be asked these questions.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

1. Participants will be recruited through Dynata, a sampling company. Participants will be compensated directly via Dynata. Participants must be older than 18 years, living in the US, have a high-school degree or less education or a Bachelor’s degree or more education, identify as a man or woman, and identify as a Democrat or a Republican. 2. We plan to recruit a sample that is balanced in terms of both social class context (high-school degree or less education, Bachelor’s degree or more education), gender (men, women), political party (Democrat, Republican), and U.S. region (Northeast, Midwest, South, West). We crossed all these specifications to have 32 separate groups (2 Education x 2 Gender x 2 Political Party x 4 US Region = 32 cells).

Sample size

Our target sample size is 1350 participants. We conducted a pilot with the same survey sampling group of N = 150, but those data will not be used in the analysis of the full study.

Sample size rationale

Our primary analysis of interest is a difference in the perceived contribution to society of volunteering versus unpaid assistance (i.e., either of the two forms: hands-on or information-based unpaid assistance). Thus, we conducted an a priori power analysis using G*Power software. We aimed to be 95% powered to detect a small effect size of 0.15 (based on prior surveys) for a repeated measures within factors ANOVA with 2 levels and 2 measurements on the outcomes at the standard .05 alpha error probability. The target total sample size was approximately 150 participants. However, we are also interested in exploring between-subjects social class differences in other measures (e.g., self-perceived social contribution, hours spent volunteering, hours spent providing unpaid assistance), so we aim to oversample and collect nearly 10 times the amount the power analysis suggests. Thus, we arrived at recruiting 1350 for the full sample (after a 150-person pilot).

Stopping rule

Dynata will stop data collection when the sample size has reached 1350.

Indices

- We will take the mean of three items of the self-perceived social contribution (items 2 and 3 will be reverse-scored) scale into one index of self-perceived social contribution. - We may combine correlating variables into a single index. Such variables will either be identified using factor analysis or confirmed using reliability analysis. - For each type of help (i.e., hands-on unpaid assistance, information-based unpaid assistance, and volunteering), we will sum the hours across each of the subcategories of each form of help to create a total number of hours for each form. However, for the total hours variables we will not include the “to your children under the age of 18” category for the unpaid assistance forms of help. In exploratory analyses, we may explore help to one’s children as its own category or as part of a sum with the other forms of unpaid assistance (however, these will not be used in the confirmatory analyses).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

IN GENERAL: To test the effect of the type of help (volunteering vs. hands-on unpaid assistance vs. information-based unpaid assistance) on ratings of its perceived contribution to society in general (“In general, how much do hours spent providing hands-on unpaid assistance to close others contribute to society?”) and the choice to help (“In general, how much of a choice do people usually have to provide hands-on unpaid assistance to close others?”), we will conduct a linear mixed-effects model for each outcome (i.e., one for perceived contribution and one for perceived choice). Specifically, the models will include a fixed effect of type of help (volunteering vs. hands-on unpaid assistance vs. information-based unpaid assistance; volunteering will be coded as the baseline) and a random intercept of the participant. The models will also include social class context, age, gender, marital status, and race as covariates. We will include a random slope for the type of help, but we will remove the random slope for the type of help if the model with it does not converge. PEOPLE DOING THE HELP: We will also test the effect of the type of help (volunteering vs. hands-on unpaid assistance vs. information-based unpaid assistance) on ratings of its perceived contribution to society and the choice to help among people who report engaging in these helping behaviors. To do so, we will repeat the same models used to test the difference in perceived contributions and choice in general but with the more specific outcome measure (e.g., “How much do the hours you spend providing hands-on unpaid assistance to close others contribute to society?”; “How much of a choice did you have to spend these hours providing hands-on unpaid assistance to close others?”). However, participants only responded to the specific helping perception questions (vs. the in general helping perception questions, which were shown to all participants) if they reported engaging in those behaviors. Thus, every participant did not have the opportunity to respond to the specific helping perception sets of questions. To account for this, we will also conduct the same linear-mixed models for perceived contribution and perceived choice among the smaller group of participants who reported engaging in both forms of help being compared in the analysis.

Transformations

SOCIAL CLASS CONTEXT: Our primary measure of social class context will be education. Using education, we will categorize participants’ current social class context. Participants will be categorized as navigating: (a) lower social class context or (b) higher social class context. Participants will be categorized as navigating a lower social class context if they have less than a four-year college degree. Participants will be categorized as navigating a higher social class context if they have a four-year college degree or more years of education. ORDINAL HOUR VARIABLES: Given the zero-inflated and skewed nature of the count variables of hours spent volunteering or providing unpaid assistance to others, we will create ordinal versions of these variables. Specifically, “0” will be coded as “none.” Then for each variable, the remaining distribution of responses will be split to reflect “low” and “high” such that there will be a roughly equal percentage of responses in “low” as there is in “high.” COVARIATES: Marital status will be coded such that “currently married” is coded as “married” and “single,” “widowed” and “divorced” is coded as “not married.” Race will be coded into a dichotomous variable with “White/European American” as one level and all other responses, including anyone who selects more than one response (i.e., multiracial) will be coded as “Person of Color.”

Inference criteria

We will use the standard p &lt; .05 criteria for determining if any models suggest that the results are significantly different from those expected if the null hypothesis were correct.

Data exclusion

We will exclude participants who fail either of the attention check questions.

Missing data

We will retain as many participants for a given analysis as possible. As such, we will include all participants who completed a given measure in our analyses of that measure. However, we may also examine whether participants who do not complete the entire survey are qualitatively different than those who do (i.e., we will conduct analyses to examine if survey non-completers (vs. completers) differ across any key demographic variables). If they do, we may run analyses without these participants to ensure that our effects are not dependent on survey non-completers.

Exploratory analysis

- Covariates: All models will include age, gender, marital status, and race as covariates. We may run analyses with and without controls and drop controls that are not significant. We may also include working status as a covariate. - Mediating role of choice and obligation in perceived contribution: We will conduct mixed mediation models to determine if the perceived choice or obligation to help mediates the effect of the type of help on perceived contribution to society. We will explore this mediating role for measures of help in general and among people who engage in these helping behaviors (both with all available data points and only among people who engage in these types of help). - Social class differences in helping behaviors: We will investigate any social class differences in helping behaviors, measured continuously and as ordinal variables. For continuous measures, we will conduct linear models with participant social class context predicting each helping behavior. For ordinal measures, we will conduct ordinal logistic regression with participant social class context predicting each helping behavior. - Social class disparities in self-perceived social contribution and in relation to helping behaviors: -- To determine if there is a social class disparity in self-perceived social contribution, we will conduct a linear model with participant social class context predicting the self-perceived social contribution scale. -- To determine how volunteering (e.g., engaging in volunteering, amount of volunteering, perceptions of the contribution to society of volunteering, and perception of the amount of choice or obligation to volunteer) and unpaid assistance to close others (e.g., engaging in unpaid assistance to close others, amount of unpaid assistance given to close others, perceptions of the contribution to society of unpaid assistance to close others, and perception of the amount of choice or obligation to engage in unpaid assistance to close others) relate to self-perceived social contribution, we will conduct linear models with the volunteering variable and unpaid assistance to close others variable(s) predicting self-perceived social contribution scale. -- We may explore if certain variables moderate the effect of other variables on self-perceived social contribution (e.g., perception of volunteering moderating the effect of hours spent volunteering). To do so, we will use linear models with an interaction term for the moderating variable. -- We may also explore how measures related to volunteering and unpaid assistance to close others predict the perception of the contribution of those helping behaviors (e.g., hours spent volunteering predicting the perceived value of those hours spent volunteering) and if any measures of volunteering and unpaid assistance to close others moderate the effect of other measures on the perceived contribution of volunteering or unpaid assistance to close others. To do so, we will use linear models and include an interaction term for any moderations. - Association between self-perceived social contribution and measures of well-being, respect, and societal integration: -- To explore how participants’ self-perceived social contribution relates to measures of their well-being, sense of respect, and societal integration (e.g., feelings of overlap with the U.S., civic obligation), we will conduct linear models with self-perceived social contribution predicting the outcome of interest. We will run separate models for each outcome. -- To explore social class disparities in measures of well-being, respect, and societal integration, we will conduct linear models with participant social class context predicting each outcome measure. -- We may also conduct mediation analyses to determine if self-perceived social contribution mediates any social class disparities in these outcomes. We will run a separate mediation analysis for each outcome. - Disparities in the perceived contribution of social groups: -- To determine if the distribution of ratings of others’ contribution to society are evenly distributed, we will calculate mean values and 95% confidence intervals for each category (e.g., “those making $26,000 or less per year”). We will consider the distribution not equally distributed if the confidence intervals do not overlap with 20% (for the income questions with 5 groups) or 33% (for the education questions with 3 groups). -- To determine if participants' party or political ideology is associated with these ratings, we will conduct linear models with the political ideology or party measure of interest predicting the contribution of various groups (e.g., “those making $26,000 or less per year”). We may also analyze the distribution of sub-categories of the sample, such as the distribution of perceived contribution of others only among Republicans or only among Democrats. - Associations between perceived contribution of social groups and support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans: -- To explore how the perceived contribution of income- and education-based social groups relate to support for redistribution and political participation among lower-income Americans, we will conduct linear models with the lowest income- or education- group predicting the outcome of interest. We will also run the same set of analyses with the highest income- or education-group predicting the outcome of interest. -- To investigate if participants’ political party and/or ideology moderate any effect, we will interact the political variable of interest with the perceived contribution of the income- or education-group to determine if it moderates any effect. To probe further, we will explore the simple effects of the perceived contribution measure at different levels of the political variables. - Social class context moderations: To examine the moderating role of social class context on the link between two factors (e.g., between the type of help and the perceived contribution of that help), we will use linear-mixed effects models in which the fixed effects include the primary predictor variable (e.g., type of help), social class context, and the interaction between social class context and the predictor, along with a random intercept for the participant. In addition, we will dummy code social class context to examine the simple effects of the predictor variable on the dependent variable at different levels of social class context. If the outcome of interest is not a repeated measure, we will use linear models in which social class is included as an interaction term with the predictor variable. - Measures of social class context: Our primary measure of social class context is education. In a series of exploratory analyses, we may examine how other facets of social class context predict or moderate outcomes, including income and subjective social class context. - Intersectionality: Additionally, we may examine the intersection of race, gender, and social class context. We will investigate how the intersection of these identities predict or moderate the outcomes.
