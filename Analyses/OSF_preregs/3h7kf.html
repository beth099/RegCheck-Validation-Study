Semantic gist effects on true and false recognition with multiple exemplars

OSF ID: 3h7kf | Registered: 2019-03-06T12:40:21.558531

Study Information

Title

Semantic gist effects on true and false recognition with multiple exemplars

Authors

Alexa Morcom, Loris Naspi, Paul Hoffman

Description

The current study aims to explore the effects of semantic and perceptual variables on true and false recognition of pictures of objects in younger and older adults. Set size is manipulated to vary the strength of semantic gist representations. For example, participants may see either two or eight different exemplars of the same concept (e.g. dog) during the study phase.

Hypotheses

Our overall hypothesis is that semantic and conceptual confusability will modulate 3 measures of recognition memory (measures defined below): false recognition of novel objects depicting basic-level concepts (e.g. dog) for which no other exemplar was studied, false recognition of lure objects depicting unstudied exemplars of studied concepts (e.g. a different table), and true recognition of studied exemplars of studied concepts (e.g. the same cat or table). Specific hypotheses for semantic and perceptual confusability effects are based on results of our first experiment (in prep) which was identical to this one but in which only 1 exemplar of each basic-level concept was encoded in the study phase. Semantic feature confusability is assumed to act at the concept level (so gist generalises across concepts) and exemplarity at the exemplar level (so gist generalises across exemplars). The number of features had similar effects to feature confusability and predictions for this variable are the same. We have multiple alternative hypotheses for age effects Two are based on prior studies suggesting older people show increases in semantically driven false recognition (Koutstaal et al., 2003): i) at least in tasks involving nameable objects, older people primarily process items at the basic level, whether by subvocal naming or otherwise (semantic categorisation account; Koutstaal et al., 2003), so will rely more on gist processing only at the exemplar level (i.e., will tend to generalise across exemplars to concepts) ii) due to greater accumulated knowledge during the lifespan, older adults will rely more on semantic gist processing at both the concept- and the exemplar- levels. iii) alternatively, older adults may show a generalised impairment in the specificity of memory representations, showing increased false recognition driven by perceptual as well as semantic confusability (Pidgeon &amp; Morcom, 2014; Burnside et al., 2017). Novel item false recognition A. Hypotheses based on our first experiment: 1) Concept-level: in the young, pictures of more semantically confusable novel (unstudied) concepts will be more likely to be falsely recognised due to cross-concept activation. 2) Concept-level: This effect may EITHER be increased in older people OR may be age-invariant even with the study of multiple exemplars of each basic-level concept if age effects on gist processing are restricted to the exemplar-level, see (i) above. 3) Exemplar-level: In the young, novel pictures judged as being an excellent exemplar of their corresponding basic-level concept will again be more likely to be falsely recognised. 4) Exemplar-level: This effect may EITHER be increased in older people OR may be age-invariant if age effects on gist processing are restricted to the concept-level. Lure false recognition B. Hypotheses based on our first experiment: 1) Concept-level: In the young, we have 2 alternative hypotheses. IF lures which are exemplars of more semantically confusable basic-level concepts were more likely to be rejected due to shared features triggering recall-to-reject, semantically confusable lures in the current study will instead be less likely to be rejected (as 2 or 8 exemplars impedes use of recall to reject), OR IF better lure rejection for concepts with many shared features was due to an emphasis on processing at the concept-level rather than the exemplar-level, leading to activation of gist across the superordinate category, we will replicate this initial finding and it may be enhanced for 8 versus 2 exemplars. 2) Exemplar-level: In the young, again we expect that lure images judged to have high exemplarity of their basic-level concept will be more likely to be falsely recognised. This may be increased when 8 versus 2 lures have been studied. 3) Exemplar-level: Although the exemplarity effect did not differ with age in our first study, it may do in the current one because of the larger set sizes (see B). 4) Young adults will falsely recognise lures which are more visually confusable (HMaxC1 ). C. Based on our first experiment and previous data suggesting older adults can be more prone to perceptually-driven false recognition (Burnside, Hope, Gill and Morcom; 2017) 1) Older adults will be more prone than the young to falsely recognise lures which are similar in colour to others in the set (Age x colour similarity). D. Overall effects of ageing for lures (could be due to semantic and /or perceptual confusability between studied &amp; unstudied exemplars). Previous categorised pictures studies typically show greater false recognition in older adults (e.g. Koutstaal and Schacter, 1997; see semantic gist hypotheses of ageing, i and ii above). 1) Older adults will be more prone to false recognition of lures than young adults now that multiple exemplars of the same basic level concept will have been studied. (Note that no age effect was found in our previous experiment when only 1 exemplar was studied). 2) This age effect may be stronger when 8 compared to 2 items have been studied per concept. Semantic-specific effects (our main experimental variables): 3) Concept- and/or exemplar-level: In older adults, greater semantic confusability and/or exemplarity effects on lure false recognition than in young (see A 1 and 2). 4) These age effects may be larger when 8 versus 2 exemplars of the same basic-level concept have been studied. Specifically, Koutstaal et al.’s (2003) semantic categorization account predicts an age-related increase in exemplarity effects - i.e., via conceptual influences operating at the exemplar level, as opposed to the concept level. Predictions for true recognition E. Based on our first experiment, and as for novel items: 1) Concept-level: In the young, studied pictures of more semantically confusable concepts will be less likely to be recognised due to greater concept-level relative to exemplar-level activation. 2) Concept-level: In older adults EITHER this effect will be increased OR it may be age-invariant even with the study of multiple exemplars of each basic-level concept, if age effects on gist processing are restricted to the exemplar-level, see (i) above. F. Based on Koustaal et al. (2003) and Pidgeon and Morcom (2014). 1) Older adults will show the same or slightly reduced true recognition relative to the young. 2) If older adults emphasise basic conceptual level processing (i above), their true recognition will be more influenced by exemplar-level activation than that of the young, i.e. they will show larger effects of exemplarity. 3) Age effects of exemplarity may be enhanced or only present when 8 versus 2 exemplars have been studied.

Study design

This is a mixed design study with 1 between-groups variable (age group), 1 within-participants variable (set size), and item-level variables operationalising semantic and perceptual confusability.

Randomization

Stimuli will be allocated randomly to experimental condition (studied, unstudied, lure and set size), creating a different list of items for each participant.

Explanation of existing data

Data have been from 102 of 120 participants. Data have been inspected for exclusion check only (criteria below).

Data collection procedures

Two samples of participants from two different age groups: - Young adults aged between 18 and 33 years. - Older adults aged between 65 and 80 years. Participants will be recruited via the University of Edinburgh volunteer panel and through advertisements. Participants will be paid £6 pounds. Participants need to be fluent English speakers, i.e., have spoken English fluently since age 5 years or earlier, and have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and good self-reported health.

Sample size

Our sample size will be 120 participants, 60 per age group, after replacements following any exclusions.

Sample size rationale

We powered our study for two principal effects of semantic confusability on lure false recognition: a) the main effect of semantic confusability and b) the higher order interaction of condition (study set size 8 versus 2) x semantic confusability. See below for details of our generalised linear mixed effects model approach. As the relevant effect sizes are unknown, we ran sensitivity analyses for different effect sizes we judged feasible based on our first experiment. In that study, only one exemplar picture was studied so we expect modulations of lure false recognition to at least as strong in the current study given the increased set sizes of 2 and 8. Power curves in R (version 3.4.4) using the simr package (version 1.0.5) show that with N = 60 per group and trial numbers per condition as specified below, we can: 1. Detect a medium-sized interaction of condition (lure_eight vs lure_two) x semantic feature confusability at 0.80 power (OR = 1.72; equivalent to Cohen’s d = 0.30) at alpha = 0.05. 2. Detect a small main effect of semantic feature confusability at 0.80 power (OR = 1.38; equivalent to Cohen’s d = 0.18) at alpha = 0.05.

Stopping rule

When target N is achieved.

Variables

Manipulated variables

The age groups are: 1) young adults 2) older adults The conditions are: 1) old_eight = studied items, where 8 exemplars of the same basic-level concept were studied 2) old_two = studied items, where 2 exemplars of the same basic-level concept were studied 3) lure_eight = lure items, further exemplars of categories where 8 exemplars of the same basic-level concept were studied 4) lure_two = lure items, further exemplars of categories where 2 exemplars of the same basic-level concept were studied 5) new_zero = unstudied novel items which are novel exemplars of unstudied basic level concepts The item level variables (defined below under Indices) are: 1) Semantic feature confusability (Clarke &amp; Tyler, 2014) 2) HMax variable (C1) (Riesenhuber &amp; Poggio, 1999) 3) HMax variable (C2) Riesenhuber &amp; Poggio, 1999) 4) Image exemplarity 5) Colour similarity 6) Other variables which will be modelled as effects of no interest using PCA with varimax rotation, after selecting the components that account for at least 85% of the variance (number of non-white pixels, colour entropy, mean backward associative strength, mean forward associative strength, phonological neighborhood density, concept familiarity, word co-occurrence, concreteness, age of acquisition).

Measured variables

Measured variables are: A. Outcome variables The single outcome variable will be the participant’s recognition memory judgement (two levels: whether an item is judged old/new).

Indices

Our model will include indices of semantic and perceptual similarity. A. Concept-Level variables (reflecting semantic processing of and across concepts) 1) Semantic confusability Our measure follows Clarke and Tyler (2014), but uses updated property norms (Devereux, Tyler, &amp; Randall, 2014). Similarity between concepts will be computed from a semantic feature matrix in which each concept is represented by a binary vector indicating whether each feature is associated with the concept or not. Similarity between each pair of concepts = 1 - the cosine angle between feature vectors. The semantic confusability of each concept with all other concepts = the sum of weighted similarity, in which the weight is the similarity between the concepts themselves (= sum of squared similarities). B. Exemplar-Level variables (reflecting semantic or perceptual properties of exemplar images of the basic-level concepts) 1) C1 and C2 HMax indexes The measures derive from the HMax computational model of vision (Riesenhuber &amp; Poggio, 1999; Serre et al., 2007) and follow Clarke &amp; Tyler (2014). They capture the low and high-level visual attributes of each picture exemplar. We will calculate visual similarity across the set of images after extracting their low and high-level visual object information: a C1 response related to early visual cortex (V1/V2), and a C2 response related to V4/posterior IT. Response vectors from the C1 and C2 layers will be computed for grey-scaled versions of each image. Then, to generate a visual similarity score for each image we will calculate the sum of all squared ranks of correlations between the image i and all other images j. 2) Colour similarity We will also calculate colour similarity of each image to the rest. After conversion of RGB into CIELab space we will compute the normalised Earth Mover’s Distance between each pair of images (Rubner et al., 2000). We will then transform the distance matrix to a similarity matrix using the exponential function to give similarity values from 0 to 1 (highest similarity). To obtain a single metric for each image, we will sum the squared ranks of similarities. Higher values indicate a higher colour similarity with all the other pictures in the set. 3) Image exemplarity We will collect image exemplarity after the main recognition memory task, as in our first experiment (as new images have been added). Pictures will be presented together with their verbal label, and participants asked to judge the exemplarity of each picture: “how closely each picture resembles your mental image of the object”. Responses will be given on a 7-point scale (1 = poor picture of concept word, 7 = excellent picture of concept word). For each picture, exemplarity scores are averaged over all participants. C. Other variables to be used as covariates of no interest in the model 1) BAS and FAS Forward and backward associative strength norms were collected as part of our first study, for the specific concepts used in this recognition memory task (in prep). 2) Word frequency Log-transformed word frequencies from SUBTTLEX-UK, based on British English television subtitles (van Heuven, Mandera, Keuleers, &amp; Brysbaert, 2014). 3) Concreteness A set of norms collected by Brysbaert et al. (2014). 4) Age of acquisition Updated norms (Brysbaert &amp; Biemiller, 2017) for the age at which acquired a certain concept in childhood. 5) Phonological neighbourhood density (pnd) From the CELEX lexical database of English (Baayen, Piepenbrock, &amp; Gulikers, 1995). 6) Visual complexity measures Two different measures of image complexity: the number of non-white pixels and colour entropy (as used in our first experiment, in prep). 7) Concept familiarity Norms collected in our previous experiment (in prep). Following Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980), we asked subjects to judge the familiarity of each picture “according to how usual or unusual the object is in your realm of experience”. Familiarity was defined as “the degree to which you come in contact with or think about the concept”. Participants were required to give their response on a 7-point scale (1 = completely unfamiliar, 7 = completely familiar) using the keyboard. For each image, familiarity scores were averaged over all participants.

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

1) We will use a series of generalised linear mixed effects model in R to analyse our results. As in our first experiment (in prep), all models will include by-subject and by-item random intercepts. 2) By default we will estimate models using glmer as for our first experiment (in prep) but if there are problems with convergence, we will switch to Bayesian estimation using the brms package (version 2.5.0). 3) To select the fixed effects in the model, we will compare progressively simpler with more complex models until the best goodness-of-fit is reached, indicating the most parsimonious model. Variables are selected in 3 blocks of predictors of interest (Concept-Level, Exemplar-Level, No-Interest). First, the full model will be fitted, then we will reduce model complexity by removing blocks of variables in the order: Concept-Level, Exemplar-Level, No-Interest variables, again following our first experiment (in prep). 4) In addition to the main effects of selected blocks of variables, we will test main effects and interactions of age x condition, and higher-order interactions of age and condition with the Concept-level and Exemplar level variables included in the selected model (see 3). 5) As the No-Interest variables are used only for controlling potential confounding effects, and are moderately correlated, we will perform a data reduction using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) with the prcomp function in R. Following a varimax rotation we will select sufficient components to account for at least 85% of the variance. 6) Once the best model is chosen, we will run the follow-up analyses. Follow-up analysis We will conduct post hoc tests with correction for multiple comparisons using Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment using p.adjusted function in R. These will be implemented separately for two families of comparisons. These will be applied first in the young group alone, and across both age groups (for effects involving age only). Similar tests will be done in the older group only to shed light on interactions between age and experimental variables of interest: 1) All main effects and interactions that include the Concept-Level variable (i.e., semantic confusability). 2) All main effects and interactions that include the Item-Level variables (i.e., C1 and C2 HMax variables, colour similarity, image exemplarity).

Transformations

1) All the continuous predictor variables will be standardised and the resulting coefficients b will be used to calculate the odds ratios as indexes of the change in odds for a one-unit standard deviation (SD) change in our fixed effects of interest. 2) Categorical variable coding will be defined so that the reference level for condition will be new_zero.

Inference criteria

1) We will use the lowest Akaike Information criteria (AIC) to select the best goodness-of-fit. 2) We will use an alpha of 0.05 for all tests of statistical significance. 3) The Wald test (z-test) will be used in the generalised linear mixed effect model.

Data exclusion

We will exclude participants if: 1) They have not been speaking English since at least the age of 5. 2) They have medical conditions (e.g., eye diseases that prevent from participating in the study, an initial form of MCI, any kind of diagnosed dementia). 3) Pr = proportion of Hits - proportion of false alarms for novel items. We will exclude participants with a Pr &lt; 0.1. 4) d-prime = Zhits - Zfa (for novel items). We will exclude participants with a d' equals to +/- 3SD. 5) They provide less than 80 per cent valid trials (i.e., 48 out of 60) for each condition (as this affects power)

Missing data

Missing values can arise for two reasons: 1) Participants’ missing responses. Linear mixed models handle missing trials well. In fact, we want to include participants with missingness to increase the power and avoid biasing the results. (But see exclusion criteria) 2) Missing values for normed variables (i.e., No-Interest) As a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) cannot be run if there are missing values, we need to estimate them. We will estimate missing data using the missMDA R package.
