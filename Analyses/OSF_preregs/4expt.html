Meritocratic Beliefs and Inequality in a Collective Risk Public Goods Game

OSF ID: 4expt | Registered: 2021-04-09T10:31:10.471208 | Showing: original version (1 version(s) on OSF)

Study Information

Hypotheses

1. We predict that (rich) participants starting with a high endowment (£20) will contribute more than (poor) participants starting with a low endowment (£10) in absolute terms in all treatment conditions. But we predict that they will contribute less in relative terms – that is, a lower proportion of their wealth than poor participants. 2. We predict that rich individuals in the effort condition will on average contribute less towards the group account than rich participants in the luck and mixed conditions. And that rich individuals in the mixed condition will contribute less than their rich peers in the luck condition. 3. We predict that poor individuals in the luck condition will on average contribute less towards the group account than their peers in the effort and mixed conditions. And that those in the mixed condition will contribute less than those in the effort condition. 4. We predict that groups in the luck condition will achieve the group target with the highest success rate, followed by those in the mixed and then those in the effort condition. Relatedly, we predict that the average amount in the group account at the end of the game (in both successful and unsuccessful groups) will be highest for groups in the luck condition, followed by those in the mixed and then the effort condition. 5. We predict that in the pre-game quiz, participants’ judgment about the ‘fair’ level of contribution from rich individuals will be lowest among subjects in the effort condition, followed by those in the mixed and then the luck contribution. We anticipate that the reverse will also be true: those in the effort condition will judge a fair contribution from poor individuals to be higher than those in the mixed condition, and higher still than those in the luck condition.

Study design

In our collective risk format participants work in groups of four to reach a common goal by making contributions from an initial endowment. In each of 10 rounds of the game, participants must decide how much they would like to contribute from their private endowment (£0, £0.75, or £1.50) towards the group target of £30. At the end of each round, participants will be shown the cumulative total contributed to the group account (but not the individual contributions of each player). If the target is achieved within or after 10 rounds, all participants in the group keep the money that they have not contributed during the game. But if the target is not reached, individuals face a 50% probability of losing their remaining funds (and a 50% probability of keeping them). To explore the effect of individuals’ beliefs in these group situations, we have adapted this collective risk format in two ways. Firstly, in each group two participants will start the game rich (with a high endowment of £20) and two will begin poor (with a low endowment £10). Secondly, all participants will complete a separate mental arithmetic ‘performance’ task before the game begins, consisting of adding up a short series of two-digit numbers. Groups will be randomly assigned to one of three treatment conditions. In the ‘effort’ condition, performance in the mental arithmetic task will determine participants’ starting endowments, with the two highest scoring participants receiving £20 to start the game and the two lowest scoring participants receiving £10 only. In the ‘luck’ condition, task performance will not impact starting endowments. However, for consistency these participants will still complete the mental arithmetic task, and will be told that the highest scoring participant in each group will receive an additional £1 at the end of the experiment. In both of these treatment groups, participants will be explicitly told about this process (i.e. whether their performance will determine their endowment or not) at the start of the experiment. In the third ‘mixed’ condition, each participant’s endowment will be randomly assigned either by task performance or by chance (and these participants will also be explicitly told this). In other words, two players’ endowments will be determined by task performance (with the higher scoring participant receiving the higher endowment), while the other two players’ budgets will be determined by a 50-50 lottery (with the winner receiving the higher endowment). This design enables us to test the extent to which beliefs about the foundation of unequal wealth distribution influence contributions to the group. Finally, before starting the public goods game, participants will complete a pre-game quiz to check their understanding of the game and to elicit their beliefs and expectations about the game. Participants will be asked, for example, what they think would represent a fair contribution from players starting the game with £20 and £10. In addition, they will be asked to predict how much these players will contribute on average and how confident they are that the group will achieve its target.

Subjects will be randomly assigned to treatment groups upon starting the experiment.

Explanation of existing data

Data collection procedures

Participants will be sourced via Amazon Mechanical Turk (mturk.com) and Prolific Academic (prolific.co). All participation will be voluntary and advertised in line with these platforms’ standards. They will be paid a pro rata fee of £7.50 per hour for their participation in the experiment. And will have the opportunity to earn additional funds during the game. All participants must be aged at least 18. No further exclusion criteria will apply, although we will be aiming to recruit a representative sample from the UK population.

Sample size

In total, we plan to run our experiment with 240 participants. This amounts to 60 groups (20 in each condition) each with 4 members. The number of subjects we end up with for our analysis will be influenced by the dropout rate.

Sample size rationale

The key dependent variable in our study is the contribution level of rich players in groups with inequality based on effort vs. luck. The sample size needed to detect an effect on this dependent variable was calculated on the basis of effect sizes in three previous studies. First, Tavoni et al. (2011) found that in unequal groups, on average rich participants contributed 36% of their endowment (compared with 47% of their endowment when working in equal groups, representing an effect size of around 23%). Second, Vicens et al. (2018) observed in groups with unequal starting endowments that the most affluent participants contributed 47% of their endowment. And third, rich participants in the study conducted by Van Dijk &amp; Wilke (1994) on average contributed 63% of their endowment in a treatment condition mirroring our luck condition, but only 47% in one mirroring our effort condition (representing a treatment effect of 25%). Taking the average of these studies, we estimate that rich participants in our luck condition will contribute around 49% of their endowment to the group account; and that the treatment effect of our effort condition will be around 24%. We therefore conducted a power analysis to calculate the sample size needed to detect an effect size of 24% with a significance level of 0.05, which indicated a minimum of 224 participants. We plan to run this experiment with 240 participants to cover the risk of dropouts, but it is important to state here that this sample size represents the minimum and, subject to budget, additional sessions will be added if necessary to form a conclusive judgment where significance is not clear.

Stopping rule

Variables

Manipulated variables

Participants’ starting endowments (£20 vs. £10) will be manipulated between subjects in all three treatment conditions. In addition, we will manipulate the cause of these unequal endowments between groups in the three conditions, as described in the Study Design section above. Manipulating only players’ endowments and their beliefs about the origin of their endowments in this way enables us to isolate the effect of players’ beliefs on their responses and decisions.

Measured variables

1. Player contributions during the game: ‘How much would you like to transfer to the group account?’ 2. Group success: proportion of groups in each condition achieving the group target; and the mean total in group accounts at the end of the game. 3. Player normative beliefs: ‘How much do you think would be a fair contribution from each player?’ 4. Player expectations: ‘How much do you predict each player in your group will contribute?’ and ‘How confident are you that the group will achieve its target?’ We will also measure limited demographic variables (i.e., age and gender).

Analysis Plan

Statistical models

We will test Hypothesis 1 (contribution level of high vs. low endowment individuals within treatment condition) using analyses of variance (ANOVAs) on the estimated marginal mean contributions (absolute and relative to starting endowment) of high and low endowment participants. Follow-up t-tests will be conducted to investigate any significant differences between endowment levels and conditions. These tests will be conducted within treatment conditions (i.e. high endowment effort vs. low endowment effort). In addition, we will conduct two-sided Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests to compare mean contributions of rich and poor participants in both successful and unsuccessful groups (as in Tavoni et al., 2011). Finally, we will evaluate the overall effect of any variation in contributions between rich and poor players by comparing the Gini coefficient at the start (G = 0.167) and end of the game. We will also test Hypotheses 2 and 3 (contribution level of high vs. low endowment individuals between treatment conditions) using ANOVAs on players’ estimated marginal mean contributions (in absolute and relative terms). Differences in these mean contributions will be compared between treatment conditions: for Hypothesis 2, this will mean comparing high endowment effort vs. high endowment mixed vs. high endowment luck; for Hypothesis 3, we will compare low endowment effort vs. low endowment mixed vs. low endowment luck. In addition, we will use linear regression models to support this analysis, with players’ contributions as the dependent variable. The base model (Model 1) will include endowment (high vs. low) as the independent variable; Model 2 will include condition (effort vs. luck vs. mixed); Model 3 will include controls for contributions in previous rounds; and Model 4 will include players’ normative beliefs and expectations (elicited during the pre-game quiz). Finally, we will measure the extent to which players’ willingness to contribute is conditional on others’ behaviour by regressing individual contributions on the average contribution of other group members in the previous round in linear mixed effects models (as in Gächter et al., 2017). We will test Hypothesis 4 (group success rate) using ANOVAs to investigate: 1) the difference in group success rates (i.e. % of groups who successfully achieve the target) between the three treatment conditions; and 2) the total mean amount contributed to the group accounts in successful and unsuccessful groups, also between treatment conditions. T-tests will be conducted to follow up on any significant differences found between treatment conditions. In addition, we will compare the total amounts in group accounts between treatment conditions using two-sided Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon tests (as in Tavoni et al., 2011). To complement this analysis, we will use logistic regression models with group outcome (binary: 1 = successful, 0 = unsuccessful) as the response term and treatment condition and mean group member normative beliefs and expectations as predictor variables. In addition, we will run a Spearman’s correlation test to investigate correlation between individuals’ normative beliefs about fair contributions and group success. We will test Hypothesis 5 (normative beliefs) using linear regression models with participants’ judgment about the fair level of contribution from high and low endowment individuals as the dependent variables (regressions will be run separately). Model 1 will include condition (effort vs. mixed vs. luck) as the independent variable; Model 2 will also include endowment (high vs. low) as an independent variable; and additional models will include covariates such as age and gender.

Inference criteria

Data exclusion

Individual response data collected during the game from a participant who does not complete the whole experiment will be withdrawn. And because this is a multiplayer game played simultaneously in a group of four participants, if one person within the group withdraws from the experiment during the game, the game will end for all members of that group. Therefore, in-game response data of all group members will also be withdrawn. However, data from these participants collected before the game begins (i.e. in the pre-game quiz) will be included in our analysis because these players’ beliefs about the game are relevant to our analysis.
