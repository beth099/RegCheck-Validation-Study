Noisy Channel (Russian)

OSF ID: 38ady | Registered: 2021-07-20T18:35:24.612062

Study Information

Hypotheses

1. Sentences with non-canonical word order will be interpreted literally less often than sentences with canonical word order. 2. Sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by deletion errors (e.g., uvidel-a -&gt; uvidel-∅) will be interpreted literally less often than sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by insertion errors (e.g., uvidel-∅ -&gt; uvidel-a). Sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by insertion errors will be interpreted literally less often than sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by substitution errors (e.g., uvidel-a -&gt; uvidel-i or uvidel-i -&gt; uvidel-a). To summarize, in terms of proportion of literal interpretations, deletion &lt; insertion &lt; substitution. 3. Sentences with non-canonical word order are more likely to be interpreted literally when filler sentences are always grammatical (non-noisy) than when filler materials are sometimes ungrammatical (noisy).

Is there any additional blinding in this study?

Participants will not be introduced to our hypotheses nor made aware of the between-participants condition they will be assigned to. Similarly, participants will not be explicitly informed about the eight types of within-participants conditions they will observe.

Study design

The study will follow a mixed design. Between participants, we will manipulate a two-level factor of environment noise (noisy fillers vs. non-noisy fillers). Within participants, each participant will be exposed to a 2X3 design. The first, two-level factor is canonicality (canonical vs. non-canonical) and the second, three-level factor is edit type (deletion vs. insertion vs. substitution). Edit types result from manipulating the gender of the names in the first last NPs of the sentence, such that a deletion edit type occurs when the first NP has a female name and the last NP has a male name, an insertion edit type occurs with the male name in the first NP and a female name in the last NP, and substitution occurs when any NP has two names (plural) and the other has a feminine name.

Randomization

We will use multiple levels of randomization. First, participants will be randomly assigned to the noisy fillers or non-noisy fillers condition. Within each between-participant condition, all filler materials are identical. Second, each participant will be assigned to one of eight lists of 32 critical items. Each critical item consists of a sentence in Russian with the following structure: NP V NP. The NPs consist of female and male names according to one of the 8 possible combinations of canonicality and gender composition of the NPs. Each list of critical items has 32 verbs, each appearing precisely once. In each of the 8 lists, each unique verb is assigned to a different combination of canonicality and gender composition, such that, across lists, each verb appears in all 8 combinations precisely once. Each critical trial was composed of a sentence and a binary yes/no question, indicating a literal or non-literal interpretation of the sentence. For each critical item, it was randomly selected which response (yes or no) indicates literal or non-literal interpretation. Finally, the order of all the items in the list was randomized for each participant.

Explanation of existing data

This is a replication and extension of an experiment that we have conducted in the past without preregistering.

Data collection procedures

Participants will be recruited through the crowd-sourcing platform Prolific. All participants will be at least 18 years old and have indicated that their first language is Russian, a natural inclusion criterion, given that the study is conducted in Russian. Additional steps will be taken to increase the probability that the participants in the study are native Russian speakers. Due to the large size of the Russophone diaspora, a participant who acquired Russian as L1 might not have native, or even good, control of Russian, e.g., a child of Russian immigrants in the United States who used to speak Russian only with her parents and only before adolescence. Therefore, we add the inclusion criterion that the participants’ country of birth is one of the Post-Soviet States, where Russian was often the main language of daily life during the Soviet Union (i.e., today’s Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Georgia, Azerbaijan, Armenia, Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia). Additionally, participants are limited to a minimum approval rate of 90%, meaning that at least 90% of their past tasks on Prolific were approved by their experimenters to be completed successfully.

Sample size

Our target sample size is 260 participants, 130 for each between-participants condition.

Sample size rationale

In the current project, we are replicating and extending a previous study. The data for the previous study had been collected and were used for the purposes of the power analysis. Following common practice, the fixed effects used for the simulation were estimated using the previously-collected data and shrunk by 25%. Variation in random effects was generated according to the variation in random effects from the previous study. We tested the differences between conditions using a logistic regression with the binary DV being literal interpretation (dummy coded: 0=non-literal, 1=literal) as predicted by edit type (insertion, deletion, substitution) with random intercepts for participants and verbs (since each critical item consisted of one unique verb in counterbalanced conditions across participants, we treat each unique verb as an item). We used treatment coding with the edit type variable, setting insertion edits to be the control level. We chose insertion because we predict it to be interpreted literally less often than substitution edits but more often than deletion edits. Therefore, treatment coding allows us to make insertion-deletion and insertion-substitution comparisons. If both comparisons are significant with effects in the predicted direction, the deletion-substitution comparison will be trivially significant, and thus need not be tested. We chose the smallest sample size such that both comparisons had power of at least 80%. We purposefully chose a sample size estimate that we believe to be conservative, since we expect to exclude up to 10% of participants for non-compliance with the experimental instructions. Please find the data and the simulation code on the study’s OSF page.

Variables

Manipulated variables

Every item consists of a sentence and a question. The manipulated variables modify properties of the sentences. Environment Noise (between-participants): - Noisy fillers: 32 of the 64 fillers will include grammatical errors. 10 of the noisy filler sentences will contain verbs that agree in number and gender with the object, in 11 filler sentences a NP will be assigned a case that makes the sentence ungrammatical, and, in the last 11 noisy filler sentences, a function word will be added or omitted to create ungrammatical sentences. - Non-noisy fillers: all fillers will be grammatical. Canonicality (within-participants): The critical sentences take the form of NP V NP. - Canonical: the verb agrees in gender and number with the first NP (resulting in an SVO sentence). - Non-Canonical: the verb agrees in gender and number with the last NP (resulting in an OVS sentence). Edit Type (within-participants): All edit types only concern non-canonical sentences. The edit type differ from each other by which NP in the sentence consists of a male name, a female name, or both. - Deletion (female name first, male name last), e.g., Джейн увидел Джо, Jane saw-mas Joe. - Insertion (male name first, female name last), e.g., Джо увидела Джейн, Joe saw-fem Jane. - Substitution (two names and a female name, regardless of order) e.g., Джо и Джейн увидела Рейчел, Joe and Jane saw-fem Rachel or Рейчел увидели Джо и Джейн, Rachel saw-pl Joe and Jane.

Measured variables

Every item consists of a sentence and a question. Participants will respond to the question by clicking on the buttons that say “yes” or “no.” Whether yes or no indicates a literal interpretation was randomized. - Literal: The participant provided an answer that indicates a literal interpretation of the sentence (i.e., assigning the subject role to the NP that has grammatical agreement with the verb). - Non-literal: the participant provided an answer that indicates a non-literal interpretation of the sentence (i.e., assigning the subject role to the NP that does not have grammatical agreement with the verb.)

Statistical models

Hypothesis I: Sentences with non-canonical word order will be interpreted literally less often than sentences with canonical word order. We will fit a Bayesian logistic regression model using the brms package in R. We will set literal interpretation to be the DV with canonicality and environment noise as IVs (main effects and interaction effect), with random intercepts for participants, verbs, with random slopes for environment noise within verbs. The model will use uninformative, flat priors, but they may be adapted to help with sampling. We will initially specify the model to have 4 chains with 2000 iterations, 500 of which are warmup. However, The number of iterations, warmup iterations, delta, and tree depth may be adjusted according to model diagnostics such that no divergent transitions after warmup are observed. Finally, if the 95% confidence interval of the posterior does not include 0, we will conclude that the conditions differ from each other significantly. Hypothesis II: Sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by deletion errors (e.g., uvidel-a -&gt; uvidel-∅) will be interpreted literally less often than sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by insertion errors (e.g., uvidel-∅ -&gt; uvidel-a). Sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by insertion errors will be interpreted literally less often than sentences with non-canonical word order that could have been caused by substitution errors (e.g., uvidel-a -&gt; uvidel-i). To summarize, in terms of proportion of literal interpretations, deletion &lt; insertion &lt; substitution. Data from canonical sentences will be removed. We will fit a Bayesian logistic regression model using the brms package in R. We will set literal interpretation to be the DV with edit type and environment noise as IVs (main effects and interaction effect), with random intercepts for participants and verbs, with random slopes for edit types within participants and random slopes for environment noise and edit types within verbs (main effects and interaction effect). The model will use uninformative, flat priors, but they may be adapted to help with sampling. We will initially specify the model to have 4 chains with 2000 iterations, 500 of which are warmup. However, The number of iterations, warmup iterations, delta, and tree depth may be adjusted according to model diagnostics such that no divergent transitions after warmup are observed. Finally, if the 95% confidence interval of the posterior does not include 0, we will conclude that the conditions differ from each other significantly. Hypothesis III: Sentences with non-canonical word order are more likely to be interpreted literally when filler sentences are grammatical (non-noisy) than when filler materials are sometimes ungrammatical (noisy). This hypothesis will be answered with the same model as Hypothesis I. Please see uploaded analysis plan.

Transformations

To contrast the deletion-insertion edits and the insertion-substitution edits, we will use treatment coding with the edit type variable, setting insertion edits to be the control (reference) level.

Inference criteria

Our inference criteria will be the 95% confidence interval of the posterior in the Bayesian models. If it does not contain zero, we will interpret that the difference between the examined condition is statistically significant (i.e., unlikely to have been caused by a type-I error).

Data exclusion

Participants will be excluded from the analyses in the following cases: - Participants who report Russian as not their first language. - Participants who report their birth country to be any other than any of the Post Soviet States. - Participants who choose a non-literal response in 25% or more of the grammatical filler items.
